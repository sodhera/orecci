## What "confident" actually sounds like

In a stage talk, confidence is about presence. In a hallway chat, a one-to-one with your manager or dinner with friends, it comes down to five small things people hear without noticing:

| Signal | Sounds unsure | Sounds confident |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Volume | People ask "sorry?" | They hear you the first time |
| Sentence endings | Voice rises or trails off | Voice settles down at the full stop |
| Getting to the point | Three hedges before the point | Point first, reason second |
| Pauses | Filled with "um" or rushed through | Left as silence |
| Taking space | Apologising for speaking | Speaking, then stopping |

You don't need to fix all five at once. Pick the one you recognise most and work on it for a week.

## 1. Posture: sit and stand so your voice can work

Confident voices come from bodies that aren't folded in. You don't need a power pose; you need room to breathe.

- **Sitting:** both feet on the floor, back off the chair's backrest, shoulders down. Hunching over a laptop squashes your breath.
- **Standing:** weight on both feet, not one hip. Arms loose at your sides or one hand holding a cup.
- **Head:** level, chin neither tucked nor jutting. Look at the person when you start a sentence and when you finish it.

## 2. Volume: one notch up

Quiet speakers often think they're at a normal volume. Test it: in your next conversation, speak one notch louder than feels natural. Most people find nobody reacts, except that fewer people say "sorry, what?".

If you're in a noisy room or a meeting with a long table, aim your voice at the person furthest from you. See [how to project your voice](/speaking-coach/guides/how-to-project-your-voice/) for the breathing behind it.

## 3. Finish your sentences

Two habits make a sentence sound unfinished:

- **Rising at the end.** Statements that go up like questions ([uptalk](/speaking-coach/glossary/uptalk/)) can sound as if you're asking permission.
- **Trailing off.** "So I was thinking maybe we could… I don't know…" hands the end of your thought to someone else.

The fix is the same for both: land the last word. Let your pitch drop slightly on the last stressed word and then stop. Practice by reading three sentences out loud and exaggerating the downward ending, then do it at normal strength.

> Before: "I think the deadline might be a bit tight? So…"
>
> After: "I think the deadline is too tight. I'd move it to Friday."

## 4. Drop the hedges

Hedges are the words we put in front of a point to soften it: *just, maybe, sort of, I might be wrong but, this is probably a silly question.* One is fine. Three in a row tell the listener you don't believe what's coming. See [hedging language](/speaking-coach/glossary/hedging-language/) for a longer list.

Here are everyday lines, before and after. These are examples; use your own words.

| Situation | Hedged | Direct |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Suggesting an idea | "This is probably a dumb idea, but maybe we could try…" | "One idea: let's try…" |
| Disagreeing | "Sorry, I might be totally wrong here, but…" | "I see it differently. Here's why." |
| Asking for help | "Sorry to bother you, I just wondered if maybe…" | "Have you got five minutes? I need help with…" |
| Turning something down | "I mean, I'd love to, it's just that I kind of…" | "I can't this week. Next Tuesday works." |
| Giving an opinion socially | "I don't know, I sort of liked it, I guess?" | "I liked it. The ending surprised me." |
| Correcting someone | "Sorry, this is really small, but I think maybe…" | "Quick correction: the meeting's at three, not two." |

Notice the direct versions aren't rude. They're shorter, and they still leave room for the other person to reply.

## 5. Let silence work for you

People who sound confident are comfortable with a one-second pause. They pause before an important word, after a question, and when they've finished. Nervous speakers fill every gap with "um", "so" or "you know" or keep talking after they've made their point.

Try this: after you finish a point, close your mouth and count "one, two" in your head. If nobody speaks, add one more sentence or ask a question. The [filler word counter](/speaking-coach/tools/filler-word-counter/) shows how often you fill gaps without noticing.

## A one-week practice plan

| Day | Focus | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Notice | Record a two-minute voice note about your day. Listen once. Pick your biggest habit. |
| 2 | Volume | Speak one notch louder in every conversation |
| 3 | Endings | In three conversations, land your last word on purpose |
| 4 | Hedges | Catch yourself saying "just" or "sorry" and drop it next time |
| 5 | Pauses | After each point, pause for two counts |
| 6 | Combine | Give your opinion in a meeting or group chat, point first |
| 7 | Review | Record the same two-minute voice note. Compare. |

Rehearsing a specific tricky conversation helps too. Speaking Coach has scenes like "Disagree in a meeting", "Ask for a raise" and "Say no", where an AI partner pushes back so you can practice holding your point out loud.

## Questions people ask

### How can I sound confident when I'm not?

Change the behaviour, not the feeling. Speak a little louder, finish your sentences and cut hedges. Listeners respond to how you sound, and their reaction tends to make you feel steadier.

### Is it rude to stop saying sorry?

No. Keep "sorry" for when you've actually done something wrong. Swapping "sorry to bother you" for "thanks for your time" sounds polite and more confident.

### Why do I get quieter when I talk to certain people?

Often it's people with more status, like a boss or someone you find intimidating. Plan your first sentence before you speak to them and say it at your normal volume.

### Does speaking slowly make you sound more confident?

A steady pace helps, especially at the start of a sentence, but very slow speech can sound stiff. Aim for a normal conversational pace with real pauses rather than rushing.

### How do I sound confident in a second language?

The same habits work: finish sentences, pause instead of filling, and keep your volume up. A clear, direct sentence with an accent sounds more confident than a perfect one mumbled.
