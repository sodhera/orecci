## Why nerves feel worse the morning of

Nerves before an interview are normal and they don't mean you're unprepared. They get worse when your mind has nothing to do with them: lying awake rehearsing, sitting in the car, waiting in reception. The plan below gives every stretch of waiting a small, concrete job.

## The night before

1. **Stop preparing by early evening.** Pick a time, such as 7 pm, and stop adding new research after it. Late cramming tends to raise anxiety more than it raises knowledge.
2. **Write three stories on one card.** One success, one problem you solved, one mistake you learned from. Three lines each. Most behavioral questions can be answered with one of these.
3. **Say your opening answer out loud twice.** Usually that's "Tell me about yourself". Out loud, not in your head. Then put the card away.
4. **Lay everything out.** Clothes, directions or the video link, a printed CV, water, charger. Decisions you make tonight are decisions you don't make in a hurry tomorrow.
5. **Plan the morning backwards.** Interview at 10:00? Leave home by 9:15, eat by 8:30, up by 7:30. Write it down.
6. **Do something ordinary.** A walk, a show, a call with a friend. If you're still awake running answers, write the worry on paper and leave it there.

## The morning

- **Eat something,** even if it's small. An empty stomach on top of nerves makes many people feel shakier.
- **Warm up your voice.** Your voice is often rough or quiet first thing. Hum for 30 seconds, then read a paragraph of anything out loud. In the car or shower is fine.
- **Do one warm-up answer.** Answer an easy question out loud, start to finish. The first time you speak about yourself today should not be in front of the interviewer.
- **Skip the extra coffee** if caffeine makes you jittery. Keep your usual amount, not double.
- **Look at your card once.** Then put it away.

## 30 minutes to 2 minutes before

| Time | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| 30 min | Arrive or log in early. In person, walk around the block instead of sitting in the car. |
| 15 min | Check in or open the call link. Phone on silent. |
| 10 min | Breathe slowly: in through your nose for about 4 seconds, out through your mouth for about 6. Repeat for two minutes. A longer exhale than inhale tends to settle people. |
| 5 min | Drop your shoulders, unclench your jaw, put both feet flat on the floor. |
| 2 min | Say your first sentence in your head: "Thanks for having me." Know exactly how you'll start. |

If you're waiting in reception, it's fine to chat with the receptionist. Small talk before the interview is a gentle warm-up, and the [small talk before an interview guide](/speaking-coach/small-talk/small-talk-before-an-interview/) has easy lines.

## The first minute in the room

The first minute is mostly greetings and small talk, which makes it the best place to settle. Use it.

- **Slow down your first sentences on purpose.** Nerves speed people up. Speak a little slower than feels natural.
- **Take a breath before your first real answer.** One second of silence is invisible to the interviewer.
- **Keep the first answer shorter.** Aim for about a minute. You can add detail when they follow up.
- **Have water.** Taking a sip is a natural way to buy two seconds later.

## If you blank on a question

Blanking happens to prepared people too. What matters is that you keep going calmly.

1. **Pause and say so.** "Let me think about that for a second." This sounds thoughtful, not lost.
2. **Repeat the question.** "So, a time I disagreed with my manager…" Saying it out loud often brings an example back.
3. **Reach for your card stories.** One of your three stories almost always fits, even loosely.
4. **Start with one true sentence.** "The first thing that comes to mind is…" Once you're talking, the rest usually follows.
5. **If nothing comes, ask to return to it.** "Could I come back to that one? I want to give you a real example." Then actually come back to it before the end.

The [mind goes blank guide](/speaking-coach/guides/mind-goes-blank-while-speaking/) has more recovery lines.

## What not to do

| Habit | Why it backfires | Try instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reading your notes up to the last second | Keeps your mind racing | Put notes away 10 minutes before |
| Telling yourself "don't be nervous" | Fighting the feeling makes it louder | "I'm ready, and it's okay to feel this" |
| Rehearsing every answer in the waiting room | Tires you out before you start | Rehearse only your first sentence |
| Apologizing for being nervous | Draws attention to it | Just slow down; most nerves aren't visible |

The best way to calm the nerves is to have already said your answers out loud to someone. Speaking Coach can play the interviewer at a Gentle, Realistic or Tough pressure level, and if you freeze you can say "I need a moment" to pause.

## Questions people ask

### Is it normal to feel sick with nerves before an interview?

Feeling jittery, a racing heart or a tight stomach before an interview is very common. If anxiety is severe or stops you from going to interviews at all, it's worth talking to a doctor.

### Should I tell the interviewer I'm nervous?

You can, briefly and once, if it helps you relax: "I'm a little nervous, because I really want this one." Then move on. Don't keep apologizing.

### How early should I arrive?

Arrive in the area 20 to 30 minutes early, then check in about 10 minutes before. For video interviews, log in and test sound and camera 15 minutes ahead.

### Does practicing more make nerves worse?

Practicing out loud in the days before usually makes nerves easier, because the answers feel familiar. Cramming new material the night before tends to do the opposite.

### What if my voice shakes when I start?

Slow down, breathe out fully before your first sentence and start at a calm, easy volume. The [guide to a shaky voice](/speaking-coach/guides/how-to-stop-voice-shaking/) has a two-minute routine.
