## Why recordings work

When you speak, you're busy thinking about what to say next. You can't also listen to how it sounds. A recording separates the two jobs: you speak first, then listen with full attention. It shows things you can't notice in the moment, such as how fast you go when nervous, which filler you lean on, or that your best point gets buried in the middle.

## Setup: keep it simple

| What | Do this | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Device** | Your phone is enough | You'll actually do it if it's easy |
| **Audio or video** | Audio for voice habits; video once a week for body language | Audio-only is less uncomfortable to start with |
| **Position** | Phone at about eye level, an arm's length or more away | Shows you as a listener would see you |
| **Room** | Quiet, with soft furnishings if possible | Echo and noise hide what you're listening for |
| **Length** | Two to three minutes | Long enough to show habits, short enough to review |
| **What to say** | The real thing: your interview answer, the opening of your talk, a work update | Practice on what matters, not random reading |

Stand if you'll stand on the day. Speak at the volume you'll use. If it's an interview answer, have someone, or a written card, ask the question first so you don't get a running start.

## The playback checklist

Don't try to catch everything in one listen. Use separate passes, each with one focus. Write notes as you go.

### Pass 1: Listen for pace and fillers

| Check | What to look for | How to measure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Pace** | Do you speed up when nervous or at the end? | Count words in one minute, or use the [pace checker](/speaking-coach/tools/pace-checker/) |
| **Fillers** | Which filler is most frequent: um, like, so, you know? | Tally by hand, or use the [filler word counter](/speaking-coach/tools/filler-word-counter/) |
| **Pauses** | Do you pause between ideas, or run everything together? | Mark where pauses fall on a transcript |
| **Long silences** | Any freezes where you lost your place? | Note the time stamp |

As a reference, conversational English is commonly around 120–160 words per minute, and presentations often land around 130–150.

### Pass 2: Opening and ending

- **How quickly did you start?** A long silence or several "um"s before the first sentence?
- **What was your first sentence?** Did it get to the point, or warm up first ("So, yeah, basically…")?
- **What was your last sentence?** Did it land, or trail off with "…so yeah, that's it"?

Openings and endings are what listeners remember most, and they're the easiest parts to fix.

### Pass 3: Video with the sound off (weekly)

- Where are your eyes? Down at notes, at the ceiling, at the camera?
- What are your hands doing? Fidgeting, hidden, or gesturing with your points?
- Posture: swaying, leaning, rocking?
- Face: does it match what you're saying?

Watching without sound stops your attention going to the words.

### Pass 4: Voice

- Does your pitch move, or stay on one note? The [monotone guide](/speaking-coach/guides/how-to-stop-sounding-monotone/) helps if it's flat.
- Does your voice drop away at the end of sentences?
- Is your volume steady and comfortable to listen to?

## One change per round

After reviewing, pick **one** thing to change, the one that would make the biggest difference to a listener. Then record the same piece again and check only that.

> **Example review log:**
>
> Round 1: 172 wpm, 11 "so"s, ended with "so yeah". Change: slow down, pause at full stops.
>
> Round 2: 148 wpm, 9 "so"s, better ending. Change: start answers without "so".
>
> Round 3: 145 wpm, 3 "so"s. Keep.

Keeping a short log like this shows progress you'd otherwise forget, which helps you keep going.

## Getting past the cringe

Almost everyone dislikes hearing their own recorded voice at first. Part of the reason is that you normally hear your own voice partly through your head as well as through the air, so a recording sounds different from the voice you know. Other people hear the recorded version all the time, and to them it sounds normal.

What helps:

- **Start with audio only.** Video adds a second thing to be self-conscious about.
- **Review like a coach, not a critic.** Write "what to keep" before "what to change".
- **Look for patterns, not flaws.** "I speed up after the first minute" is useful. "I sound awful" isn't.
- **Give it a few sessions.** The discomfort usually fades quickly once your voice becomes familiar.
- **Delete freely.** These recordings are for you. You don't have to keep them.

## When to get outside feedback

Recordings show you what you do, but not always what a listener makes of it. Every few rounds, get another view: a friend, a colleague, or a tool. The [free speech review](/speaking-coach/tools/free-speech-review/) measures pace, filler words and pauses in your browser. Speaking Coach goes further: after each session you see how you sounded, what to keep, and tips that quote something you actually said, then you can retry the same moment for 90 seconds and see what changed.

## Questions people ask

### Why does my recorded voice sound so weird?

You usually hear your own voice partly through your head while speaking, so the recording sounds unfamiliar. Other people hear the recorded version, and it sounds normal to them.

### How often should I record myself?

For a specific event, record each rehearsal or every other one. For general improvement, two or three short recordings a week is a good habit.

### Should I record audio or video?

Start with audio for voice habits like pace, fillers and pauses. Add video once a week to check eye contact, hands and posture.

### What should I record myself saying?

The real thing you're preparing for: an interview answer, the opening of a talk, a work update. If you have nothing coming up, explain your job or retell your week for two minutes.

### How do I know if I'm improving?

Keep a short log of one or two numbers per recording, such as words per minute and filler count, plus one note. Compare the first and the fifth recording on the same topic.
