## The four-part answer

| Part | What you say | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Admit it** | A short, plain statement. No long apology. | "I don't know that one." |
| **Give what you know** | The nearest thing you're sure about | "What I can tell you is…" |
| **Show how you'd find out** | Your reasoning or where you'd look | "I'd start by checking…" |
| **Commit to a follow-up** | What, who, and when, if it fits the setting | "I'll send it by Friday." |

You won't always need all four. In a quick meeting, "I don't know, I'll find out by tomorrow" is enough. In an interview, the middle two matter most, because they show how you think.

## Honest phrases for each setting

### In a job interview

Interviewers sometimes ask questions you can't answer on purpose, to see how you handle the edge of your knowledge.

- "I haven't worked with that tool directly. The closest I've used is X, and here's how I'd approach learning it."
- "I'm not sure, but let me reason through it out loud."
- "I don't know the exact answer. My best guess is X, because Y. Is that the direction you were thinking?"
- "That's outside what I've done so far. Here's what I would do in my first week to get up to speed."

Thinking out loud is especially welcome in technical interviews. The [technical interview guide](/speaking-coach/interviews/technical-interview/) covers it in detail.

### In a meeting

- "I don't have that number with me. I'll check and post it in the channel by end of day."
- "I don't know yet. That's something we should find out before we decide."
- "Good question. Who's closest to that? Maybe Tom knows."
- "I can tell you how it worked last year, but I'd want to confirm it's still the same."

### In a presentation Q&A

- "I don't know, and I don't want to guess in front of everyone. Can I follow up with you after?"
- "That's beyond what our study looked at. What we did find was…"
- "I'm not the right person for that, but [name] is, and I'll connect you."

## Partial answers: say what you can

Often you know part of the answer. Lead with the part you're sure of and be clear about where the certainty ends.

> **Example, in a meeting:** "I know the delay is on the supplier side and that it's affecting the two largest orders. I don't yet know whether it will push past the 20th. I'll have that from them by Wednesday."

That answer is useful even though it's incomplete. The listener knows what's certain, what isn't and when the gap closes.

Words that mark the line clearly:

| Certain | Uncertain |
| --- | --- |
| "I know that…" | "I'm not sure whether…" |
| "The data shows…" | "My guess is… but I'd want to check." |
| "We've confirmed…" | "I haven't confirmed…" |

Avoid hedging everything ("I think maybe it's probably around…"). Be firm about what you know so your uncertainty about the rest sounds deliberate.

## Follow-up promises that sound competent

A vague "I'll get back to you" is easy to say and easy to forget. A specific one builds trust.

| Weak | Strong |
| --- | --- |
| "I'll look into it." | "I'll check with legal and email you by Thursday." |
| "Let me get back to you." | "I'll send the figure in the channel after this meeting." |
| "I'll find out." | "I'll ask the vendor today and update you tomorrow morning." |

Then keep the promise. In an interview, you can follow up in your thank-you email: "You asked about X. I looked into it and…" It turns a gap into a good impression.

## What not to do

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bluffing | If you're wrong, people doubt everything else you said | Say what you're sure of and stop there |
| Long apologies | Makes a small gap feel big | One short, calm sentence |
| Freezing in silence | Looks like panic | "Let me think about that for a second." |
| Answering a different question | Listeners notice the dodge | Name the gap, then give the related fact |
| Promising without a time | Easy to forget, sounds evasive | A specific person and day |

## Practice the moment

The hard part isn't knowing the phrases; it's saying one calmly when everyone is waiting. Pick two phrases that sound like you and practice them out loud until they come automatically. ## Questions people ask

### Is it okay to say "I don't know" in a job interview?

Yes, if you follow it with what you do know or how you'd work it out. It's usually better than guessing, and many interviewers respect it.

### How do I say "I don't know" professionally?

Keep it short and pair it with a next step: "I don't have that information right now. I'll find out and get back to you by tomorrow."

### Should I guess if I'm not sure?

Only if you label it as a guess and explain your reasoning: "My best guess is X, because Y." Never present a guess as a fact.

### What if I don't understand the question?

Ask for clarification: "Do you mean the cost to us or to the customer?" Rephrasing it back also gives you a moment to think.

### How do I avoid looking incompetent when I don't know something?

Be confident about what you do know, specific about how you'll find out, and fast with the follow-up. That combination tends to make you look reliable rather than unprepared.
