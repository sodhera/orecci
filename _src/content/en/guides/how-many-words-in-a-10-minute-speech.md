## The word count for 10 minutes

| Pace | Words in 10 minutes |
| --- | --- |
| Slow (110 words per minute) | 1,100 |
| Average (140 words per minute) | 1,400 |
| Fast (170 words per minute) | 1,700 |
| **Write this many** (80–90% of average) | 1,120–1,260 |

Conversational English is commonly cited at about 120 to 160 words per minute, and presentations often land around 130 to 150. Over ten minutes, slow and fast end up 600 words apart, more than four minutes of talking at an average pace.

Slides cost time that holds no words: the click, the glance at the screen, "as you can see here". With slides, plan near the bottom of the range, around 1,100 to 1,150 words: the slow pace the [speech length calculator](/speaking-coach/tools/speech-length-calculator/) suggests for slide talks.

**In pages,** 1,400 words is nearly three single-spaced pages, or five to six double-spaced, using the rough rule of thumb of 500 words to a single-spaced page of 12-point text. That is too much to read from: write the script to find your words, then speak from notes.

## What fits in 10 minutes

Three sections, each with its own point, plus an opening that says where you are going and a close that brings people back. Ten minutes is long enough for listeners to lose their place, so they need a structure they can hear: a roadmap at the start ("three things: what went wrong, what we changed, what didn't work") and a [signpost](/speaking-coach/glossary/signposting/) at each change of section ("That's the problem. Here's what we did about it."). Ten minutes suits:

- a **short conference or meetup slot**; check whether questions come out of your ten minutes ([conference talks](/speaking-coach/presentations/conference-talk/))
- a **team presentation**, such as a project review or a class project split between three or four speakers ([group presentations](/speaking-coach/presentations/group-presentation/))
- a **short keynote at a small event**, such as a team offsite or a society dinner
- a **job interview presentation** with a ten-minute brief ([what panels expect](/speaking-coach/interviews/interview-presentation/))

Each section is a small talk of its own: a claim, one example or chart, and what it means for this audience. A fourth section doesn't fit: four would get about 245 words each, under two minutes, which is fine for a status update and thin for an argument.

## A timing plan for 10 minutes

| Part | Time | Words | Slides (example) | What goes in it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Opening | 45 seconds | 105 | 1 | A specific moment or number, then your message |
| Roadmap | 15 seconds | 35 | 0 | The three sections, said out loud instead of an agenda slide |
| Section one | 2 minutes 30 seconds | 350 | 2 | The problem, or your first point, with its evidence |
| Section two | 2 minutes 30 seconds | 350 | 3 | The main point: what you did or found |
| Section three | 2 minutes | 280 | 2 | What it means for the audience, or what didn't work |
| The close | 45 seconds | 105 | 1 | The message again, one thing to do, where to find you |
| Slide changes and pauses | 1 minute 15 seconds | 0 | | Clicks, glances at the screen, laughs, water |
| **Total** | **10 minutes** | **1,225** | **9** | |

Words are counted at 140 per minute and rounded to the nearest 5. Give the most time to the section with your strongest evidence. For ten-minute timings as problem–solution, past–present–future or a story, see [how to structure a presentation](/speaking-coach/guides/how-to-structure-a-presentation/).

## A 140-word opening to time yourself

This made-up opening of a ten-minute conference talk is exactly 140 words. It makes the problem specific, then gives the roadmap. Read it out loud with a timer.

> On a Saturday morning two years ago, our food bank opened its doors with forty families waiting outside and three volunteers inside. Fifty-two people were on the schedule that day. Most of them had a good reason not to come, and not one of them had told us. I'm Ruth, and for six years I've coordinated the volunteers at a small-town food bank. In the next ten minutes I want to convince you of one thing: people don't miss shifts because they don't care. They miss shifts because our schedules make it easy to forget. I'll show you three things. First, what our no-shows actually looked like, week by week. Second, the two small changes that cut them by more than half. And third, the change that made everything worse, so you can skip it. Let's start with that Saturday.

Your pace is 8,400 divided by your time in seconds. Over ten minutes, the difference adds up:

| Your time for the sample | Your pace | 10 minutes at your pace | Write this with slides |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 50 seconds | 168 words per minute | 1,680 words | about 1,340 words |
| 55 seconds | 153 words per minute | 1,530 words | about 1,220 words |
| 60 seconds | 140 words per minute | 1,400 words | about 1,120 words |
| 65 seconds | 129 words per minute | 1,290 words | about 1,030 words |
| 70 seconds | 120 words per minute | 1,200 words | about 960 words |

The last column is 80% of the one before it, rounded to the nearest 10: the bottom of the range, because slides cost time. Twenty seconds' difference on the sample becomes 480 words over the whole talk, about 3 minutes 26 seconds at an average pace. A 1,400-word script written for an average pace takes 12 minutes 44 seconds for a slow speaker.

The opening is only a sample, so time one full run with your slides, or measure it with the [pace checker](/speaking-coach/tools/pace-checker/). In Speaking Coach you can add your slides as a PDF, swipe through them while you speak, then answer the questions your audience would ask.

## Slides for a 10-minute presentation

There's no fixed number. Somewhere between 6 and 12 suits most ten-minute talks, and the plan above uses 9. It depends on the slides: one photo can be on screen for 20 seconds, while a chart you walk through can hold the room for two minutes. You can usually skip the agenda and "about me" slides: say the roadmap out loud, and who you are in one sentence.

## What to cut when you're over

At ten minutes, cut a section or an example, not a word here and there. Dropping one example of 70 to 140 words frees 30 to 60 seconds at once. Cut in this order:

1. **The agenda and "about me" slides,** and the talking over them.
2. **Background and method.** One sentence, such as "We tracked every Saturday shift for six months," not the spreadsheet.
3. **The second example in each section.** One per section is enough.
4. **Detailed tables.** Put them in a handout and say the one number that matters.
5. **An optional half of section three.** Decide before the day which half you would drop.

Then plan a cut for the day itself. With pauses spread through the talk, you should start section two at about 4:00. If you get there after 4:30, drop that optional half. Never cut the close, and don't eat into the time for questions.

## Questions people ask

### How many words is a 10-minute speech?

About 1,400 words at an average pace of 140 words per minute, and between 1,100 and 1,700 depending on your pace. For a talk with slides or pauses, write 1,120 to 1,260.

### How many pages is a 10-minute speech?

Nearly three single-spaced pages, or five to six double-spaced, at roughly 500 words to a single-spaced page.

### How many slides do I need for a 10-minute presentation?

Usually 6 to 12, depending on what is on them. Fewer, simpler slides are easier to follow than many you have to rush.

### Is 1,500 words too long for a 10-minute speech?

Yes, for most speakers. 1,500 words takes 10 minutes 43 seconds at 140 words per minute before any pauses, and 8:49 even at a fast 170. Cut it to about 1,200, usually by dropping one example from each section.

### How many words is a 15-minute speech?

About 2,100 words at 140 words per minute, and between 1,650 and 2,550 depending on your pace. For a real 15-minute slot, write about 1,680 to 1,890.
