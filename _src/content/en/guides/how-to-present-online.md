## Why online is harder

In a room, the audience has nowhere else to look. Online, your talk competes with email, messages and a second screen, and you can't see whether people are with you. Many online presentations fail not because the content is weak but because the speaker presents exactly as they would in a room, at the same energy and pace, without checking in.

This guide covers keeping attention. For looking into the camera, see the [eye contact on video calls guide](/speaking-coach/guides/eye-contact-on-video-calls/).

## Energy for the camera

A webcam flattens you. Small expressions and gestures shrink, and your voice comes through a laptop speaker. To come across as engaged, you need to give a little more than feels natural.

- **Stand up if you can.** Raise the laptop or camera to eye level. Standing tends to give your voice more energy and makes gestures easier.
- **Gesture inside the frame.** Sit or stand far enough back that your hands show when you gesture at chest height.
- **Lift your voice variety.** Vary pitch and pace a bit more than usual. Flat delivery is even flatter over a call. The [monotone guide](/speaking-coach/guides/how-to-stop-sounding-monotone/) has drills.
- **Smile at the start.** A warm first ten seconds sets the tone for people deciding whether to keep watching.
- **Use a separate microphone if you have one,** even a basic headset. Clear audio matters more than video.

## Interaction every three to five minutes

People stay with you when they have something to do. Plan an interaction point at least every three to five minutes, and write them into your notes.

| Interaction | How | Good for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Chat waterfall** | "Type your answer in the chat, but don't press enter until I say go." | Getting everyone to answer at once |
| **Quick poll** | A built-in poll with two to four options | Opinions, checking knowledge |
| **Reactions** | "Give a thumbs up if you've used this before." | Quick pulse checks in big groups |
| **Name a person** | "Priya, you've seen this in sales. Does it match?" | Small meetings; warn people beforehand |
| **Unmute question** | "Can one person unmute and tell me…" | Teams that know each other |
| **Think-pause** | "Take ten seconds and jot down one example." | Training and workshops |

Start with an easy one in the first two minutes, such as "Type where you're joining from in the chat." Once people have interacted once, they're more likely to do it again.

## Slide sharing that works

Slides on a video call are seen in a small window, often next to faces, sometimes on a phone.

1. **Share a window, not your whole screen.** It hides your notifications and other tabs.
2. **Use presenter view on a second screen,** or print your notes, so you're not switching windows.
3. **Fewer words, bigger text.** One idea per slide. If someone on a laptop can't read it, cut it.
4. **Turn off notifications** on your computer and phone.
5. **Test sharing before the call** with a colleague, including any video or sound in your slides.
6. **Stop sharing for discussions.** Going back to gallery view brings faces back and signals "your turn".

## Handling the chat

Chat is a gift, but reading it while presenting splits your attention.

- **Assign a chat watcher** if the session matters. Ask them to pass on questions at set points or message you privately.
- **Set chat times.** "I'll check the chat at the end of each section." Then do it.
- **Read questions aloud** before answering, with the person's name: "Sam asks whether this applies to contractors."
- **Acknowledge comments you can't answer now:** "Good point in the chat about budget. I'll come back to that."
- **Don't read every message.** Group similar questions and answer them together.

The [Q&A guide](/speaking-coach/guides/how-to-handle-q-and-a/) covers tough questions and ending on your point.

## A structure for a 20-minute online talk

This is an example plan. Adjust it to your topic.

| Minutes | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| 0–2 | Warm opening, one easy chat question |
| 2–6 | First section, then a poll |
| 6–10 | Second section, then name someone for a quick reaction |
| 10–14 | Third section, chat waterfall |
| 14–18 | Questions, read from chat |
| 18–20 | Prepared close and one clear next step |

## The ten-minute pre-check

1. Join early. Check camera, microphone and lighting.
2. Close every app you won't use.
3. Open your slides and test sharing the window.
4. Put water within reach.
5. Set your phone to silent and face down.
6. Say your first two sentences out loud.

## Practice the online version

Rehearse online talks out loud, at presentation energy, ideally on the same setup. ## Questions people ask

### How do I keep people engaged on Zoom?

Give them something to do every three to five minutes, keep slides simple and readable, and speak with a bit more energy than you would in a room.

### Should I have my camera on while presenting?

Yes, if you can. Seeing your face helps people stay connected. Use speaker view or a small video window so your face shows alongside the slides.

### Is it better to stand or sit when presenting online?

Standing often gives more vocal energy and easier gestures, but sitting is fine if the camera is at eye level and you sit upright. Choose what keeps you comfortable and in frame.

### How do I present online without seeing faces?

Build in reactions and chat questions so you get signals, and ask a colleague to message you if you lose people. Speak to the camera as if it were one person.

### How long should a virtual presentation be?

Shorter than in person if you can. Many online talks work best at 20 minutes or less, with interaction built in. Longer sessions need breaks and more frequent activities.
