## The word count for 1 minute

| Pace | Words in 1 minute |
| --- | --- |
| Slow (110 words per minute) | 110 |
| Average (140 words per minute) | 140 |
| Fast (170 words per minute) | 170 |
| **Write this many** (80–90% of average) | 112–126 |

Conversational English is commonly cited at about 120 to 160 words per minute, so 140 is a fair middle. Write under it, because a real minute is not all talking: you breathe, you wait for a nod, you pause before your last line. For any other length, use the [speech length calculator](/speaking-coach/tools/speech-length-calculator/).

**On paper,** 140 words is a little over a quarter of a page. That assumes about 500 words to a single-spaced page of 12-point text, which is only a rough rule of thumb. Fonts and margins change it, so count words, not lines.

## What fits in 1 minute

One point. A minute is the right size for:

- an **elevator pitch**, when someone has asked for more than a sentence ([how to write one](/speaking-coach/guides/elevator-pitch/))
- a **self-introduction** to a class, a club or a new team ([examples for class](/speaking-coach/speeches/self-introduction-speech/))
- a **short toast** at a dinner or a leaving party
- a **one-minute answer**, when the person running a meeting says "give us the short version"

What doesn't fit: a second point, a story that needs more than two sentences of setup, a list longer than three items, or thanks to more than one person.

A quick test: say your point in one sentence. If you need "and" to join two ideas, you have two speeches. Pick one.

## A timing plan for 60 seconds

| Part | Time | Words | What goes in it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Opening line | 10 seconds | 25 | Your name, or the point itself. No warm-up |
| The one point, with one example | 30 seconds | 70 | The detail people will repeat to someone else |
| Closing line | 10 seconds | 25 | What you want them to do, ask or remember |
| Pauses | 10 seconds | 0 | A breath after the opening, a beat before the close |
| **Total** | **60 seconds** | **120** | |

Words are counted at 140 per minute and rounded to the nearest 5.

## A 140-word sample to time yourself

This made-up self-introduction to a new team is exactly 140 words. Read it out loud at your normal speed with a stopwatch running.

> Good morning, everyone. My name is Dana Okafor, and I joined the support team on Monday. Before this I spent six years in a bike shop, fixing whatever rolled through the door. That job taught me one thing I still use daily. People rarely describe the real problem first. A customer would say the brakes felt strange, and the trouble was a bent wheel. So I learned to ask one more question before picking up a tool. I plan to work the same way here. When you send me a ticket, expect a question back before an answer. It is not doubt. It is how I get things right the first time.
>
> Away from work, I am rebuilding an old touring bike, slowly and badly. If you know anything about gears, come and find me. I would love the help.

If that took you 60 seconds, you speak at 140 words per minute. If it didn't, your pace is 8,400 divided by your time in seconds.

| Your time | Your pace | Write this for a 1-minute slot |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 50 seconds | 168 words per minute | about 145 words |
| 55 seconds | 153 words per minute | about 130 words |
| 60 seconds | 140 words per minute | about 120 words |
| 65 seconds | 129 words per minute | about 110 words |
| 70 seconds | 120 words per minute | about 100 words |

The last column is 85% of your pace, rounded to the nearest 5. If you finished well under 55 seconds, try again as if a room were listening. Nerves usually speed you up on the day, and a rushed minute is harder to follow than a short one.

The sample itself is too long for a strict one-minute slot: 140 words leaves no room to pause. Drop the last paragraph about the bike (28 words) and you have 112 words, which is 48 seconds of talking and 12 seconds of air. To measure your pace from your own words instead, use the [pace checker](/speaking-coach/tools/pace-checker/).

## Slides for a 1-minute speech

Usually none. If the format gives every speaker a slide, as some team introductions and pitch rounds do, use one, and make it a picture, a name or a single number. A slide with sentences on it gets read while you talk, and in 60 seconds you can't afford to compete with your own slide. It depends on the slide, but more than one is hard to justify.

## What to cut when you're over

At this length, 15 extra words is about 6 seconds, a tenth of your time. Cut in this order:

1. **The warm-up.** "Thanks so much for having me, I'm really excited to be here" is 12 words and tells nobody anything.
2. **The second example.** One is enough to prove one point.
3. **The list.** Three hobbies or four job titles become the one that someone might ask you about.
4. **The recap.** Nobody needs a summary of something they heard 40 seconds ago.
5. **Hedges.** "I guess what I'm trying to say is" can go every time.

Keep your name, the one concrete detail and your last line.

## Questions people ask

### How many words is a 1-minute speech?

About 140 words at an average pace, and between 110 and 170 depending on how fast you speak. For a real one-minute slot, write 110 to 125.

### How many words is a 30-second speech?

About 70 words at 140 words per minute, and between 55 and 85 depending on your pace.

### How many pages is a 1-minute speech?

A little over a quarter of a single-spaced page, or about half a page double-spaced, if a single-spaced page holds roughly 500 words. That is a rough guide, so count words.

### Is 200 words too long for a 1-minute speech?

Yes. 200 words takes about 1 minute 26 seconds at 140 words per minute, and 1 minute 11 seconds even at a fast 170. Cut it to about 120.

### How many sentences is a 1-minute speech?

It depends on how long your sentences are. With spoken sentences of 10 to 15 words, 140 words is roughly 9 to 14 sentences. The sample on this page has 14.
