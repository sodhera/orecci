## Why a story helps in a work talk

Data tells people what is true. A story shows them what it's like. In a presentation full of charts, a short story is often the moment people sit up, and the part they repeat to someone afterwards. But only if it's short and clearly connected to your point. A long, wandering anecdote does the opposite.

## The 60-second story arc

| Part | What it does | Time | Starter |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Moment** | Drop the audience into a specific time and place | ~10 s | "Last March, on a Tuesday night…" |
| **Stakes** | Show what could go wrong, or what someone wanted | ~15 s | "If the order didn't ship by Friday…" |
| **Turn** | What happened, what changed, what someone did | ~20 s | "Then she tried something different…" |
| **Point** | One sentence linking it to your message | ~10 s | "That's why we're proposing…" |

Sixty seconds is about 130 to 150 words at a typical presentation pace. Write your story, count the words, and check it with the [speech length calculator](/speaking-coach/tools/speech-length-calculator/).

## An example, before and after

This is a made-up example for a talk proposing a new customer support process.

### Before: the story that drags (about two minutes)

> "So, I want to tell you about something that happened. A while back, I think it was early last year, maybe February, we had a customer, I won't say the name, it was a mid-size company, they'd been with us for a few years, and they had an issue with their invoices. Now, our billing system at the time, which some of you will remember, was the old one before we migrated, and it had this quirk where…"

The audience is 30 seconds in and nothing has happened yet.

### After: the 60-second version

> "Last February, a customer emailed us at 11 pm: their payroll was due at 9 the next morning and our invoice was blocking it. The ticket went into a queue. Nobody saw it until 10. By then they'd missed payroll for 40 people. The fix took our engineer four minutes. Four minutes, after an eleven-hour wait. That gap, between how fast we can fix things and how fast we find out, is what this proposal is about."

What changed:

- Starts in the moment, not with "I want to tell you about…"
- One clear stake (payroll for 40 people) instead of background
- The turn is a contrast (four minutes vs eleven hours)
- The last sentence names the point

## Where to put the story

| Placement | When it works |
| --- | --- |
| **The opening** | You need attention fast, or the topic feels dry. Skip the welcome and start with the moment. |
| **Just before key data** | The story makes people care, then the numbers show it's not a one-off. |
| **At a turning point** | Moving from problem to solution. The story shows the problem in human terms. |
| **Before your call to action** | A short story about what success looks like makes the ask concrete. |

One story per 10 to 15 minutes of talk is plenty for most work presentations. More than that and it starts to feel like you're avoiding the substance. The [opening a speech guide](/speaking-coach/guides/how-to-start-a-speech/) has more on story openings.

## Details that land

The difference between a forgettable story and a memorable one is usually one or two specific details.

- **A precise time or number.** "11 pm", "40 people", "four minutes". Specific beats vague.
- **One line of dialogue.** "She said, 'Just tell me if it's going to be fixed by morning.'" Short, real-sounding speech makes a scene come alive. Keep it clearly illustrative if it's a composite.
- **One sensory detail, at most.** "The office was empty except for the cleaner." Enough to picture it.
- **A person, not a segment.** "A store manager in Leeds" is more vivid than "some of our retail customers". Change names and details to protect privacy.

## How to cut a story that drags

Read your story out loud and time it. If it's over 90 seconds:

1. **Cut everything before the moment.** "So, I want to tell you a story about…" and the backstory usually go.
2. **Cut names and history the audience doesn't need.** If a detail doesn't affect the stakes or the turn, delete it.
3. **Cut the second example.** If you have "and another time…", pick the stronger one.
4. **Replace explanation with one detail.** Instead of describing how stressful it was, say what the clock said.
5. **End on the point, not a recap.** Don't retell the story after telling it.

## Telling it well out loud

- **Slow down in the moment and the turn.** Rushing the important part is the most common delivery mistake.
- **Pause before the point.** A one- or two-second pause tells the audience: here's why this matters.
- **Don't read it.** Know the four beats and tell it in your own words. A story read from a slide sounds like a report.
- **Rehearse it more than the rest.** It's short, so five extra runs take five minutes.

Speaking Coach can be your test audience: in a presentation session you swipe through your slides while you speak, and the feedback afterwards includes your pace and long pauses, so you can hear whether the story rushes.

## Questions people ask

### How long should a story in a presentation be?

Usually 30 to 90 seconds. Long enough to have a moment, stakes and a turn; short enough that the audience doesn't wonder where it's going.

### Can I use a story in a business presentation?

Yes. A short customer, project or team story is common in pitches, updates and proposals. Keep it tied to the decision or message you want.

### What if I don't have a personal story?

Use a customer's or a colleague's story with permission and changed details, or a short, clearly labelled hypothetical: "Imagine you're the store manager at 8 am on a Monday…"

### Should I start my presentation with a story?

It's often a strong opening, especially for dry topics. Start straight in the moment, without announcing that a story is coming.

### How do I make sure the story connects to my point?

Write the final sentence first: "That's why…". If you can't finish it clearly, the story doesn't fit this talk.
