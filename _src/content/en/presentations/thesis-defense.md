## What the committee is actually checking

By the time you defend, your committee has usually read the thesis. The presentation is not there to tell them everything in it. It shows three things:

- **You can explain your work clearly** to people who haven't lived inside it.
- **You know where it's weak.** Limitations you name yourself sound like expertise; limitations they find sound like gaps.
- **The work is yours.** Questions on method and choices show whether you understood every decision.

Formats vary a lot between universities, countries and degree levels. Before anything else, find out: how long you present, whether the defense is public, how long questions last, and whether there is a closed session afterwards. Your supervisor and the graduate office are the sources for this.

## A slide structure that works

For a typical 20–30 minute master's defense or 30–45 minute PhD presentation, give each section a time budget:

| Section | Share of time | Slides (30 min) | What to say |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Title and question | 5% | 1–2 | Your research question in one sentence, and why it matters |
| Background and gap | 15% | 3–4 | Only the literature needed to show the gap you fill |
| Method | 20% | 4–5 | What you did and why you chose it over the obvious alternative |
| Findings | 35% | 6–8 | Your main results, one per slide, each with a plain-English title |
| Contribution and limits | 15% | 2–3 | What's new, what it doesn't show, what you'd do next |
| Conclusion | 10% | 1–2 | The answer to your research question, restated |

A useful test: each findings slide should have a title that is a full sentence stating the result, such as "Shaded streets were 3°C cooler at peak", not "Results 2". If the committee only read your slide titles, they should get the whole argument.

For a PhD with several studies, don't give every study equal time. Pick the one or two that carry your main contribution and summarise the rest on one slide.

## Write your first and last minute word for word

The opening and closing are where nerves are worst and clarity matters most. Example opening for a made-up master's thesis:

> "My thesis asks one question: does adding street trees reduce summer heat for the people who live on that street, or only for the street itself? I measured indoor and outdoor temperatures in 40 homes over two summers. The short answer is that trees helped outdoors, but indoors the effect depended almost entirely on the building."

Example close:

> "So, back to the question: street trees cool the street, but they don't reliably cool homes. That matters for cities that count tree planting as a heat plan for residents. The next step I'd want to see is the same study in newer buildings."

Learn the middle as a sequence of points, not a script.

## Rehearse to time

1. **Do your first run alone, out loud, with a timer.** First runs almost always run long, because everything feels important.
2. **Cut background first.** The committee knows the field. Keep method and findings.
3. **Do a run for non-specialists**, such as friends or family. If they can't say back your research question, your opening needs work.
4. **Do a mock defense with your lab or peers**, with them asking real questions for as long as the real Q&A will last.
5. **Do a final run in the actual room** if you can, with the actual projector.

## Prepare for the questions

Write a list of 20–30 questions and practice answering them out loud, not just in your head. Most committee questions fall into these groups:

| Type | Example question | How to answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Method | "Why didn't you use a control group?" | Give the reason for your choice, then admit what it costs |
| Alternatives | "Couldn't this be explained by building age instead?" | Say what you did to rule it out, or say it's a limit |
| Significance | "So what? Who should care about this?" | Name a real group and what they'd do differently |
| Literature | "How does this fit with [a known study in your field]?" | Agree or disagree specifically, with a reason |
| Detail | "Walk me through how you cleaned this data." | Answer step by step; it's checking the work is yours |
| Future | "What would you do with another year?" | One concrete next study, not a wish list |

When answering:

- **Repeat or rephrase long questions** to check you understood, and to give yourself a few seconds.
- **Answer first, then explain.** "Yes, it could be building age. Here's why I don't think it is."
- **It's fine not to know.** "I didn't test that. My guess is X, and I could check it by Y." See [how to answer a question when you don't know](/speaking-coach/guides/answer-question-you-dont-know/).
- **Don't defend everything.** Accepting a fair criticism calmly is a strong answer.
- **Keep answers under about a minute** and let them follow up.

## Mistakes to avoid

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Presenting every chapter equally | You run out of time on findings | Weight time toward method and findings |
| Dense slides copied from the thesis | The committee reads instead of listening | One result per slide, sentence titles |
| Hiding limitations | They'll find them, and it looks evasive | Give limitations their own slide |
| Arguing with every question | Looks defensive | Accept fair points, defend core choices |
| Rushing at the end | The conclusion gets lost | Cut a slide rather than speed up |

## Questions people ask

### How long is a thesis defense?

It depends on the university and the degree. Master's presentations are often 15–30 minutes and PhD presentations 30–60, each followed by questions, but check your own department's rules.

### How many slides for a thesis defense presentation?

Roughly one slide per minute is a common starting point, so 20–30 for a 30-minute talk. Fewer, clearer slides are better than many crowded ones.

### What are the most common thesis defense questions?

"Why did you choose this method?", "What are the limitations?", "What is your contribution?", "How does this relate to [a key study]?" and "What would you do next?" Prepare a spoken answer to each.

### Can you fail a thesis defense?

It happens, but rules and outcomes vary a lot by institution, and many results ask for revisions rather than a pass or fail. Your supervisor usually wouldn't let you defend if the work wasn't ready; ask them directly if you're worried.

### Should I bring notes?

Brief notes are usually fine, and a printed copy of your thesis to look up details during questions is common. Check with your supervisor.
