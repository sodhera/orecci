## The word count for 5 minutes

Presentations commonly land around 130–150 words per minute. That gives you a budget:

| Your pace | Words in 5 minutes | Write this many (leaves room for pauses) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Slow and deliberate (120 wpm) | 600 | About 550 |
| Typical presentation (130–150 wpm) | 650–750 | About 600–650 |
| Fast (160 wpm) | 800 | About 700 |

Write to the lower end. Real talks run longer than practice: you pause for a laugh, repeat a point someone looked confused by, or wait for a slide. A script of 750 words almost always runs over 5 minutes on the day.

Measure your own pace once with the [pace checker](/speaking-coach/tools/pace-checker/), then use the [speech length calculator](/speaking-coach/tools/speech-length-calculator/) to turn your draft's word count into minutes.

## The structure, minute by minute

Five minutes is enough for **one point** well supported. It is not enough for three equal points. Build it like this:

| Time | Section | Words (approx.) | What goes in it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0:00–0:30 | Opening | 70 | Your point in one sentence, and why it matters to this audience |
| 0:30–1:45 | Support 1 | 170 | Your strongest reason or example |
| 1:45–3:00 | Support 2 | 170 | Second reason, ideally a different kind (a story if the first was data) |
| 3:00–3:45 | Objection or limit | 100 | The obvious "but…" and your answer |
| 3:45–4:30 | Close | 100 | Your point again, and what you want them to do or remember |
| 4:30–5:00 | Buffer | 0 | Empty on purpose |

That's about 610 words. The buffer is not optional; it's what stops you overrunning.

## How many slides

For 5 minutes, 3–5 slides is a comfortable number: a title, one per supporting point, and a closing slide that restates the point. More slides mean less time per slide, and you'll end up rushing through clicks.

Exceptions: timed formats such as Ignite (20 slides, auto-advancing) need many slides by design; see [how to give a lightning talk](/speaking-coach/presentations/lightning-talk/).

## An example outline

A made-up example for a team meeting, 5 minutes, on switching to shorter sprints:

1. **Opening (0:30).** "I think we should move from two-week sprints to one-week sprints for the next quarter. Here's why, and the one risk I see."
2. **Support 1 (1:15).** "Last quarter, four of our six sprints had work carried over. Shorter sprints force smaller tickets, which are easier to finish."
3. **Support 2 (1:15).** A short story: "On the billing project, we found the blocker on day nine. With one-week sprints we'd have raised it at a review on day five."
4. **Objection (0:45).** "The obvious worry is more meeting time. I'd cut the review to 30 minutes and keep planning to 45, so total meeting time stays about the same."
5. **Close (0:45).** "So: one-week sprints for one quarter, then we look at the carry-over numbers again. I'd like a yes or no by Friday."

## What to cut when you run over

First drafts of a 5-minute talk usually run well over. Cut in this order:

1. **Background and history.** "Before I start, a bit of context…" Most of it can go.
2. **Your third point.** If you have three supports, keep the two strongest.
3. **Qualifiers and asides.** "Of course, this varies, and there are many factors…"
4. **Duplicate examples.** One example per point is enough.
5. **Long lists.** Replace "there are seven reasons" with the best one.

Don't cut the opening sentence, the strongest example or the close. And don't fix overrunning by talking faster; that makes everything harder to follow.

## How to rehearse to exactly 5 minutes

1. **Read the draft aloud once with a timer** to get a baseline. Reading silently doesn't count; it's much faster than speaking.
2. **Cut to your word budget** using the list above.
3. **Rehearse from keywords, not the script**, at least three times, timing each.
4. **Note where you should be at 2:30.** If you're behind at the halfway point, you know to skip your objection section.
5. **Do the last run standing up, with slides**, as close to the real conditions as you can.

## When the 5 minutes are strict

Some settings cut you off at time: competitions, class assignments, conference slots, interview tasks. For these:

- Aim to finish at 4:30 in rehearsal.
- Have a "skip line" ready: "In the interest of time, let me go straight to the conclusion."
- If there's a visible timer, glance at it only at your section changes.

## Questions people ask

### How many words is a 5-minute speech?

About 650–750 words at a typical presentation pace. For a talk with slides or pauses, write closer to 600.

### How many slides for a 5-minute presentation?

Usually 3–5. Use more only if the format requires it, such as an auto-advancing lightning talk.

### How many points can I cover in 5 minutes?

One main point with two or three pieces of support. Trying to cover more turns the talk into a list.

### How do I start a 5-minute presentation?

State your point in the first sentence, then say why it matters to this audience. Skip the long introduction; the [guide to opening lines](/speaking-coach/guides/how-to-start-a-speech/) has examples.

### Can I read a 5-minute presentation from a script?

You can, but it usually sounds flat. Memorise the first and last lines and speak the middle from keywords.
