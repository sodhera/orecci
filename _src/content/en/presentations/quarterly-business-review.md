## What a QBR is for

A quarterly business review is a scheduled meeting, usually with a client's decision-makers, to look back at the last quarter and plan the next. Done well, it's the meeting where the client sees the value they're getting and you learn what's changing on their side. Done badly, it's an hour of usage charts nobody asked for.

Internal QBRs, where a team presents to leadership, follow the same logic: goals, results, learnings, plan. The difference is the audience: leadership wants decisions and risks, clients want proof of value and a say in what's next.

## Before the meeting

1. **Re-read last quarter's goals.** The whole meeting hangs on them. If there weren't any agreed goals, the first job of this QBR is to set some.
2. **Send a draft agenda a week ahead** and ask: "Is there anything you'd like to add?" This turns it into their meeting too.
3. **Ask who's coming.** If an executive joins for the first time, you'll need a one-minute context at the start.
4. **Talk to your main contact beforehand.** Ask what their boss cares about this quarter, and whether there's anything they don't want raised in front of others.
5. **Know your bad news** and decide how you'll say it. Never let the client discover it on a slide.

## An agenda that keeps it a dialogue

| Item | Time (60 minutes) | Who talks most |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Welcome and agenda check | 3 min | You |
| Their priorities: "What's changed for you since last quarter?" | 10 min | Client |
| Results against last quarter's goals | 12 min | You, with questions |
| What we learned, what changed | 8 min | Both |
| Proposed goals for next quarter | 12 min | Both |
| Open questions and feedback | 10 min | Client |
| Next steps, owners and dates | 5 min | You |

Asking about their priorities before you present results is the single best change most QBRs need. It tells you which results to emphasise, and it shows you care about their business, not just your product.

## Presenting results against goals

Keep it to the goals you agreed, in the client's terms. A simple scorecard works:

| Goal (agreed last quarter) | Target | Result | Status | Comment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cut average ticket response time | Under 4 hours | 3.2 hours | Met | New routing rules from week 3 |
| Onboard the Leeds office | All 60 users by end of quarter | 45 users | Partly met | 15 delayed by their IT migration |
| Reduce manual reporting | Save 5 hours a week | Not measured | Unclear | We need a baseline; proposal below |

(Made-up example.) Say the headline before the table: "Two of three goals met, one partly, and one we couldn't measure, which I want to fix."

### Saying a missed goal out loud

> "We missed the Leeds onboarding target: 45 of 60 users. Fifteen were delayed by the IT migration on your side, but we also didn't flag the risk early enough. Next quarter, I'd suggest a fortnightly check-in with your IT lead so that doesn't happen again. Does that work for you?"

Name it, explain it honestly without blaming, propose a fix, and ask for agreement.

## Questions that keep the client talking

Use open questions, then listen. For example:

- "What are your top priorities for next quarter?"
- "What would make next quarter a success for you, and for your boss?"
- "Where did we make your life harder this quarter?"
- "If you could change one thing about how we work together, what would it be?"
- "Who else in your organisation should we be talking to?"

When they answer, follow up once before moving on: "Tell me more about that." The [open-ended questions](/speaking-coach/glossary/open-ended-question/) entry has more examples.

## Agreeing next quarter's plan

Propose two to four goals, each with a number, a date and an owner on both sides. Then ask for changes. End with a recap you say out loud:

> "So, three goals for next quarter: Leeds fully onboarded by end of January, response time held under four hours, and a baseline for manual reporting by mid-quarter so we can measure it. I'll send this in writing today."

Send the written summary the same day.

## Delivery: sounding like a partner, not a vendor

- **Talk about their business more than your product.**
- **Don't read the slides.** Say the headline and let the table be the detail.
- **Pause after every question** you ask. Silence is where clients think.
- **If an executive is there, give them the bottom line in the first two minutes**; they may leave early.

Rehearse the hard part out loud: the missed goal and the client's pushback. Speaking Coach lets you describe that situation and have the partner play a frustrated client, then shows you how you sounded and a line to try instead.

## Questions people ask

### What should be included in a QBR?

The client's current priorities, results against last quarter's goals, what you learned, proposed goals for next quarter, and agreed next steps with owners.

### How long should a QBR be?

Often 45–90 minutes. One hour is a common default; shorten it if the account is small or things are going smoothly.

### How many slides for a QBR?

Keep it lean: an agenda, a scorecard, a page of learnings and a page of proposed goals, plus an appendix for detail.

### How do I present bad results in a QBR?

Say them early and plainly, explain without blaming, propose a specific fix, and ask the client if they agree with it.

### What's the difference between a QBR and a status meeting?

A status meeting tracks tasks. A QBR steps back to look at business outcomes and agree goals for the next quarter, usually with more senior people in the room.
