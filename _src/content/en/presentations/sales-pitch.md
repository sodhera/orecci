## Why most pitches sound like pitches

A pitch sounds scripted when it would be the same no matter who was listening. The prospect can hear it: the features come in the same order, the examples are about someone else, and the questions at the end are "Does that make sense?"

The fix is order. Ask first, then talk. When you pitch after hearing the prospect's problem, your pitch can't be generic, because it's built from what they just told you.

## A meeting plan for 30 minutes

| Stage | Time | What you're doing | You're talking |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Open | 2–3 min | Brief small talk, agree the agenda and time | Half |
| Discovery | 10 min | Questions about their situation, problem, and what it costs | A quarter or less |
| Pitch | 8–10 min | Show how you solve the problem they described | Most |
| Objections and questions | 5 min | Explore concerns | Half |
| Next step | 2 min | Agree what happens next, with a date | Half |

Example agenda line at the start:

> "We've got 30 minutes. I'd like to spend the first ten understanding how you handle scheduling today, then show you the parts of our tool that are relevant, and leave time at the end to decide if a next step makes sense. Does that work?"

That last question matters. It gives the prospect a say from the first minute.

## Discovery questions that open people up

Use open questions that ask for stories and numbers, not yes or no. Some examples:

- "Walk me through what happens today when a driver calls in sick."
- "What does that cost you, roughly, in a bad week?"
- "What have you tried already? What happened?"
- "If this were fixed, what would you do with the time?"
- "Who else is affected when this goes wrong?"
- "What would make you choose to change this now, rather than next year?"

Then listen, and write down their exact words. If they say "it's chaos every Monday", you'll use "Monday chaos" in the pitch, not "scheduling inefficiencies".

For more on question types, see [open-ended questions](/speaking-coach/glossary/open-ended-question/).

## Pitch in their words

When you move to the pitch, summarise what you heard before you show anything:

> "So if I've got this right: Mondays are the problem, because sick calls come in by phone and your dispatcher rebuilds the routes by hand, which takes most of the morning. And it's got worse since you added the second depot. Is that fair?"

Then connect each part of the product to one thing they said:

| They said | Weak pitch line | Strong pitch line |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Monday mornings are chaos" | "Our AI-powered routing engine optimises in real time." | "When a driver calls in sick, the routes rebuild in about a minute, so your dispatcher's Monday looks like a Tuesday." |
| "We tried a tool before and nobody used it" | "We have best-in-class adoption." | "Drivers only use the app to tap 'can't work'. Everything else stays with your dispatcher." |
| "The second depot made it worse" | "We support multi-site operations." | "Both depots share one driver pool, so a sick call at one can be covered from the other." |

Show three relevant things, not twelve. If they ask about something else, you can show it then.

## Check in, don't present at them

Every few minutes, stop and ask a short question:

- "Is that how it would work for you?"
- "Which of those matters most?"
- "What would your dispatcher say if she saw this?"

These keep the prospect talking and tell you what's landing. Avoid "Does that make sense?", which invites a polite yes and nothing else.

## Handling objections

Objections usually mean the prospect is taking you seriously. Don't rush to answer. Use four steps:

1. **Acknowledge.** "That's fair, it's a real cost."
2. **Ask.** "When you say it's expensive, is that compared with what you pay now, or with the budget you have this year?"
3. **Answer the real concern**, briefly.
4. **Check.** "Does that address it, or is there something else behind it?"

Common objections and a calm reply:

| Objection | What it often means | A reply to try |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "It's too expensive." | Value isn't clear yet, or budget timing | "Compared with what? Let's look at what the Monday problem costs you now." |
| "We're happy with what we have." | Change feels risky | "What would have to go wrong for you to look again?" |
| "Send me some information." | Polite exit | "Happy to. What would you need to see in it to take it further?" |
| "Not right now." | Timing or priority | "What's happening between now and then that makes later better?" |
| "I need to talk to my boss." | They're not the only decision-maker | "Of course. What will they ask you? Should we include them in the next call?" |

## Close with a next step, not a hope

End by proposing something specific: "Would it make sense to run a two-week trial at the first depot? I can set it up Thursday." A pitch that ends with "Let me know what you think" usually ends there.

## Questions people ask

### How long should a sales pitch be?

The pitch itself is often 5–10 minutes inside a 30–60 minute meeting. The rest is discovery and conversation.

### How do I make my sales pitch sound less scripted?

Ask questions first, use the prospect's own words, and show only what's relevant to them. Know your points, not your sentences.

### What's the best way to open a sales call?

A brief greeting, then agree the agenda and time, and ask if it works for them. See [small talk before a sales call](/speaking-coach/small-talk/sales-call-small-talk/) for how much chat to do first.

### How do I handle "it's too expensive"?

Acknowledge it, then ask what they're comparing against. Price objections are often about value or budget timing, and you can't answer until you know which.

### How do I practice a sales pitch?

Run it out loud with someone playing a skeptical prospect, including objections. Interview-style drills like [sell me this pen](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/sell-me-this-pen/) are also good practice in asking before pitching.
