## Why people leave webinars

In a room, leaving is awkward. On a webinar, it's one click, and another tab is always open. People leave when nothing changes on screen for a long time, when the first ten minutes are housekeeping and biographies, or when they can't tell whether the useful part is coming. Each of those has a fix.

## Plan a run of show

Write a minute-by-minute plan and share it with everyone involved. A made-up example for a 45-minute webinar:

| Time | What happens | Who | Change for the viewer |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0:00 | Welcome, one housekeeping line, the promise | Host | Host on camera |
| 0:02 | Poll: "What's your biggest payroll headache?" | Host | Poll |
| 0:04 | Section 1: what's changing and when | Presenter | Slides |
| 0:12 | Chat prompt: "Type your business size in the chat" | Presenter | Chat |
| 0:13 | Section 2: worked example with real numbers | Presenter | Screen share |
| 0:22 | Short story from a customer's situation (anonymised) | Presenter | Camera only |
| 0:25 | Section 3: what to do this month, step by step | Presenter | Slides |
| 0:33 | Q&A from chat | Host and presenter | Two faces on screen |
| 0:43 | Recap, one action, where to get the slides | Host | Host on camera |
| 0:45 | End on time | | |

The last column is the important one: something changes at least every five to seven minutes.

## The first two minutes

Skip the long introduction and agenda. Say what they'll get, when, and what to do.

> "Thanks for joining. In the next 45 minutes you'll learn the three payroll changes coming in January and exactly what to do about each one this month. Questions go in the Q&A box, and we'll answer them at the 33-minute mark. The recording and slides come by email tomorrow. Let's start with a quick poll."

That tells late joiners and doubters exactly why to stay.

## Talking to a camera

- **Look at the lens when you make a point**, not at the faces on screen. Put a small sticker next to the lens as a reminder. More in [eye contact on video calls](/speaking-coach/guides/eye-contact-on-video-calls/).
- **Raise the camera to eye level.** A stack of books under a laptop works.
- **Light from the front**, such as a window or a lamp behind the camera, not behind you.
- **Frame yourself from mid-chest up**, with a little space above your head.
- **Use an external microphone or headset** if you can. Viewers forgive poor video more readily than poor audio.
- **Close everything else** and turn off notifications before you share your screen.

## Energy without an audience

Talking to a lens with no reactions drains energy, and what feels normal to you looks flat on screen. Things that help:

1. **Stand up if you can.** Your voice has more range and energy.
2. **Picture one person**, a real customer or colleague, and talk to them.
3. **Exaggerate slightly:** a bit more vocal variety, a bit bigger smile, slightly slower. See [how to stop sounding monotone](/speaking-coach/guides/how-to-stop-sounding-monotone/).
4. **Read the chat out loud.** "Maria says she has 12 staff. Good, this example is close to yours." It turns silence into a conversation.
5. **Use a co-host.** Two voices are easier to listen to than one, and the switch itself resets attention.

## Chat and Q&A

| Role | Job during the webinar |
| --- | --- |
| Presenter | Delivers content; reads chat only at planned points |
| Moderator or host | Watches chat, answers quick questions in writing, groups similar questions, picks the best for live Q&A |
| Producer (for bigger events) | Runs the platform, polls, recordings and tech problems |

If you're presenting alone, tell people when you'll check the chat, and stick to it. Trying to read chat while you speak breaks your flow.

During Q&A:

- **Read the question aloud** before answering, so the recording makes sense.
- **Answer in under a minute.** Long answers stall the whole session.
- **Group similar questions:** "A few of you asked about part-time staff…"
- **If you run out of time**, say how unanswered questions will be handled, for example in the follow-up email, and do it.
- **Have two or three seeded questions ready** in case the chat is quiet at first.

## When something breaks

- **Your audio or video drops:** the host should have a holding line ready, and you rejoin. Agree this in advance.
- **Screen share fails:** have the slides as a PDF the host can share instead.
- **A slide is wrong:** correct it out loud and move on.

Do a full tech rehearsal on the actual platform at least a day before, with everyone who'll speak.

## Rehearse the talking, not just the tech

Run your opening and each section out loud to the camera, timed. Watch one recording back. ## Questions people ask

### How long should a webinar be?

Many run 30–60 minutes including Q&A. Shorter is easier to keep people for; plan your content for the time, not the other way round.

### How do I keep people engaged in a webinar?

Change something every five to seven minutes: a poll, a chat prompt, a demo, a new speaker or a story. And tell people early when the useful part comes.

### Should I show my face during a webinar?

Usually yes, at least for the opening, transitions, stories and Q&A. A face on screen holds attention better than slides alone.

### How do I handle a quiet chat?

Ask an easy question that anyone can answer, like where they're watching from or their role. Have a few seeded questions ready for Q&A.

### What if nobody asks questions?

Answer one or two questions you've prepared: "A question we often get is…" It usually gets the chat moving.
