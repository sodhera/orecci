## Meaning

It is an outline for a speech that asks people to do something, built around the order in which listeners tend to be persuaded: first they notice, then they care, then they see a fix, then they imagine it working, then they act. Some textbooks word the steps slightly differently, for example "gain attention, present the need, satisfy the need, visualization, call to action", but the order is the same.

## The five steps, with examples

Example topic: a student asking the school to install water refill stations. All details are made up.

| Step | What you do | Example line |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Attention** | Grab interest with a [hook](/speaking-coach/glossary/speech-hook/) | "This term, the bin outside our canteen collected 3,112 plastic bottles. I counted." |
| **2. Need** | Show the problem and why it matters to them | "We pay for every one, the vending machine runs out by lunch, and the fountains don't fill a bottle." |
| **3. Satisfaction** | Present your solution and how it works | "Two refill stations, one by the gym and one in the canteen, paid from this year's student fund." |
| **4. Visualization** | Help them picture the result, or the cost of doing nothing | "Imagine next summer: no queue at the machine, cold water after practice, and a bin that's half empty." |
| **5. Action** | Ask for a specific step | "Vote yes on Friday's student fund proposal." |

## A second example: a proposal at work

Made-up example: a two-minute pitch to your team about meetings.

> **Attention:** "How many of you had a meeting this week that could have been a message?"
>
> **Need:** "We each spend about five hours a week in status meetings, and most of what we hear there is already in the tracker."
>
> **Satisfaction:** "For one month, let's replace the Monday and Wednesday stand-ups with a written update by 10 a.m., and keep Friday's meeting for decisions only."
>
> **Visualization:** "Picture Monday morning with a free hour before your first call. And if it doesn't work, we go back to normal in four weeks, with nothing lost."
>
> **Action:** "If you're in, reply 'yes' in the team channel by Thursday and I'll set it up."

The visualization step shows both sides: the gain if they act, and that the risk of trying is small. That is often what makes the [call to action](/speaking-coach/glossary/call-to-action/) feel easy to say yes to.

## Where it's used

Wikipedia's article describes the sequence as widely used in public speaking, marketing, advertising and communication. The action step changes with the setting:

| Setting | What the action step asks for |
| --- | --- |
| Class persuasive speech | Vote, sign or change one habit |
| Sales pitch | Book a demo or start a trial |
| Fundraising appeal | Donate today |
| Proposal to a manager | Approve a small trial |

## Rough timing for a 5-minute speech

| Step | Time |
| --- | --- |
| Attention | 30 seconds |
| Need | 1 minute 15 seconds |
| Satisfaction | 1 minute 30 seconds |
| Visualization | 1 minute |
| Action | 30 seconds |

## Monroe's motivated sequence vs. other structures

| Structure | Steps | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monroe's motivated sequence | Attention, need, satisfaction, visualization, action | Getting an audience to act |
| Problem-solution | Problem, solution, sometimes action | Explaining a fix to people who already care |
| [PREP](/speaking-coach/glossary/prep-method/) | Point, reason, example, point | A short opinion in a meeting or interview |

## How to write one

1. Write the action first: one specific thing you want the audience to do.
2. Find the need this audience feels, not the one you feel.
3. Make the solution concrete: who, what, when and what it costs.
4. Write the visualization in plain, specific detail, with the result if they act and if they don't.
5. Choose an opening that leads straight into the need.
6. Say it out loud and time it. For a full outline and delivery tips, see [how to write a persuasive speech](/speaking-coach/speeches/persuasive-speech/).

## Questions people ask

### What are the 5 steps of Monroe's motivated sequence?

Attention, Need, Satisfaction, Visualization and Action: get attention, show the problem, offer a solution, help the audience picture the result, and ask them to act.

### Who created Monroe's motivated sequence?

Alan H. Monroe, a communication professor at Purdue University, in the mid-1930s.

### What is the visualization step?

Describing what the future looks like if the audience acts, or what happens if they don't, in concrete detail.

### Can I use it outside speeches?

Yes. It fits sales pitches, fundraising appeals and proposals to a manager.

### Is it the only persuasive structure?

No. Problem-solution is simpler, and [ethos, pathos and logos](/speaking-coach/glossary/ethos-pathos-logos/) describe the kinds of appeal you make within any structure. Monroe's adds the visualization and action steps.
