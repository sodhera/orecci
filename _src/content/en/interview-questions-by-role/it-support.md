## Why help desk interviews are partly phone calls

A lot of help desk work happens on the phone or in chat with someone who is annoyed and can't describe the problem. So interviewers often run a role play: "I'm the user, my email isn't working, go." They're watching for a calm voice, good questions, and a logical order. Getting the fix right matters less than how you get there.

If you're new to IT, this is where you can stand out. Customer service experience counts a lot here.

## Troubleshooting out loud

When asked "Walk me through how you'd fix this," say each step and why. A general pattern:

1. **Calm and gather:** what exactly happens, when it started, what changed, does it affect anyone else?
2. **Check the simple things first:** cable, power, Wi-Fi switch, is the right account signed in?
3. **Narrow it down:** is it one device, one user, one app or everyone? Can you reproduce it?
4. **Try the most likely fix,** then confirm with the user that it worked.
5. **Escalate** if needed, with clear notes.
6. **Document** what you found and did.

> Example answer for "my laptop won't connect to Wi-Fi": "First I'd ask if anyone near them has the same problem, because if the whole floor is down it's a network issue and I'd escalate straight away. If it's just them, I'd check whether Wi-Fi is turned on and whether they can see our network. Then I'd forget the network and reconnect, and check if their password changed recently, because that often breaks saved Wi-Fi. If it's still failing, I'd check the adapter driver or try a cable, and note everything in the ticket."

Saying "is it just you, or everyone?" early is a strong signal. It's the fastest way to know whether this is one ticket or an outage.

## The frustrated user

Help desk interviewers often play an angry caller. The trick is to calm them before you troubleshoot.

| What not to say | What to say instead |
| --- | --- |
| "Did you try turning it off and on?" | "Let's try a quick restart together. It clears a lot of problems." |
| "That's not my department." | "I'll get the right team on this and stay on until they pick it up." |
| "You need to update your DNS cache." | "I'm going to reset one setting. It takes about ten seconds." |
| "That's user error." | "That one catches a lot of people. Here's a faster way next time." |

> Example opening line: "I'm sorry, I know you've got a meeting in ten minutes. Let's get you printing. Can you tell me what you see on the screen when you click print?"

Then give them a time: "If this doesn't work in five minutes, I'll send you to the printer on the third floor so you're not late." People cope better when they know what's next. For more lines, see [how to handle an angry customer](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/how-would-you-handle-an-angry-customer/).

## Ticket priority

"How do you prioritize tickets?" Use impact and urgency, not who shouts loudest.

> Example answer: "I look at how many people are affected and whether work has stopped. The whole finance team unable to log in comes before one person's slow laptop. If a senior executive calls, I treat their issue with the same rules, but I'm realistic that some companies set VIP priority, and I'd follow that policy. Either way, I tell people where they are in the queue and when to expect a response."

Mention your ticketing habits: clear notes, updating the user, and not leaving a ticket silent.

## Explaining tech simply

"Explain a VPN to someone non-technical." Use one comparison, one reason it matters to them, and stop.

> Example answer: "A VPN is like a private tunnel from your laptop to the office. When you're at a café, everything goes through the tunnel, so nobody on the café's Wi-Fi can see what you're sending, and you can reach the office files as if you were at your desk."

The guide to [explaining complex ideas simply](/speaking-coach/guides/how-to-explain-things-simply/) has more techniques.

## Security questions

Expect at least one, such as a phone password reset. Say you'd always verify identity with the company's process, like a callback to the number on file, and never reset a password just because the caller sounds urgent or senior. Urgency is a classic social engineering trick.

## How to practice

Practice one troubleshooting walkthrough out loud and time it: about a minute and a half. Then practice an angry caller with someone who doesn't know tech terms. Speaking Coach's built-in "Handle a rude customer" scene gives you a caller who reacts to how you speak.

## Questions people ask

### Do I need certifications for a help desk job?

Not always. Some employers ask for an entry-level certification, while others value customer service experience and a home lab. Read the job post and mention what you have.

### What if I don't know the answer to a technical question?

Say how you'd find out: check the knowledge base, search the error message, or escalate with good notes. Don't guess.

### How technical are help desk interviews?

Usually basic to moderate: networking basics, passwords and accounts, printers, email, and common operating system problems. The soft skills carry as much weight.

### How do I show I'm good with people?

Tell one short story about calming someone down, from IT or any customer-facing job, and what you said.
