## What the interviewer is listening for

PT interviews are often run by a clinic director or senior therapist, so expect clinical follow-ups. They want to hear how you think, not a textbook. Three things matter most: your reasoning is clear, you adjust when something isn't working, and you can treat well inside a real schedule.

Keep patients anonymous. Use age range, condition and setting, never names or identifying details.

## The case walk-through

This is the question that decides most PT interviews. Use a simple order and say *why* at each step.

1. **Who and why:** age range, diagnosis or complaint, and the patient's own goal.
2. **What you found:** the key findings from your evaluation, not every measure.
3. **Your plan and reasoning:** what you chose to treat first and why.
4. **What changed:** progress, a setback, and what you adjusted.
5. **Outcome and discharge:** where they ended, and how you knew it was time.

Example answer for an outpatient therapist:

> "A man in his fifties came in six weeks after a rotator cuff repair. His goal was to lift his grandson again. Range of motion was limited and he was guarding badly. I followed the surgeon's protocol, but spent the first sessions on pain education because fear was limiting him as much as the shoulder. At week ten strength stalled, so I added functional lifting with a weighted bag the size of a toddler. He was discharged at sixteen weeks, lifting his grandson, with a home program he could keep up."

Aim for about two minutes. If they want more detail, they'll ask.

## "A patient stops progressing. What do you do?"

They want a process, not "push harder". Show that you reassess before you change anything.

| Step | What to say |
| --- | --- |
| Reassess | "I'd repeat the key measures to confirm it's a real plateau." |
| Check adherence | "Are they doing the home program? Has anything changed at home or work?" |
| Review the plan | "Is the load or the type of exercise still right for where they are?" |
| Look for red flags | "Is there anything that needs the physician to see them?" |
| Talk about goals | "Sometimes the honest answer is that we're near their ceiling, and we plan discharge." |

## Home exercise adherence

The weak answer is "I give them a handout." Strong answers show you make the program fit the person's life.

> "I keep home programs to three exercises at most and tie each to something they already do, like heel raises while the kettle boils. I ask them to show me the exercises before they leave, and at the next visit I ask what got in the way, not just whether they did them. If someone hasn't done any, I'd rather cut the program to one exercise they'll actually do."

## Productivity targets

Be honest. Interviewers know targets are a pressure point. They want someone who meets them without cutting corners on care or documentation.

> "I've worked with a target of around 85% productivity. What helped most was finishing notes at the point of care and grouping similar patients in my schedule. When a target meant I couldn't safely see a patient, I raised it with my director with specific cases, not as a general complaint."

Use your own real numbers. Don't quote a percentage you didn't work to.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Reciting every test and measure in your case instead of the ones that changed your plan.
- Talking about patients in a way that could identify them.
- Calling patients "non-compliant" without saying what you did about it.
- Complaining about productivity without showing how you managed it.

Saying a case out loud is harder than writing it up. Try it once with the [interview answer review](/speaking-coach/tools/interview-answer-review/) to see your pace and where you pause. Speaking Coach's interviewer can also ask the "why did you choose that?" follow-ups a clinic director would.

## Questions people ask

### What do new grad PT interviews focus on?

Your clinical rotations, what you learned from your clinical instructors, and how you'd handle a full caseload. Pick one case from a rotation and know it well.

### How technical do the questions get?

It depends on who interviews you. A senior therapist may ask about specific techniques or exercise choices for a condition. Answer with your reasoning, and say plainly if something is outside what you've done.

### Should I ask about productivity expectations?

Yes. Ask what the target is, how it's measured and how much documentation time is built into the day. It shows you've thought about the real job.

### How do I answer "why this setting"?

Name what draws you to the patients and pace of that setting, with one experience that shows it, such as a rotation in home health where you liked seeing patients in their own environment.
