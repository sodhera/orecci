## The core of every team leader question

A team leader usually has responsibility without much power. You can't hire, fire or set pay, but you're expected to hit the team's numbers. So the hidden question in almost every answer is: **how do you get people to do things when you can't make them?**

Your answers should show three habits: you lead by example, you're fair about who does what, and you deal with problems early instead of waiting for the manager.

## "How do you motivate people without formal authority?"

Skip "I lead by example" on its own. Say what you actually did, and what people did differently afterwards.

Example answer for a call centre or operations team:

> "On my last team, our after-call work time was creeping up and nobody saw why it mattered. I started sharing the queue numbers in our morning huddle, so people could see customers waiting while notes were being written. I also took the hardest calls myself during the busiest hour. Within a month our wrap time was back to target. People changed because they could see the queue, not because I told them to."

## Workload balance

Interviewers want a method, not a feeling. Show that you look at the work, talk to the people, and adjust.

1. **Make the work visible.** A shared board or list of who has what.
2. **Check capacity weekly,** not just when someone complains.
3. **Match work to skill and growth.** Give stretch tasks to people who want them, not only to your fastest person.
4. **Address the coaster privately** with what you've noticed and what you need.

> "One colleague was handling every escalation because she was quickest. She was burning out and two newer people weren't learning. I split escalations by day, sat with the newer two for their first few, and kept her as the backup. It was slower for two weeks, then the team had three people who could handle escalations instead of one."

## "Two teammates are in conflict. What do you do?"

The answer they want has an order: listen to each person separately, bring them together around the work, agree something specific, and follow up. Escalate only if it affects customers or safety, or keeps happening.

| Step | What you say or do |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Talk to each person alone | "I've noticed things are tense with Sam. What's going on from your side?" |
| 2. Find the work problem underneath | Often a handover, a shared task or credit for work |
| 3. Meet together, focused on the fix | "We need handovers that work for both of you. What would that look like?" |
| 4. Agree one specific change | A written handover note at 3pm, for example |
| 5. Check in a week later | Briefly, with each of them |

Don't say you'd "tell them to be professional." It ends the conversation without fixing anything.

## "What would you do in your first 30 days?"

Show that you'd learn before changing things, then make one visible improvement.

| When | Focus |
| --- | --- |
| Week 1 | One-to-ones with each person: what's working, what's annoying, what they want to learn |
| Week 2 | Learn the numbers and the process; do the work alongside the team |
| Week 3 | Agree with your manager on the one or two things to improve first |
| Week 4 | Make one small change the team asked for, and say why |

For a longer version of this answer, see [what would you do in your first 90 days](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/what-would-you-do-in-your-first-90-days/).

## Mistakes to avoid

- **"I'm a natural leader."** Let your examples say it.
- **Every answer is about you doing the work.** Show you got others to do it too.
- **Escalating everything.** It suggests you can't handle the role.
- **Escalating nothing.** It suggests you'd hide problems from your manager.

The conflict scenario is the one candidates ramble on most, because it has many moving parts. Practice it out loud with the steps above and keep it near 90 seconds. Speaking Coach's interviewer follows up on what you said, so you can test whether your answer holds up when they ask "and if that didn't work?"

## Questions people ask

### What's the difference between a team leader and a supervisor interview?

Team leader interviews focus more on leading peers while doing the same work. Supervisor interviews add more on discipline, policies and scheduling. The [supervisor questions](/speaking-coach/interview-questions-by-role/supervisor/) cover those.

### What if I'm applying to lead my current team?

Expect questions about managing friends and former peers. Say how you'd reset the relationship early, and that you'd hold everyone, including friends, to the same standard.

### Should I describe my leadership style?

Only with an example. A style label without a story sounds rehearsed.

### What if I've never led a team?

Use times you trained a new starter, ran a project, organised a rota or covered for the leader. Those are leadership moments even without the title.
