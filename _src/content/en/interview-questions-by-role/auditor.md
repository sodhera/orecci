## What the interviewer is listening for

An audit manager or head of internal audit wants three things from you: you follow evidence, not assumptions; you can deliver bad news to a senior person without losing the relationship; and you keep your judgement independent. External audit interviews lean more on busy season and teamwork. Internal audit interviews lean more on business understanding and influencing managers.

## A finding management disputed

This is the question that separates good auditors. Show that you went back to the evidence, listened to their view, and stayed firm where it mattered.

Example answer (made-up details):

> "In a purchasing review I found 7 of 40 purchase orders over the approval limit had no second signature. The procurement manager said approvals happened by email, so the control was working. I asked for the emails and got them for 4 of the 7. I updated the finding to 3 exceptions and changed the wording from 'control not operating' to 'approval evidence not retained in the system'. He accepted that version, and the fix was a simple system change rather than a disciplinary issue."

Why it works: you reopened the evidence, you changed the finding where the facts changed, and you kept the part the evidence still supported.

| Weak response to pushback | Stronger response |
| --- | --- |
| "The audit standard says so." | "Here's what I tested and what I found. Is there evidence I haven't seen?" |
| Dropping the finding to keep the peace | Adjusting the wording only if new evidence supports it |
| Escalating straight away | Talking to the process owner first, then your manager if it isn't resolved |

## Sampling

Interviewers usually want your reasoning, not a formula. Say what drives the size and how you pick items.

> "Sample size depends on how often the control runs and how much risk sits behind it. For a daily control I'd test more items than for a quarterly one, and I follow our methodology's table. I pick items randomly or systematically across the whole period, and I add specific high-value or unusual items on top, labelled separately, so the random sample stays clean."

Then cover exceptions: you'd find out whether it's a one-off or a pattern, extend the sample if your methodology calls for it, and report it either way.

## Independence

Answer with practical behaviour, not a definition.

- You'd declare a personal or financial link to a client before starting work.
- You wouldn't take on management decisions for an area you audit, even if asked to help.
- You'd keep friendly with client staff without letting them shape your conclusions.

Example answer (made-up details):

> "On my first engagement, the client's controller offered to prepare the reconciliation we were meant to test 'to save time'. I thanked him, said we needed to get it from the system ourselves, and told my senior the same day so she knew why that test took longer."

If asked about an ethical conflict, the [ethical dilemma](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/tell-me-about-a-time-you-faced-an-ethical-dilemma/) page has a structure that works.

## Busy season

For external audit, everyone asks. Show planning and honesty, not just stamina.

> "I keep a list of every open request by client and date. When a client is slow, I chase on day two, not day five, and I tell my senior early if it puts the deadline at risk. Last busy season I had three engagements overlap in February. I agreed priorities with both seniors on a short call each Monday, and that stopped me getting pulled in two directions."

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Treating management as the enemy.** Auditors who are combative get less information.
- **Defining independence from a textbook** without an example of what you'd actually do.
- **Saying you'd "just extend the sample"** without saying why or what you'd tell your manager.
- **Pretending busy season doesn't bother you.** Show how you manage it.

Delivering a finding to someone who disagrees is a conversation, and it's worth rehearsing out loud. Speaking Coach can play the manager who pushes back so you can practise staying calm and specific.

## Questions people ask

### What's the difference between internal and external auditor interviews?

External audit interviews focus on accounting knowledge, teamwork and busy season. Internal audit interviews focus more on risk, how the business works and how you'd influence managers to fix control gaps.

### What if I've never had a finding disputed?

Use a time someone disagreed with your work or conclusion in another setting, such as a reviewer or a team member, and show the same steps: evidence, listening, adjusting only where the facts changed.

### How technical are audit interviews?

Expect questions on controls, testing, risk and accounting basics, and sometimes a short case. Graduate roles focus more on motivation and teamwork than technical depth.

### How do I answer "Why audit?" honestly?

Name what you actually like: seeing how many businesses work, the structure of testing, or the training path. Then connect it to this firm or team specifically.
