## What hiring managers are really asking

Scrum master interviews rarely test whether you can recite the framework. They test how you behave when it isn't being followed. Hiring managers have seen scrum masters who act as meeting schedulers and scrum masters who act as rule enforcers. They want neither. The 2020 Scrum Guide calls scrum masters "true leaders who serve" the team and the organization, which is what the coaching vs controlling question is getting at.

Your best evidence is stories where the team changed something because you helped them see it, not because you told them to.

## A team that ignores the process

"The team skips retros and the stand-up runs 30 minutes." A weak answer enforces the rules. A strong one finds out why first.

> Example answer: "I'd ask, in a one-to-one or a short session, what the stand-up is for them right now. Usually a 30-minute stand-up means people are solving problems in it, often because they have no other time to talk. On one team I suggested we keep the stand-up to 15 minutes and stay behind afterwards with only the people who needed to. For the skipped retros, I asked why, and the answer was 'nothing changes.' So in the next retro we picked one action only, gave it an owner, and checked it first thing the following retro. Attendance came back once people saw changes happen."

Notice the pattern: ask, find the real problem, run a small experiment, check the result.

## Removing blockers

"How do you handle a blocker outside the team's control?" Show that you take it on, escalate with clear facts, and also help the team unblock themselves next time.

| Blocker | What you'd do |
| --- | --- |
| Waiting on another team | Talk to their lead directly, agree a date, make the dependency visible on both boards |
| Missing access or tools | Escalate with impact: "Three developers idle until this is fixed" |
| Unclear requirements | Bring the product owner and developer together the same day |
| A recurring blocker | Raise the pattern in the retro and with management, not just the instance |

> Example line: "I don't want to be the only person who can unblock things. After I've fixed one, I'll ask the team who they'd contact next time, so they can go directly."

## Stakeholder pressure mid-sprint

"A senior stakeholder wants new work added mid-sprint." This is a favorite, because it tests spine and diplomacy at once. Know the guide's position: scope can be clarified and renegotiated with the product owner as more is learned, but no changes are made that would endanger the sprint goal, and only the product owner can cancel a sprint.

> Example answer: "I'd say: 'I understand it's urgent. The product owner decides what the team works on, so let's take this to her now.' Then I'd help them have that conversation with the trade-off visible: what's in the sprint, the goal, and what would be dropped. If the request truly matters more than the goal, the product owner can swap work in a way that keeps the goal, or cancel the sprint. What I won't do is let it bypass her and land on a developer's desk directly."

The key is redirecting to the product owner without sounding like a gatekeeper. The skills overlap with [tell me about a time you persuaded someone](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/tell-me-about-a-time-you-persuaded-someone/).

## Coaching vs controlling

"What's the difference between coaching and managing?" Give a short definition, then an example.

> Example answer: "Controlling is telling the team how to work. Coaching is asking questions so they find a better way and own it. For example, when a team's estimates kept being wrong, I didn't impose a new method. I asked them to compare three finished stories with their estimates and say what surprised them. They noticed every big miss involved the same external system and decided to split those stories earlier. That stuck, because it was their idea."

Also mention when you'd step in more directly: when the team is new to Scrum, or when there's a conflict they can't resolve. Being clear about that flexibility is often what senior interviewers want to hear. The general version is [what is your leadership style](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/what-is-your-leadership-style/).

## Metrics

If asked about metrics, mention ones that help the team learn, like cycle time, how often sprint goals are met, and escaped defects. Be careful with velocity: say it's a planning tool for the team, not a performance score to compare teams.

## How to practice

Say the mid-sprint answer out loud to someone playing a pushy stakeholder who won't take "talk to the product owner" for an answer. Speaking Coach's Tough pressure level gives you a partner who pushes back and follows up on what you said.

## Questions people ask

### Do I need a certification to be a scrum master?

Many job posts ask for one, but interviewers usually care more about real team experience. Mention the certification briefly, then give examples.

### What if I've only been a scrum master on one team?

That's fine. Use different situations from that team: a hard retro, a blocker, a stakeholder conflict. Depth beats variety.

### How do I answer "Is a scrum master a project manager?"

Say no, and explain the difference: a project manager typically owns scope, schedule and delivery, while a scrum master helps the team and organization work effectively within Scrum.

### What's a good question to ask the interviewer?

Ask what the team's last retro changed, and who the product owner is and how available they are. Both tell you how much Scrum is really practiced there.
