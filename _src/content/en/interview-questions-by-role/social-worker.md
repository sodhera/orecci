## How social work interviews are run

Most social work interviews are panel interviews, often with a team manager, a senior practitioner and sometimes someone from HR or a person with lived experience. Many include a written exercise or a case scenario you read beforehand. The panel scores reasoning: how you weigh risk, use policy, involve others and keep the person at the center. See the [panel interview guide](/speaking-coach/interviews/panel-interview/) for answering a group.

A rule for every case example: change or remove anything that could identify a client.

## A hard case

*"Tell me about the most difficult case you've worked on."* Pick one where your judgment was tested, not just one that was sad.

Example answer (details changed):

> "I worked with a single father of two who had a history of alcohol misuse and had just relapsed. The school had raised concerns about the older child's attendance and hygiene. What made it hard was that the children were clearly attached to him and he was trying. I visited unannounced as well as planned, talked to the children on their own, and worked with the school and his recovery worker. With my manager, we agreed a safety plan with a family member who could step in. I was honest with him that if drinking put the children at risk again, we'd escalate. Over six months things stabilized. What I'd do differently is involve the grandmother earlier. She was the protective factor we nearly missed."

## Safeguarding with incomplete information

This is the core of the interview. The panel wants your thinking out loud.

A structure for the answer:

1. **Immediate safety.** Is anyone at risk right now? If so, act today.
2. **What you know and don't know.** Separate facts from concerns.
3. **Who else holds information.** School, health visitor, GP, police, other agencies.
4. **Consult.** Your manager or supervisor; don't decide high-risk cases alone.
5. **The child's or adult's voice.** What do they say and want?
6. **Record and review.** Write down the decision and reasoning, and set when you'll look again.

Example line: "If I wasn't sure, I'd lean toward the action that keeps the child safe while I find out more, and I'd write down why."

Use the terms your service and country use for thresholds and reporting duties, and only where you're sure they apply.

## Working with resistant clients

Example answer:

> "I start by assuming the resistance makes sense from their side. A mother I worked with wouldn't open the door for weeks. Her own mother had had children removed. I left notes saying what I was there for and what I wasn't, and I met her at the children's center instead of at home. I was honest about my concerns and about what would happen if things didn't change, but I also asked what she wanted for her kids. Engagement came when she saw I'd do what I said, every time."

## Boundaries and burnout

| Question | What the panel wants | Example detail |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Boundaries | Warmth without over-involvement | Not sharing personal numbers; explaining your role at the start |
| Burnout | Self-awareness and real habits | Protected time after hard visits; using supervision honestly |
| Caseload | Prioritizing by risk | Reviewing cases weekly by risk, flagging early when you're over capacity |
| Supervision | Reflection, not just task lists | Bringing a case where you were unsure and what you learned |

Example answer on burnout:

> "I notice it first as dreading my phone. When that happens, I bring it to supervision rather than pushing through. Outside work I run and keep one evening a week completely off. I'm honest with my manager if my caseload is beyond what I can do safely, because that's a safeguarding issue too."

## How to prepare

1. Choose three anonymized cases: a safeguarding decision, a resistant client, and a disagreement with another professional.
2. For each, know what you weighed, who you consulted, the outcome, and what you'd change.
3. Practice the safeguarding structure out loud on a new scenario each day for a week.
4. Rehearse with someone who asks "Why that and not something else?" Speaking Coach's interviewer follows up on what you actually said, which is close to how panels probe.

For the general version, see [tell me about a time you made a difficult decision](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/tell-me-about-a-time-you-made-a-difficult-decision/).

## Questions people ask

### How do I answer "Why social work?"

Give a real reason and connect it to the values of the job, such as rights, protection and empowerment. Avoid saying only that you want to help people.

### What if I'm newly qualified?

Use cases from your placements, with details changed. Panels expect less experience but the same quality of reasoning.

### Should I mention theory or models?

Briefly, if it shaped what you did, such as a strengths-based approach. Show how you used it, not just its name.

### How long should case answers be?

About two minutes. Long enough for your reasoning, short enough for the panel to probe.

### What if I disagree with the panel's scenario details?

Ask a clarifying question, state your assumption, and continue. That shows the professional curiosity they want.
