## Hotel or private home: what changes

The questions overlap, but the emphasis is different.

| | Hotel housekeeper | Home housekeeper |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who interviews you | Executive housekeeper or supervisor | The homeowner, or an agency first |
| Biggest concern | Pace and consistency across many rooms | Trust, discretion and fitting the household's way of doing things |
| Typical extra question | "How many rooms a shift?" | "How do you handle our things, our children, our pets?" |
| What to bring up | Checklists, teamwork with laundry and maintenance | References, how you learn a family's preferences |

In both, the interviewer wants to picture you working alone and doing it right.

## "Walk me through how you clean a room"

Saying your order out loud proves you have one. A typical hotel checkout order goes from top to bottom and from clean to dirty:

1. Knock, announce yourself, prop the door, and check the room for damage or left items.
2. Strip the bed and remove trash and used towels.
3. Bathroom: apply product first so it has time to work, while you do something else.
4. Make the bed to the hotel's standard.
5. Dust high to low, then restock amenities.
6. Finish the bathroom, then vacuum or mop working back to the door.
7. Final look from the doorway, as the guest would see it.

> Example answer: "I spray the bathroom first so the product can sit, then strip the bed and take out the trash. I make the bed, dust from top to bottom, restock, then finish the bathroom. I vacuum last, backing out the door, and I always stand in the doorway for a final look, because that's the first thing a guest sees."

## Pace: give a real number, and the trade-off

"How many rooms can you do in a shift?" Give the honest number from your last job and the type of rooms. Then show you know speed never beats standards.

> Example answer: "At my last hotel I did 14 to 15 standard checkout rooms in an eight-hour shift, fewer if they were suites. If I'm running behind, I tell my supervisor early instead of rushing the last few rooms, because a room that fails inspection takes longer to redo."

If you have no hotel experience, talk about a home or other cleaning job and say you expect to build speed in the first weeks.

## Finding a guest's valuables

This is the integrity question. There is only one right answer: you do not keep it, move it around, or decide what it is worth. You follow the lost-and-found procedure and tell your supervisor. In a home, you leave it where it is safe and tell the employer.

> Example answer: "If it's a checkout room, I'd stop, call my supervisor, and hand it in to lost and found with the room number and time, so there's a record. If the guest is still staying, I'd leave it exactly where it was and not touch it. Once I found an envelope of cash under a pillow and handed it in. The guest called that afternoon, and it was all there."

Keep it short and calm. A long story about how honest you are sounds less believable, not more. The page on [ethical dilemma questions](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/tell-me-about-a-time-you-faced-an-ethical-dilemma/) covers how to answer integrity questions in general.

## Working unsupervised

"How do you keep your standards when nobody checks?" Answer with the habits that do the checking for you.

- A personal checklist, mental or on the cart, that you follow every room.
- The doorway check at the end.
- Telling your supervisor about problems you found, like a broken lamp or a stain you couldn't remove, so nothing is hidden.

> Example answer for a home job: "In my last family's house, I worked alone four days a week. I kept a weekly list: bathrooms and kitchen every visit, windows and the fridge on Fridays. When I couldn't get something out of the sofa, I texted a photo rather than leave it. They said they never had to check my work."

## Repetition and safety

Expect "How do you stay focused doing the same thing all day?" Talk about rhythm, small targets, and taking pride in the finished room. More ideas are in [how to answer questions about repetitive work](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/how-do-you-handle-repetitive-work/).

For safety, know the basics: never mix bleach with ammonia or acid cleaners, lift with your legs, and report needles or broken glass instead of picking them up with bare hands.

## How to practice

1. Say your cleaning order out loud in under a minute.
2. Prepare one honesty story and one "I was behind" story.
3. Have someone ask a follow-up after each answer, such as "And if the supervisor isn't answering?" A partner who follows up, like Speaking Coach's voice interviewer, shows you where your answer is thin.

## Questions people ask

### Do I need experience to get a housekeeping job?

Not always. Many hotels train new housekeepers. Mention any cleaning you have done, at home, in a restaurant or for a family, and say you are reliable and on time.

### What if I'm nervous about speaking English in the interview?

Keep sentences short and focus on the steps. Clear, simple answers are better than long ones. Practice the cleaning-order answer out loud several times before the day.

### Should I ask about the room quota?

Yes, toward the end. "How many rooms is a typical shift here?" is a fair question and shows you take pace seriously.

### What do home employers ask that hotels don't?

They ask about pets, children, privacy, keys and alarm codes, and how you like to receive instructions. Answer with what you did in a previous home and offer references.
