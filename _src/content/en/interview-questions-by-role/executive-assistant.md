## What the executive is really hiring

An executive assistant interview usually ends with the executive themselves. By then, they want to know one thing: *will this person make my week easier without me having to explain everything?* That means judgment, discretion and systems, more than software skills. Expect to be asked for specific examples, and expect follow-ups like "And what did you do the next time?"

## Anticipating needs

Don't say "I'm very proactive." Show it.

Example answer:

> "I build a weekly preview every Friday: every meeting next week, what my executive needs for each, and anything I've noticed, like two back-to-back meetings across town with no travel time. Before board meetings, I have the last meeting's actions and the attendee list with notes on anyone new. On travel days, I put the car, flight and hotel details in one message, not three. The goal is that she never has to ask me where something is."

## Gatekeeping

*"A senior leader insists on a meeting with the CEO today, but the calendar is full."* This tests whether you can say no to powerful people without damaging relationships, and whether you know when not to.

Example answer:

> "First I'd find out what it's about and how urgent it really is. If it's something the CEO would want to know today, like a customer crisis, I'd interrupt or move something lower priority, and I'd tell the CEO why. If it can wait, I'd offer the earliest real slot and, if it helps, a five-minute call between meetings. I'd say it as 'here's what I can do,' not 'no.' And I'd tell my executive afterwards, so if she disagrees with my call, I learn her priorities."

The last sentence matters. Good gatekeepers adjust to their executive's judgment over time.

## Confidential information

You'll see salaries, reorganizations and private health or family matters. Interviewers ask for an example and then a scenario: *"A colleague you're friendly with asks if the layoff rumors are true."*

Example answer:

> "I'd say, kindly, that I can't talk about anything I see in my role, and that's the same answer I'd give about any rumor, true or not. I wouldn't hint, joke, or say 'I can't say' with a meaningful look. If I could tell the rumor was spreading and upsetting people, I'd let my executive know, because she may want to communicate sooner."

## The day that fell apart

Pick a real day with several things going wrong at once, and tell it in order.

Example answer:

> "My executive was flying to a client pitch when the flight was cancelled the evening before. I booked the first morning flight and a backup train, moved the pitch from 9 to 11 with the client's assistant, and rearranged three internal meetings to video. At 6am the morning flight was delayed, so we switched to the train. I sent the deck to the client in advance in case of Wi-Fi trouble. She made the pitch with ten minutes to spare. Since then I book a backup option for any high-stakes travel."

## How strong answers differ

| Topic | Generic answer | Strong answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Anticipating needs | "I'm proactive" | A routine, like a Friday preview, and what it caught |
| Gatekeeping | "I protect their time" | How you judge urgency, what you offer instead, and that you report back |
| Confidentiality | "I'm very discreet" | What you'd say word for word when asked about a rumor |
| Crisis day | "I stayed calm" | Ordered actions, a backup plan, what you changed afterwards |
| Tools | A list of software | How you use one system to track everything |

## How to prepare

1. List your executive's three biggest recurring needs in your current job and how you handle each. That's your answer to most questions.
2. Prepare two stories: a crisis day and a gatekeeping call you got right, or got wrong and learned from.
3. Practice the gatekeeping scenario out loud, with someone pushing. Saying no warmly is a speaking skill. Speaking Coach can play the insistent leader and respond to exactly what you say.
4. Prepare questions for the executive, such as how they like to receive updates and what their last assistant did that they miss.

For general prioritizing, see [how do you prioritize your work](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/how-do-you-prioritize-your-work/).

## Questions people ask

### What's the difference between this and an administrative assistant interview?

EA interviews focus on one or two executives and on judgment: making decisions for them. [Administrative assistant interviews](/speaking-coach/interview-questions-by-role/administrative-assistant/) cover more general office support.

### How do I show I can handle a demanding executive?

Describe how you learned a past executive's preferences and adjusted, with one example. Avoid complaining about anyone.

### Should I talk about personal tasks?

If the job includes them, say what you've handled and that you're comfortable with it. If you have limits, say them politely and early.

### What if I've only supported a manager, not a C-level executive?

The skills transfer. Show your systems, discretion and judgment, and say how you'd scale them to a busier calendar.

### What questions should I ask the executive?

Ask how they like to communicate during the day, what a great first month would look like, and how much they want you deciding without checking.
