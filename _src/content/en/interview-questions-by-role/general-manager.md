## What owners and regional leaders want to hear

A general manager runs a whole unit: a hotel, a dealership, a restaurant group, a branch or a business line. Interviewers want proof that you understand where the money comes from and where it leaks, and that you can lead through a layer of managers. Generic leadership language won't carry you; numbers and decisions will.

Prepare a one-line description of your current business: "A 180-room hotel, about 90 staff, five department heads reporting to me." Then know your revenue, your largest cost lines and how they moved under you.

## Owning a P&L

Expect "Walk me through your P&L" or "Which lines did you watch weekly?" Answer by naming the lines and what you did with each.

| P&L line | What you might say |
| --- | --- |
| Revenue | "I tracked it daily against last year and forecast, and met sales every Monday." |
| Labor | "My biggest controllable cost. Schedules were built to forecast demand, and I reviewed overtime weekly." |
| Cost of goods | "We moved from monthly to weekly inventory counts when food cost drifted." |
| Repairs and maintenance | "I planned it by quarter so it didn't hit one month and distort the picture." |
| Controllables overall | "Each department head owned their lines and walked me through them monthly." |

Use your own business's lines. The point is to show you know which few lines matter most and that you push ownership down to your managers.

## Turning around a location

This is the story that wins or loses the job. Structure it in four parts.

1. **What you inherited.** Specific, without trashing your predecessor.
2. **What you found.** The two or three real causes.
3. **What you changed, in order.** With your managers named by role.
4. **What happened.** Figures over a time frame.

Example answer (made-up details):

> "I took over a car dealership that had lost money for two years. Service was the bigger problem than sales: customers waited a week for appointments and many didn't come back. I hired a service manager, added an early shift so we could take cars from 7 a.m., and set a target of booking within three days. On sales, I stopped the discounting that was making the sales numbers look fine and losing money per car. In 18 months the service department grew enough to cover most of our fixed costs, and the dealership made a profit in the second year."

## Leading managers

Leading managers means coaching them to run their departments, not doing their jobs. Show that difference.

Example answer:

> "My housekeeping manager was strong with her team but always over budget. I didn't take over her schedule. We sat down weekly with her labor report for six weeks until she could tell me before I asked where the overtime came from. By the end she was setting her own targets, and I'd stopped needing to look."

Also be ready for a manager who didn't improve, and what you did.

## Your first 90 days

Show restraint first, then action. A simple plan:

- **Days 1–30: learn.** Meet every manager one to one, work shifts in each department, read the last 12 months of results and guest or customer reviews.
- **Days 31–60: decide.** Pick the two problems that cost the most and agree a plan with the owners.
- **Days 61–90: act and report.** Start the changes, set measures, and report progress plainly.

A useful line: "I won't change anything big in the first month unless it's a safety or legal issue." More on this in [first 90 days](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/what-would-you-do-in-your-first-90-days/).

## Mistakes to avoid

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| No figures | Owners think in numbers | Revenue, margin, labor percentage, roughly and honestly |
| "I" for everything in a turnaround | Sounds like you don't lead managers | Name which manager did what |
| Blaming your predecessor | Sounds petty | Describe what you found, neutrally |
| A 90-day plan full of changes | Sounds like you won't listen first | Learn, decide, then act |

Say your turnaround story out loud and time it: under two minutes, with the numbers in. Speaking Coach's AI interviewer reads the job post you add and follows up on what you actually said, like an owner asking "And what did that cost?"

## Questions people ask

### What if I've never fully owned a P&L?

Talk about the lines you did own, such as labor or departmental budget, and how you managed them. Say clearly what you haven't owned and how you'd get up to speed.

### How do I answer "How do you control labor cost?"

Explain scheduling to demand, reviewing overtime weekly, and cross-training so you flex people instead of adding hours. Add one example where you cut cost without hurting service scores.

### Should I share confidential figures from my current employer?

Use percentages and changes ("labor from 34% to 30% of revenue") rather than exact revenue, and say you're keeping specifics confidential. Interviewers respect that.

### How do I show I can handle an unpopular decision?

Pick one: closing a department, cutting hours, letting a manager go. Say how you decided, how you told people and what you did for those affected.
