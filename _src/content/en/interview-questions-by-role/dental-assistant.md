## Who's interviewing you and what they need

In most practices, you'll meet the office manager and the dentist, sometimes together. Small practices often do a working interview too, where you assist for a few hours. The dentist is picturing you beside them for eight hours: will you anticipate, keep the patient calm, and keep the room safe? The office manager is wondering whether you can cover the front desk when someone's off.

## Chairside support

*"How do you anticipate what the dentist needs?"*

Example answer:

> "I read the schedule and the chart before each patient, so I know the procedure and have the tray, materials and any X-rays ready before the dentist walks in. During the procedure I watch the dentist's hands and the stage of the work, not just wait to be asked: suction and retraction so they can see, the next instrument passed before they reach. Every dentist has their own preferences, so in my first weeks I'd keep notes on how you like things set up."

The last line matters in an interview with the dentist present. It shows you'll adapt to them.

## The anxious patient

Many patients are nervous, and assistants often spend more time with them than the dentist does.

Example answer:

> "I'd introduce myself, sit at their eye level, and ask what worries them most, because it's different for everyone: the needle, the noise, or a bad past experience. I'd explain what's going to happen in simple steps and agree a signal, like raising a hand, for when they need a break. During the procedure I'd keep telling them what's next and how long it will take. And I'd tell the dentist what they told me, so we're working together."

For a child who won't open their mouth: get down to their level, explain with simple words, show the mirror on a finger first, involve the parent, and never force. Then tell the dentist so they can decide.

## Infection control

This is where you can't be vague. Walk through your routine in order. Follow the steps your practice and local rules require; the example below shows the shape of a good answer, not a protocol.

Example answer:

> "After each patient, I take off my gloves and do hand hygiene, then put on fresh utility gloves to break down the room. Sharps go in the sharps container straight away. Instruments go to the sterilization area for cleaning, packaging and the sterilizer, and I check the indicator before they're used again. I wipe down every surface we touched with the approved disinfectant for its full contact time, then put on fresh barriers. I don't rush that step even when we're behind."

*"What if you saw a coworker skip a step?"* Say you'd mention it to them straight away and, if it was a patient safety issue or kept happening, tell the office manager or dentist. Don't say you'd ignore it to keep the peace.

## Moving between assisting and the front desk

Example answer:

> "In my last practice I covered the front desk two days a week. I'm comfortable checking patients in, booking appointments and answering basic questions about their treatment. If someone is upset about a bill, I listen first, look at the breakdown with them, and explain what each part is. If I can't answer an insurance question, I say who can and when they'll hear back. I like switching, because I can tell a patient at the desk exactly what their procedure will feel like."

## Mistakes to avoid

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "I just follow the dentist's lead" | Sounds passive | Describe how you prepare and anticipate |
| Vague infection control | It's a safety red flag | Your routine, step by step |
| "I'd tell nervous patients not to worry" | Dismisses the fear | Ask what worries them, explain, agree a signal |
| "I don't do front desk" | Practices need flexibility | Say what you can cover and what you'd learn |

## How to prepare

1. Write your room turnover routine in order, then say it aloud until it takes under a minute.
2. Prepare one story about an anxious or upset patient.
3. Practice the anxious patient as a role-play, since tone matters as much as words. Speaking Coach can play the patient and react to what you say.
4. Review the general [angry customer question](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/how-would-you-handle-an-angry-customer/) for billing scenarios.

## Questions people ask

### Will there be a working interview?

Many practices use one, often paid. Treat it like a normal day: ask where things are, follow their setup, and talk to patients the way you would on the job.

### What if I'm newly qualified?

Use your externship or training clinic. Describe real patients you assisted with and your sterilization routine there.

### Do they ask about X-rays and other certifications?

Often, because what assistants may do varies by location. Say clearly what you're certified or trained for and what you're working toward.

### How do I answer "Why dental assisting?"

Name one real reason, such as liking hands-on work with people, and add one thing about this practice, such as its patients or the procedures it does.

### What should I ask at the end?

Ask how the dentist likes the room set up, how front desk and clinical duties are split, and what training they offer. See [what to ask at the end of an interview](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/do-you-have-any-questions-for-us/) for more.
