## What the head guard is listening for

Lifeguard interviews are often short, sometimes held right after a swim test. The aquatics supervisor or head guard wants to know you'll act fast and correctly in an emergency, you'll enforce rules without starting fights, and you'll stay focused on the water when nothing is happening for hours. Your certification shows you've been trained. The interview shows whether you'd use that training under pressure.

Many candidates are teenagers applying for a first job. That's normal, and interviewers don't expect long work histories. They expect clear, confident, step-by-step answers.

## Rescue scenarios

Interviewers ask "What would you do if…" to check you'll follow your training in order. Talk in steps, out loud, as if you're doing it. Use the exact sequence and signals from your own certification course and the facility's emergency action plan; the example below is a general shape, not a replacement for your training.

Example answer (made-up details):

> "First I'd signal the other guards using our facility's whistle signal so someone covers my zone and someone calls for help if needed. Then I'd enter the water the way I was trained for that depth and reach the swimmer with my rescue tube, keeping it between us. I'd bring them to the side, and with help, get them out of the water. Once they're out, I'd check whether they're breathing and responsive and follow my first aid and CPR training. After that, I'd fill out an incident report."

| Strong scenario answer | Weak scenario answer |
| --- | --- |
| Clear steps in order | "I'd jump in and save them." |
| Mentions signalling other guards | Acting alone |
| Uses your rescue equipment | Swimming out without it |
| Follows your training and the facility's plan | Making it up as you go |
| Ends with reporting | Stops when the swimmer is out |

If you're not sure what this facility's procedure is, say so and say you'd follow it: "I'd follow your emergency action plan, which I'd learn in my first week."

## Enforcing rules with teenagers

This is where many new lifeguards struggle, especially if the swimmers are their own age. Show you can be firm without being rude.

1. **First warning:** short and clear. "Hey, walk on the deck, please."
2. **Second time:** name the reason. "I've asked once. If someone slips here, they can hit their head."
3. **Third time:** a consequence, as the facility allows. "Take a ten-minute break on the bench."
4. **If it continues:** involve the head guard or manager.

Example answer (made-up details):

> "At my last job at a summer pool, a group of boys kept doing flips off the side in the shallow end. I blew my whistle, made eye contact and said, 'No flips in the shallow end, it's only four feet.' They did it again ten minutes later, so I sat them out for ten minutes. After that they were fine, and one of them asked me about becoming a lifeguard."

Don't argue, don't lecture, and don't let it become personal.

## Staying alert

Interviewers want practical habits, not "I just focus."

- Scan your whole zone in a pattern, every few seconds, including the bottom of the pool.
- Change your posture and position when you rotate.
- Drink water and use your breaks to rest your eyes.
- Keep your phone away while on the stand.

> "I scan my zone from one corner to the other and always check the bottom. I find it easier to stay sharp if I count the swimmers in my zone every few minutes."

## First aid

Be ready for a minor scenario: a cut, a bee sting, a fall on the deck. Answer with your training steps: protect yourself with gloves, assess, treat within your training, and call for more help if needed. Mention your certifications and expiry dates, and only claim what you hold.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Hero answers.** Signal and get help; don't act alone.
- **Joking about rules.** It sounds like you won't enforce them.
- **Guessing about medical care** beyond your training.
- **Ignoring the question about other guards.** Say you'd speak up or tell the head guard, because a distracted guard is a safety risk.

Scenario answers are easier to say calmly after you've practised them out loud. Speaking Coach can give you a scenario and ask what you'd do next.

## Questions people ask

### What should I bring to a lifeguard interview?

Your certifications or proof you've booked the course, a list of your availability, and swimwear if a swim test is part of the day.

### What if this is my first job?

Use school, sports or volunteering: being responsible for younger kids, staying focused in a long practice, or speaking up when something was unsafe.

### How do I answer "Why do you want to be a lifeguard?"

Give a real reason: you love swimming, you want responsibility, or someone at your pool inspired you. Then add that you take the safety side seriously.

### Do beach and pool lifeguard interviews differ?

Yes. Beach roles often ask about open water, currents, weather and larger areas to watch, and may need a different certification. Check the job post.
