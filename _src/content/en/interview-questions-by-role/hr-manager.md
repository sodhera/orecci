## What a senior HR interview is testing

At manager level, nobody doubts you know the policies. The panel, often a senior leader plus someone from HR, wants to know how you behave when the right answer is uncomfortable: when the accused is the top performer, when a leader wants a quick exit, when people are scared about their jobs.

One rule runs through every answer: talk about real cases without names or identifying details. Telling a juicy story in an HR interview is the fastest way to fail it.

## The sensitive investigation

*"An employee reports harassment by a high-performing director. Walk me through what you do."*

Example answer:

> "First I'd thank the employee, take notes in their words, and explain what happens next and what confidentiality I can and can't promise. Then I'd look at interim steps so they're not working alone with the director while we look into it, without anything that feels like a punishment for reporting. I'd decide who investigates. If it's very senior or there's a conflict, I'd bring in an external investigator and involve legal counsel. Interviews with the employee, the director and witnesses, all documented. Then a decision based on the evidence, not on how valuable the director is, and I'd close the loop with both people. The thing I'd watch most is retaliation afterwards."

The panel is listening for sequence, neutrality and the line about not protecting a high performer.

## Policy or people?

*"Is HR the employee's advocate or the company's?"* Picking one side sounds naive either way.

Example answer:

> "I'm an advocate for fair process, which ends up serving both. In my last role a manager wanted to dismiss someone for lateness, and policy allowed it. When I looked, the lateness started when her shifts changed and she lost her childcare. We moved her start time by thirty minutes and the problem stopped. Policy was the floor, not the answer. But if someone breaks a serious rule, I'm not going to bend it because I like them."

## Layoffs

Example answer:

> "I led HR through a reduction of about forty roles across two sites. I pushed for clear selection criteria agreed before names were discussed, and we reviewed the list for any pattern that looked unfair. I trained every manager on the conversation itself: short, kind, no debating the decision, and a written pack with the details. We did all conversations on one morning so rumors didn't run ahead. The part I'd improve is the remaining staff. We under-communicated with them for the first week and lost two people we wanted to keep."

Admitting what you would do differently makes the rest more credible.

## Building culture

"Culture" answers drift into pizza and away days. Talk about systems that change behavior.

Example answer:

> "When I joined, exit interviews kept mentioning managers who never gave feedback. So rather than a values poster, we changed the manager routine: monthly one-to-ones became part of each manager's goals, I ran short workshops on giving feedback, and we added two questions about it to our engagement survey. A year later the feedback scores had moved and the exit comments had changed. Culture is what managers do on a Tuesday."

## Weak and strong answers

| Question | Weak answer | Stronger answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Investigation | "I'd talk to both sides and sort it out" | Ordered steps, interim measures, documentation, retaliation watch |
| Advocate or company | "I'm always on the employee's side" | Fair process, with a real example of both directions |
| Layoff | "It was hard but necessary" | Criteria, manager training, timing, what you'd change |
| Culture | Events and perks | One system you changed and how you measured it |
| Pushing back on a leader | "I'd do what they asked" | Name the risk, offer an alternative, escalate if needed |

## How to prepare

1. Choose four anonymized cases: an investigation, a pushback on a leader, a reduction or restructure, a culture change. Remove anything identifying.
2. For each, know the result and one thing you'd do differently.
3. Practice saying the investigation steps in order in about 90 seconds. Long, wandering answers sound unsure.
4. Rehearse with someone who pushes back. Speaking Coach's interviewer follows up on your actual words, and Tough pressure is closer to a skeptical executive.

See also [tell me about a time you disagreed with your manager](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/tell-me-about-a-time-you-disagreed-with-your-manager/), which comes up in most HR loops.

## Questions people ask

### How much detail can I give about real cases?

Enough to show your judgment, never enough to identify anyone. Use roles and rough numbers, not names, teams or dates that point to a person.

### What if I've never run a layoff?

Say so, then describe the closest thing: a restructure, a site closure you supported, or how you would approach one step by step.

### How do I show business sense as an HR manager?

Tie your examples to outcomes leaders care about, such as retention, hiring time, absence or legal risk, and use real numbers where you have them.

### What should I ask the panel?

Ask about the biggest people problem they want solved in the first year and how HR and the leadership team work together today. More ideas: [questions to ask at the end](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/do-you-have-any-questions-for-us/).

### Should I mention employment law?

Mention that you involve legal counsel when the risk is high. Don't cite specific laws unless they are central to the role and you know them well.
