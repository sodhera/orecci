## What makes this interview different

An assistant manager wears two hats: loyal number two when the manager is there, decision-maker when they're not. Interviewers probe both. They want someone who won't undermine the manager, and someone who won't freeze or phone them for every call when they're off.

A good test for your answers: does each one show you either **making the manager's job easier** or **making a decision on your own and owning it**?

## "How would you support a manager you disagree with?"

The answer they want: raise it privately, with a reason, then back the final decision in front of the team.

Example answer:

> "I'd raise it one to one, not in front of the team. Something like, 'I'm worried the new break rota leaves the floor thin at lunch. Can I show you last Saturday?' If they still want to go ahead, I'd roll it out as our decision, not theirs, and keep an eye on lunch coverage so we have facts if it needs another look."

Avoid "I'd just do what I'm told." It sounds safe, but it tells them you won't flag problems.

## "Tell me about a time you led when the manager was off"

Pick a shift where something went wrong and you dealt with it. Name the decision you made without asking.

> "Our manager was on holiday when two people called out on a Saturday. I called in one part-timer, closed the second till during the quietest hour, and moved our strongest person to the busiest section. I texted the manager a two-line summary at the end of the day instead of calling mid-shift. We hit the day's target, and she asked me to cover the next holiday too."

## Managing former peers

If you're being promoted from inside, expect this question. Interviewers want three things: you'll talk to people early, you'll be fair, and you won't play favourites with friends.

1. **Have a short one-to-one with each person in your first week.** Ask what they need from you.
2. **Say the change out loud.** "I'm still me, but I'll have to make some calls you won't love."
3. **Apply the same rules to friends first.** The team watches how you treat your friends.
4. **Keep the social side, minus the gossip.** Lunch is fine; talking about other staff is not.

> "When I became shift lead, my closest friend at work started coming back late from breaks. I raised it privately the first time, the same way I would with anyone else. It was awkward for a week. After that, the rest of the team took me more seriously."

## Opening and closing duties

This question checks whether you're reliable with keys, cash and security. Walk through it in order, briefly. The interviewer is listening for checks, not chores.

| Opening | Closing |
| --- | --- |
| Arrive early, check the building is secure before entering | Final walk-through of every area, including toilets and stockroom |
| Count tills and safe against the previous close | Count tills with a second person and record any variance |
| Check the schedule against who has turned up | Lock up in the set order and set the alarm |
| Brief the team on the day's goal | Leave a handover note for the opener |

Then add one problem you've handled, such as a till that came up short: "I recounted with a witness, checked the receipts, wrote it up and told the manager first thing, as policy says."

## Mistakes to avoid

- Talking only about tasks you've done, not decisions you've made.
- Criticising your current manager to show you'd do better.
- Saying you'd "stay friends" with former peers as if nothing changes.
- Describing an opening or closing routine with no checks or second counts.

Practice the "former peers" answer out loud. It's the one people ramble on, because it feels personal. Aim for about a minute, and use the [pace checker](/speaking-coach/tools/pace-checker/) to see whether you speed up when you're nervous.

## Questions people ask

### Is an assistant manager interview harder than a supervisor interview?

It usually covers more ground: cash, security, scheduling and covering for the manager, not just leading a shift. Expect more "what would you do" scenarios.

### What if I've never had a formal leadership title?

Use moments where you led anyway: training new starters, running a section, handling a complaint before calling anyone. The [leadership with no title](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/tell-me-about-a-time-you-showed-leadership/) page shows how to frame them.

### Should I say I want the manager's job eventually?

Yes, if it's true, but say it as growth: "I'd like to be ready to run my own store in two years, and I'd learn that best from backing up a good manager."

### How do I answer if I disagree with a company policy?

Say you'd follow it, raise your concern through the right channel with a specific example, and never tell the team you think the rule is wrong.
