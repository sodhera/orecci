## What interviewers want from a project manager

Whether the project is a hospital wing, a software rollout or a marketing event, PM interviewers are listening for the same habit: you make problems visible early, put clear options in front of the right person and keep a record. They are less interested in which methodology you prefer than in what you did on the day the plan stopped matching reality.

So every answer below needs numbers: the original date, the slip, the budget, the number of stakeholders. "It went a bit over" sounds like you weren't tracking.

## A project that slipped

Don't pick a project that was late through no fault of anyone's. Pick one where you can show how you found the slip, how you told people and how you recovered or reset.

Example answer (made-up details):

> "I was running the move of our customer support team to a new phone system, due live on 1 March. In week six of ten, the vendor told us number porting would take three weeks longer than quoted. I mapped two options for our sponsor: keep 1 March with only half the team on the new system, or move the whole go-live to 22 March. I recommended the split, because the old contract ended in April and a split gave us time to fix problems on a smaller group. She agreed. Half the team went live on time, the rest on 20 March. Now I ask vendors for a written porting timeline before we set a go-live date."

Notice the structure: when you found out, the options, your recommendation with a reason, the decision-maker's call, the result and the lesson. That is the order a sponsor wants to hear it in, so it's also the order an interviewer trusts.

## Scope creep

Show that you don't just say yes or no. You make the cost visible and let the sponsor choose.

1. **Capture the request** in writing, even if it came in a hallway.
2. **Size it:** extra days, cost, people, risk.
3. **Offer the trade:** "We can add the second warehouse to the rollout if we move go-live by three weeks, or drop the reporting module to phase two."
4. **Get the decision recorded** and update the plan.

> "Halfway through an office fit-out, the client asked for glass walls in two meeting rooms. I priced it with the contractor, about two weeks and 8% of budget, and gave the client the choice in a one-page change request. They chose to add it and push the move-in date. Because it was signed, nobody was surprised when the date moved."

## Stakeholder conflict

The interviewer is checking whether you get people talking about the shared goal instead of their positions, and whether you know when to escalate.

> "Finance wanted the new expense system live before year-end; the regional managers wanted it after, because December was their busiest month. I got both into one 30-minute meeting and asked each what would go wrong in their worst case. Finance's real worry was the audit, not the date. We agreed to launch in January with a manual audit report covering December, which I built with one analyst. Both groups signed off."

If you couldn't resolve it, say who you escalated to and how you framed it. Escalating with options is a skill, not a failure.

## How you track risk

Answer this one concretely: what's in your risk log, how often you review it and one risk that actually came true.

| What you say | Why it works |
| --- | --- |
| "I keep a risk log with owner, likelihood, impact and next step." | Shows a system, not a feeling |
| "We review the top five in every weekly status meeting." | Shows a rhythm |
| "Each risk has a trigger, like 'if the permit isn't back by the 10th'." | Shows you act before it's a crisis |
| "One we logged in week two, a key supplier closing for a holiday, did happen; we'd already ordered early." | Proof the log was used |

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Methodology lectures.** Mention Agile, Waterfall or PRINCE2 only when it explains a decision.
- **Hero stories.** Staying up all night to rescue a project says the plan failed. Talk about the earlier warning sign.
- **Blaming the vendor or the team.** Describe what you controlled.
- **No sponsor in the story.** PMs recommend; sponsors decide. Show you know the difference.

Answers like the slipped-project story fall apart under "Why didn't you see it sooner?" Practice that follow-up out loud. Speaking Coach's interviewer reads the job post you add and follows up on what you actually said.

## Questions people ask

### What's the difference between project manager and product manager interviews?

Project manager interviews focus on delivery: schedule, budget, scope and risk. Product manager interviews focus on what to build and why. See [product manager interview questions](/speaking-coach/interview-questions-by-role/product-manager/).

### Do I need a certification to answer well?

No. If you have one, mention it briefly, but interviewers weight real project stories much more than acronyms.

### How do I answer if I've never had the PM title?

Use projects you ran informally: an office move, an event, a system rollout you coordinated. Say what you planned, tracked and reported.

### How should I talk about a project that failed?

Own your part, give the numbers and spend most of the time on what you'd do differently. See [talking about a missed deadline](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/tell-me-about-a-time-you-missed-a-deadline/).
