## What the interviewer is listening for

Electrical interviews are usually run by a foreman, a project manager or the owner of a contracting firm. They've worked with electricians who cut corners and ones who don't. They're listening for three things: you won't hurt yourself or anyone else, you can find a fault without guessing, and you'll get the job done without fights with the other trades.

## Safety and lockout/tagout

Expect this early. A vague answer here can end the interview. Walk through your steps in order, and make it clear you test before you touch, every time.

OSHA describes lockout/tagout as the practices that protect workers from equipment starting up or releasing stored energy while it's being serviced, and requires employers to train workers in their procedures. Your answer should follow your employer's written procedure. A typical sequence:

1. **Know the equipment** and every energy source feeding it.
2. **Tell the people affected** that you're shutting it down.
3. **Shut it down and isolate it** at the disconnect.
4. **Apply your own lock and tag.**
5. **Release or block stored energy,** such as capacitors.
6. **Verify it's dead:** test your meter on a known live source, test the circuit, then test your meter again.

Example answer:

> "I don't work on anything I haven't locked out myself, with my own lock and my own tag. After isolating, I test my meter on a known source, test the circuit, then retest the meter. If someone tells me a circuit is off, I still test it. I once found a panel fed from two sources that weren't on the drawings, and testing caught it."

If they ask about live work, say you'd only do it when it's justified, with the right permit, PPE and training, as your employer's procedures require, and that you'd push back if someone tried to rush it.

## Troubleshooting a fault

Show a method, not a lucky guess. Start with information, narrow it down, then prove the fix.

> "A restaurant had a kitchen circuit tripping a couple of times a day. I asked when it happened, which was usually during the lunch rush. I checked the load and found the circuit was close to its limit with the fryers on, but it also tripped once with them off, so load wasn't the whole story. I split the circuit at each junction box and insulation-tested the sections. One box behind the dishwasher had water in it from a leaking seal. I replaced the box, fixed the seal and moved the fryer to its own circuit. No trips since."

The structure: symptom, questions you asked, what you ruled out, what you found, how you proved the fix.

## Reading drawings

They want to know you can work from prints, and what you do when the drawings and the site don't agree.

> "On a new job I go through the legend and the notes first, then the single-line diagram to understand how power flows, then the floor plans and panel schedules. If what's on site doesn't match the drawings, I don't improvise. I take a photo, mark it up and raise it with the foreman so it goes through as an RFI or a change, not something I decided on my own."

## Working with other trades

On site, your conduit, their ductwork and the plumber's pipes all want the same space. Interviewers are checking that you solve it with a conversation, not an argument.

> "If another trade has taken my route, I'll talk to their lead first. Often there's an easy fix if we look at it together. If not, it goes to the general contractor or my foreman. I don't move another trade's work, and I'd expect the same from them."

## Mistakes to avoid

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Try instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "I just flip the breaker and get on with it" | Fails the safety question immediately | Walk through lockout and testing |
| Troubleshooting by swapping parts | Expensive and doesn't find root causes | Describe how you narrowed it down |
| "I'd just reroute it" | Ignores drawings and the chain of approval | Raise it with the foreman |
| Badmouthing other trades | Suggests site conflicts | Show how you solved one |

Talking through a fault out loud is harder than doing it. Practice your best troubleshooting story until it fits in about two minutes; the [speech length calculator](/speaking-coach/tools/speech-length-calculator/) helps with timing. ## Questions people ask

### What should a first-year or apprentice electrician talk about?

Your training, the jobs you've helped on, how you follow safety procedures, and how you learn from journeymen. See the [apprenticeship questions](/speaking-coach/interview-questions-by-role/apprentice/) for more.

### Will there be a written or practical test?

Some employers include a short test on code, theory or a practical task. Ask when they invite you, so you can review.

### How do I talk about code knowledge?

Name the code edition you work to, how you check it on the job, and a time code shaped a decision. Don't quote sections you aren't sure about.

### Should I bring my own tools?

Ask beforehand. Be ready to say which hand tools and meters you own, and that you keep them in good condition.
