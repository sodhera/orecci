This page covers residency, fellowship and attending job interviews. It is not about medical school admissions.

## Residency versus job interviews

The questions overlap, but the panel's worry is different.

| Interview | What the panel is worried about | What to show |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Residency or fellowship | Will you be safe, teachable and good to work with at 3 a.m.? | Insight, honesty about limits, how you ask for help |
| Attending or staff job | Will you fit the group, carry your share and keep patients and staff happy? | Judgment, reliability, how you lead a team and handle complaints |

In both, clinical knowledge is mostly assumed from your training and references. What decides it is how you think and how you'd be to work with.

## Why this specialty

The weak version is a list: "I like the variety, the pathophysiology and the patients." The strong version is one moment plus a reason that will still be true in ten years.

Example answer for internal medicine (made-up details):

> "On my medicine rotation I looked after a man admitted with confusion. It took three days of slow, careful history-taking and talking to his daughter to find that a new medication was the cause. I liked that the answer came from thinking, not from a single test, and that once he was well I could see him through to discharge. That kind of puzzle is most of internal medicine, and I want to spend my career on it."

For a job interview, add why this group or hospital: their patient population, a service they run, the way they teach.

## A difficult patient

Pick a patient who was hard to care for, not a patient you didn't like. Show empathy for why they behaved that way, what you did and the outcome. Remove anything identifying.

> "A patient with chronic pain was demanding more opioids and had shouted at two nurses. Instead of starting with the medication, I sat down and asked what a good day looked like for him. He said he just wanted to walk to the bathroom alone. We agreed a plan around that goal with physiotherapy and a non-opioid option, and I told him exactly when I'd review it. The shouting stopped, and the nurses said the plan made their conversations easier."

## A medical error

This is the question candidates fear most and handle worst. Saying you've never made a mistake is not believable. The panel wants to know that you notice, disclose and learn.

1. **What happened**, plainly and briefly. Own your part.
2. **What you did immediately** for the patient's safety.
3. **Who you told:** your senior, the patient or family, the incident reporting system.
4. **What changed**, in your own practice or the system.

> "As an intern I wrote a dose for a patient's usual medication from an old list. The pharmacist caught it before it was given. I thanked her, corrected the order, told my senior and filed an incident report. Since then I check the current medication list against at least two sources before I prescribe on admission, and our team added that check to the admission template."

Choose an error that was caught or caused no lasting harm, and spend most of your time on steps three and four. There's more on structure in [how to answer the mistake question](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/tell-me-about-a-time-you-made-a-mistake/).

## Teamwork

Interviewers often ask about nurses, because a doctor who ignores nurses is a safety risk. The best story is one where someone else raised a concern and you listened.

> "A nurse called me overnight to say a post-op patient 'just didn't look right,' though the observations were normal. I went to see her rather than advising on the phone. She was more drowsy than at handover, and a blood gas showed she was retaining carbon dioxide. We adjusted her treatment and escalated to the senior. I thanked the nurse the next morning in front of the team."

## Mistakes to avoid

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Try instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Speaking about patients in identifiable detail | Breaks confidentiality, and panels notice | Age range, setting and problem only |
| Blaming nurses or juniors in a story | Signals how you'll treat their staff | Share responsibility, credit others |
| "I've never made a mistake" | Not credible, and it worries them | A real, caught error and what changed |
| Answers that last three minutes | Panels have a long list to get through | 60–90 seconds, then let them follow up |

Practice the error and difficult-patient answers out loud; they are the ones that go wrong under pressure. With Speaking Coach you can add your CV, and the AI interviewer asks about your actual rotations and follows up on what you say.

## Questions people ask

### How do I answer ethical scenario questions?

Think out loud: name the competing principles, say what more you'd want to know, say who you'd involve and give a decision. Panels mark the reasoning more than the conclusion.

### Should I prepare for a panel or one-to-one?

Both are common. Many programs use panels; see [how to prepare for a panel interview](/speaking-coach/interviews/panel-interview/).

### How do I answer "Where do you see yourself in five years?"

Be honest about your goals in the specialty and link them to what this program or job offers. More in [the five-years question](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/where-do-you-see-yourself-in-five-years/).

### What if I'm asked a clinical question I don't know?

Say what you do know, how you'd find the answer and who you'd ask. Guessing confidently is worse than admitting a gap.

### What should I ask at the end?

Ask about supervision on nights, how feedback works, what past trainees went on to do or, for a job, how call is shared.
