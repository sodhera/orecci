## What the panel is listening for

Case management covers many settings: social services, healthcare, mental health, housing, substance use, child welfare, insurance. The details differ, but interviewers in all of them want the same core skills:

- **Assessment:** you find out what a client actually needs, not what you assume.
- **Coordination:** you get several services to work together and keep track of who is doing what.
- **Persistence:** you follow up when things stall, with the client and with agencies.
- **Records:** your notes are timely, accurate and useful to the next person.
- **Judgement:** you prioritise by risk and know when to escalate.

Use real examples, but strip out anything that could identify a client. Saying "a client in her sixties with diabetes" is fine; names and specifics are not. Interviewers notice and respect this.

## Coordinating services

"Tell me about a time you coordinated services" is the question that defines the role. Use the [STAR method](/speaking-coach/guides/star-method/) and spend most of your time on the actions: who you contacted, how you kept everyone aligned, and how you checked it worked.

Example answer for a hospital discharge case manager:

> "A man in his seventies was being discharged after a fall. He lived alone, needed home oxygen, and his daughter lived two hours away. I set up the oxygen delivery before discharge, booked a home health visit for the first week, and arranged meal delivery through a local programme. I called the daughter to agree who would check in on which days and wrote it in a one-page plan for him and her. The day after discharge I called to confirm the oxygen had arrived; it hadn't, so I chased the supplier and it came that afternoon. He wasn't readmitted in the next month."

The follow-up call is the strongest part. It shows you don't assume a referral means a service.

## A client who misses appointments

This scenario tests whether you see non-attendance as a problem with the client or a sign of a barrier. Strong answers look for the reason before any consequence.

Example answer:

> "First, I'd reach out in whatever way they're most likely to answer: a call, a text, or through someone they trust if they've consented to that. When I reached them, I'd ask what's getting in the way rather than reminding them of the rules. In my experience it's often transport, work hours, childcare, phone credit or simply feeling overwhelmed. Then I'd change what I can: a different time, meeting them somewhere closer, a reminder text the day before. I'd document each attempt. If there were safety concerns, I'd follow our policy and talk to my supervisor rather than just closing the case."

| Weak approach | Stronger approach |
| --- | --- |
| "I'd remind them it's their responsibility." | Ask what's getting in the way |
| "After three misses, I'd close the file." | Try different contact methods, document, follow policy |
| "I'd keep scheduling the same slot." | Change the time, place or format |

## Documentation

Interviewers ask about documentation because it's where case managers fall behind. Describe a habit with a time attached.

> "I write notes the same day, ideally within an hour of contact, and I block the last 45 minutes of every day for anything left. I keep notes factual: what was said, what was agreed, who's doing what by when. I write as if the next reader is a colleague covering my caseload tomorrow, because sometimes they are."

## Managing a caseload

"How do you prioritise?" needs a method, not "I'm organised."

1. **Risk first.** Safety concerns, housing loss, medical needs, deadlines with legal or funding consequences.
2. **Time-bound tasks next.** Applications, renewals, court dates, discharge dates.
3. **Routine contact** on a schedule, so stable clients don't disappear.
4. **Ask for help early** when the caseload is beyond what you can do safely.

Mention the tool you'd use, whether a case management system, a spreadsheet or a weekly review, and say you'd raise caseload concerns with your supervisor rather than quietly cutting corners.

## Boundaries and burnout

Expect "How do you set boundaries?" and "How do you prevent burnout?" Give honest, concrete answers: you don't share your personal number, you're clear about what you can and can't do, you use supervision, and you have something outside work that resets you.

## How to prepare

1. Prepare four anonymised stories: coordination, a hard-to-reach client, advocacy, a client who refused help.
2. Learn the main services and agencies in the area you'd be working.
3. Practise each answer out loud at about 90 seconds to two minutes.
4. Rehearse with follow-ups. Speaking Coach can play the interviewer, or a reluctant client, and follow up on what you said.

## Questions people ask

### Do I need a social work degree to be a case manager?

It depends on the employer and setting. Many roles accept degrees in related fields or relevant experience. Check the job posting's requirements.

### How do I talk about clients without breaking confidentiality?

Remove names, places and identifying details. Describe the situation in general terms and focus on what you did.

### What if I've never formally been a case manager?

Use experience coordinating help for people: support work, healthcare, housing, volunteering or caring for a family member. The skills transfer.

### How do I answer questions about difficult clients?

Show empathy and limits together. Say what you did to understand the client, how you stayed safe, and when you involved your supervisor.

### What should I ask the interviewer?

Ask about typical caseload size, supervision, and which agencies they work with most. The answers tell you a lot about the job.
