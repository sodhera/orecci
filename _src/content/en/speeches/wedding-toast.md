## When guests give toasts

Most weddings have set speeches from the wedding party. Guests usually toast in a few situations:

- **The couple asked you.** Often a close friend, a mentor, or a relative who isn't in the wedding party.
- **An open-mic moment.** Some receptions invite anyone to say a few words.
- **The rehearsal dinner or a smaller party** around the wedding, where toasts are more relaxed. See our [rehearsal dinner speech guide](/speaking-coach/speeches/rehearsal-dinner-speech/).
- **A small wedding** with no formal speeches, where the host asks a few people to say something.

If none of these apply, don't stand up unasked. Even a lovely toast can throw off a planned schedule. If you'd like to say something, ask the couple or the planner a few days ahead.

## The four-line formula

A guest toast doesn't need a story arc. It needs four moves.

| Step | What to say | Example (made up) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Who you are | Your name and how you know them | "I'm Jordan; Alex and I have worked side by side for five years." |
| 2. What you've seen | One specific observation about them, or them together | "I've watched Alex go from answering emails at midnight to leaving at six, because Chris was cooking." |
| 3. A wish | What you hope for them | "I hope you keep choosing dinner together over everything else." |
| 4. The toast | Glasses up, their names | "To Alex and Chris." |

That's about 60 to 75 seconds spoken slowly. If you add a short story in step 2, you'll land around two minutes, which is the limit for a guest.

## Example toasts by how you know the couple

These are examples with made-up details. Use them for the shape, then put in your own.

### A college friend of one partner

> "Hi everyone, I'm Nadia. Sophie and I shared a terrible flat in our second year, with a broken heater and a landlord who never answered the phone. Sophie fixed the heater herself with a YouTube video and a butter knife. So when she told me she'd met someone who was 'actually handy', I knew it was serious. Dev, you're the first person I've seen her let help. Sophie and Dev, I hope your house is always warm. Cheers."

### A coworker

> "I'm Mark, and I share a desk with Rachel, which means I've heard about this wedding every single day for a year. Every day. But I've also heard how she talks about Leon: like he's the best part of her week. Leon, she brags about you. Rachel, Leon: here's to many happy, well-planned years. To the two of you."

### An aunt or uncle

> "I'm Ruth, Ben's aunt. I've known Ben since he was born, and I can tell you he has never been this calm in his life. Lily, whatever you're doing, keep doing it. We're delighted to have you in the family. To Ben and Lily."

### The friend who introduced them

> "I'm Kate, and I'd like a small amount of credit. Three years ago I invited both of them to a quiz night because I needed two more people on my team. We lost. They left together. Best quiz I've ever been to. To Hannah and Luis."

## What not to do

| Mistake | Why it's a problem | Instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Talking longer than the best man | It upsets the rhythm of the evening | Stay under two minutes |
| A story only you and the groom understand | Most guests are lost | Pick something a stranger can follow |
| Mentioning past relationships | Uncomfortable for the couple and families | Leave them out |
| Improvising after a few drinks | Toasts wander and run long | Write four lines and stick to them |
| Making it about you | "Let me tell you about my own wedding…" | Keep the focus on the couple |
| Starting with "I wasn't going to say anything, but…" | Sounds unplanned | Start with your name |

## How to deliver it

1. **Wait for a quiet moment** or for the host to hand over. Stand, so the room knows you're speaking.
2. **Hold your glass in one hand** and, if there is one, the microphone in the other. If there's no mic, speak a little louder and slower than feels natural. Our tips on [projecting your voice](/speaking-coach/guides/how-to-project-your-voice/) help in a noisy room.
3. **Look at the couple** when you speak to them, and at the room when you raise the glass.
4. **Say "to [names]" clearly** and wait for the room to echo it before you drink and sit.

Say the toast out loud at least three times before the wedding, ideally standing up. A one-minute toast feels much shorter in your head than it is out loud, and you'll find the line that trips you up. If you want to check the timing, you can try it with the [free speech review](/speaking-coach/tools/free-speech-review/), which shows your pace and filler words.

## Questions people ask

### How long should a wedding toast from a guest be?

One to two minutes. That's roughly 130 to 260 words. Shorter is almost always better than longer for a guest.

### Can I give a toast if I'm not in the wedding party?

Yes, if the couple has asked you or there's an open-mic moment. Otherwise, ask the couple or planner first.

### Should I write it down?

Write it, and keep a small card in your pocket. Four lines are easy to memorise, but the card stops you rambling if nerves hit.

### What do I say when raising the glass?

Something simple like "Please raise your glasses to Hannah and Luis." Their names are the last words.

### Is it okay to be funny?

Yes, if the joke is warm and everyone can follow it. One light line is plenty in a short toast.
