## What only a friend can say

Families knew your friend as a child, a sibling, a parent. You knew them in the parts of life they chose: the late nights, the trips, the jokes, the things they told you before they told anyone else. A friend's eulogy gives the family a picture of the person they were away from home, and gives the friends in the room permission to remember them as they really were.

If your friend died young or suddenly, the room may be in shock. Your job isn't to explain it or make sense of it. It's to make them present for a few minutes.

For the general approach, see [how to write a eulogy](/speaking-coach/speeches/eulogy/). This page covers what's particular about speaking for a friend.

## What to include

1. **How you met (20 to 30 seconds).** Short and specific. Often funny.
2. **Who they were with friends (60 seconds).** Their role in the group: the planner, the one who called, the one who made everyone dance.
3. **One or two stories (90 seconds).** Choose stories that show character, not just funny moments.
4. **What they gave you (45 seconds).** How you're different because you knew them.
5. **To their family (20 seconds).** A direct word. They need to hear how loved their child, sibling or partner was.
6. **Goodbye (15 seconds).** A last line, to your friend or to the room.

That's about four minutes, roughly 500 words.

## Humour: when it helps and when it doesn't

Many friendships run on jokes, and leaving them out can make the person unrecognisable. Laughter at a funeral is a relief, and families are often grateful for it. But it has to fit.

| Use humour when | Be careful when |
| --- | --- |
| They were funny, and everyone knew it | The death was sudden, violent or by suicide; keep it gentle and brief |
| The story shows who they were, not just a laugh | The joke needs context only your friend group has |
| You'd tell it in front of their grandmother | It involves drinking, drugs or anything the family may not know |
| The family has said they want laughter | You're unsure; ask a family member |

A good pattern: funny story, then a sincere line straight after. "That was Leo. He'd make you laugh until you forgot why you were upset. And then he'd ask, properly, if you were okay."

## Example eulogy for a friend (about 4 minutes)

This is a made-up example. Use your own memories and words.

> "I'm Sophie. I met Hannah on the first day of university, when she knocked on my door at 11 p.m. to ask if I had a cheese grater. I didn't. She came in anyway and stayed until three. That was Hannah. She didn't wait to be invited into your life. She just turned up with a reason, and then she stayed.
>
> In our friend group, Hannah was the one who remembered. Birthdays, job interviews, the date of your driving test. She'd text the morning of: 'You've got this. Call me after.' I don't think any of us ever had to tell her twice about something that mattered.
>
> She was also, and her mum will back me up, the worst driver any of us has ever known. On a trip to Wales in 2019, she got us lost for four hours and refused to use the map, because 'the sea is on the left, so we're fine.' We were not fine. We ended up at a farm, where a man gave us directions and a dozen eggs, and Hannah talked to him for so long that he invited us to his daughter's wedding. We went.
>
> That's what I want you to remember. Hannah made every wrong turn into a story, and every stranger into a friend.
>
> When my dad died two years ago, Hannah drove three hours, badly, to sit with me. She didn't say anything wise. She just made tea and stayed until I fell asleep. I didn't know until later that she'd taken unpaid leave to do it.
>
> Mr. and Mrs. Ward, Tom: I want you to know that Hannah talked about you all the time. She was so proud of where she came from. You raised someone who made everyone around her braver and kinder. All of us here are proof.
>
> Hannah, I'll text you on your birthday. I know you'd want me to. Thank you for knocking on my door."

## If your friend died young

- **Talk about their life, not the loss of their future.** "She was going to…" lines can be devastating. One is enough; focus on what they did and who they were.
- **Don't explain or speculate about the death.** That's not your role, and the family may not want it discussed.
- **Speak to the other friends.** They're often overlooked in grief. "To everyone who loved her: we'll keep each other going."
- **Be honest that it's unfair** if it feels right, in one line, then return to the person.

## Gathering material

1. Ask the friend group: "What's the story you'd want told?" You'll get more than you need.
2. Look through your messages and photos. Their exact words in a text can be powerful.
3. Talk to a family member about what they'd like, and what to avoid.
4. Choose two stories, not ten.

## Getting through it

- **Print it in large type** and mark where to breathe.
- **Read it aloud at least three times beforehand.** The first reading is the hardest. If your voice shakes, see [how to stop your voice shaking](/speaking-coach/guides/how-to-stop-voice-shaking/).
- **Arrange a backup**, another friend who has a copy and can finish it if you can't.
- **Pause when you need to.** Nobody in the room is in a hurry.

## Mistakes to avoid

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Only inside jokes | The family feels shut out | Stories anyone can follow |
| Making it about your grief | It becomes about you | Focus on them; one line about how you feel |
| Revealing things the family didn't know | Can hurt people at their worst moment | Check anything uncertain with a family member |
| Speculating about the death | Painful and often wrong | Leave it out |
| Going over time | Funerals run on a schedule | Three to five minutes |

## Questions people ask

### How long should a eulogy for a friend be?

Three to five minutes, or about 400 to 650 words. Ask the family or officiant how much time you have, as there may be several speakers.

### Can I use humour in a eulogy for a friend?

Yes, if they were funny and the story shows who they were. Follow a funny moment with a sincere line, and avoid jokes the family wouldn't understand or want.

### What do I say to the family in a eulogy?

Speak to them directly, by name. Tell them how much their loved one meant to their friends and something specific they said or did about their family.

### What if I'm too upset to give the eulogy?

You can ask someone else to read it, or read it with another friend beside you. Arrange a backup reader in advance, so you don't have to decide in the moment.

### How do I write a eulogy for a friend who died young?

Focus on who they were and what they did, not on the future they lost. Avoid explaining the death, and speak to the friends and family who are grieving with you.
