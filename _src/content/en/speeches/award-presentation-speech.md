## Your job: make the moment about them

Presenting an award is a short speech with a clear goal: when the winner walks up, everyone in the room should know why they deserve it. You're not the star. You're the one who sets the stage, says the name and then gets out of the way.

## The four-part structure

| Part | What you say | Time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. The award | What it's for and why it matters to this group | 15 to 20 seconds |
| 2. The criteria or story | What it took to win this year, or what the winner did | 30 to 45 seconds |
| 3. The name | Announce clearly, with a short pause before | 5 seconds |
| 4. The handover | Lead applause, present the award, step aside | 10 seconds |

About 60 to 90 seconds in total, or 150 to 200 words. A lifetime achievement or major honour can run two to three minutes.

## Surprise or known winner?

| Situation | Build the speech like this |
| --- | --- |
| Winner is a surprise | Describe what they did without names or giveaway details, then reveal the name at the end |
| Winner is already known | You can use their name throughout; spend the time on what they did |
| Several nominees | Read nominees first (with one line each), then "And the winner is…" |
| Lifetime or long-service award | More story, more history; can be warmer and longer |

For a surprise, choose details that only become obvious in hindsight. "This person rebuilt our onboarding while also covering two vacancies" makes people start guessing, which builds attention.

## Example presentation scripts

These are made-up examples. Use your own award, details and names.

### Example: company award, surprise winner (about 75 seconds)

> "The Customer Champion award goes each year to the person whose name customers mention most when they write in to say thank you.
>
> This year's winner had a hard start. They joined in January, in the middle of our system migration, when complaints were at their highest. Instead of hiding behind the script, they started calling customers back personally. By June, three of our biggest accounts were asking for them by name. One customer sent a box of pastries to the office with a note that just said, 'Tell them they saved my week.'
>
> They'd hate me saying any more, so I'll stop.
>
> The Customer Champion award for 2026 goes to … Nadia Okafor."

### Example: school or club award with nominees (about 90 seconds)

> "The Volunteer of the Year award recognises someone who gave their time, without being asked, to make this club better for everyone.
>
> Our nominees are: Ben Harris, who rebuilt the clubhouse kitchen over three weekends; Sophie Wu, who started our Saturday juniors session; and Raj Mehta, who has driven the away-match minibus every week for two seasons.
>
> All three of them deserve it. But one of them also stepped in as treasurer in March when nobody else would, and sorted out three years of receipts in a shoebox.
>
> The Volunteer of the Year is … Raj Mehta."

### Example: long-service award (about 2 minutes, opening only)

> "Twenty-five years ago this month, a young engineer walked into our Leeds office with a toolbox and a borrowed suit. She's still got the toolbox. The suit, we're told, went a long time ago…"

## Saying the name

The name is the moment everything has been building to. Get it right:

1. **Check the pronunciation** in advance, privately, and write it phonetically on your card.
2. **Pause for one or two seconds** before the name. Not a dramatic five-second pause, just enough for the room to lean in. See [how to use pauses](/speaking-coach/guides/how-to-use-pauses/).
3. **Say the full name, slowly**, a little louder than the rest.
4. **Start clapping straight away** and look toward the winner.
5. **Hold the award, shake hands, turn for the photo**, then step aside so they can speak if there's an [acceptance speech](/speaking-coach/speeches/acceptance-speech/).

## Mistakes to avoid

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Talking about yourself | The audience is waiting for the winner | One line about why you're presenting, if any |
| Giving the name away early | Kills the surprise | Save identifying details for the end |
| Reading a long list of achievements | It sounds like a CV and the energy drops | One or two specific examples |
| Mispronouncing the name | The winner's moment starts with a stumble | Check and practise it out loud |
| An overlong dramatic pause | Feels like a game show | One or two seconds, then the name |
| Fumbling the handover | Award, envelope, microphone and handshake at once | Agree beforehand who holds what |

## Delivery tips

- **Look at the audience** during the build-up and at the winner when you say the name.
- **Have the award and name ready.** If there's an envelope, open it before you start talking, unless it's part of the moment.
- **Rehearse once out loud with a timer.** A minute feels long when you write it and short when you say it. The [speech length calculator](/speaking-coach/tools/speech-length-calculator/) helps you check your draft.

If you want to rehearse with an audience that listens, Speaking Coach can play the room and show how your pace and pauses came across.

## Questions people ask

### How long should an award presentation speech be?

About a minute for most awards. A lifetime achievement or major honour can run two to three minutes. If there are many awards in one evening, keep each to under a minute.

### What do you say when presenting an award?

Explain what the award recognises, share one or two specific things the winner did, then announce their name and lead the applause.

### Should I say the winner's name at the beginning or end?

At the end, if it's a surprise. If everyone already knows who won, you can use the name throughout and spend your time on the story.

### What do I say when handing over the award?

A short line works: "Congratulations, this is so deserved." Then shake hands, pose for the photo and step aside.

### Can I make a joke when presenting an award?

A light one about the winner's habits works if it's kind and doesn't overshadow what they achieved. Keep the focus on why they earned it.
