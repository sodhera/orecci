## This page is for toasting someone else

If you're the one retiring, see [your own retirement speech](/speaking-coach/speeches/retirement-speech/). This page is for the colleague, manager or friend who raises a glass to them. Your job is to make the retiree feel seen in front of the people they worked with, without turning it into a eulogy or a roast.

## The four-part structure

1. **How you know them (15 seconds).** "I'm Priya. Gary hired me nine years ago, and for the first month I was scared of him."
2. **One story (60 to 90 seconds).** A specific moment that shows who they were at work. A small, true story beats a list of achievements.
3. **What they leave behind (20 seconds).** A habit, a phrase or a way of working the team will keep.
4. **The wish and the toast (15 seconds).** Something specific about what they're heading to, then glasses up.

The whole thing runs about two minutes, or 250 to 300 words. If several people are speaking, aim for one minute.

## Coworker, boss or employee: what changes

| You're toasting | Focus on | Tone | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A peer you worked beside | Shared moments, the daily stuff | Warm, a bit of teasing | Jokes only the two of you get |
| Your boss | What they taught you, how they treated people | Grateful, respectful, light humour | Flattery that sounds like you want something |
| Someone who reported to you | Their reliability, what they built, what you learned from them | Generous, specific | Talking mostly about yourself as the manager |
| A long-serving colleague you barely knew | Their reputation, what others say about them | Short and sincere | Pretending you were close |

## Example toasts

These are made-up examples. Use your own stories and names.

### Example: for a coworker (about 90 seconds)

> "For those who don't know me, I'm Jen, and I've sat across from Carol for eleven years. Eleven years of her humming while she does spreadsheets.
>
> Here's what I'll remember. In 2019 we lost a client on a Friday afternoon. Everyone else went quiet. Carol picked up the phone, called them back, and asked one question: 'What would it take for you to stay?' She listened for forty minutes. They stayed, and they're still with us. That's Carol. She calls back.
>
> Carol, you're off to the coast and, I'm told, a sailing course. The rest of us will be calling clients back and humming badly.
>
> Please raise a glass: to Carol."

### Example: for your boss (about 2 minutes)

> "I'm Marcus. Twenty-two years ago, Dr. Lee hired a nervous lab assistant who broke a centrifuge in his first week. I was waiting to be fired. Instead she showed me how to fix it, then said, 'Now you're the one who knows how it works.'
>
> That was how she managed everyone. She made you the expert in the thing you'd just got wrong. Half the people in this room run their own teams now, and I'd bet most of us still do that.
>
> She also never once sent an email after 6 p.m. We tested it.
>
> Dr. Lee, you've given this lab thirty-one years. You've told us your plan is your garden and your grandchildren, in that order, depending on the weather. We hope both thrive.
>
> Everyone, to Dr. Lee."

### Example: a short toast for a team send-off (about 30 seconds)

> "Ray, you've been the first one in and the last one to leave the warehouse for twenty-six years. You trained most of us on the forklift, and you never let us forget it. Enjoy the mornings off. To Ray."

## Finding the story

If you're stuck, ask yourself or two colleagues:

- What did they always say? A phrase people still repeat is gold.
- When did they help someone without being asked?
- What did they do that nobody else would have?
- What will be strange about the office without them?

Pick one story, not three. One story told well is remembered; three are a list.

## What to leave out

- **Age jokes.** "Finally old enough" or "over the hill" lines are tired and can sting.
- **Anything about why they're leaving** if it wasn't fully their choice.
- **Health, money or pension details.**
- **Office politics**, past restructures or the boss before them.
- **"We'll be lost without you"** said as though it's a problem for them to solve.

## Mistakes to avoid

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reading their career history | It's a CV, not a toast | One story that shows character |
| Making it about you | The toast becomes your speech | One line about how you know them, then them |
| Teasing that goes too far | Their family may be in the room | Tease their habits, never their competence |
| Going past three minutes | Others are waiting to speak, and drinks are warm | Cut the background first |
| Forgetting the toast | Everyone stands with a glass, unsure what to do | End with "To [name]" every time |

## Practise it once out loud

Say it with a timer and listen for where you rush. Most people speed up during the story. You can check your speed with the [pace checker](/speaking-coach/tools/pace-checker/); a relaxed toast sits around 120 to 150 words a minute. If you'd like an audience that listens and feedback that quotes your own words, Speaking Coach has an "Open a presentation or toast" scene for exactly this.

## Questions people ask

### How long should a retirement toast be?

One to three minutes. If you're one of several speakers, keep it to about a minute.

### What's the difference between a retirement speech and a retirement toast?

The retiree gives the speech; colleagues give toasts. A toast is shorter and ends with everyone raising a glass to the retiree.

### Can I make jokes in a retirement toast?

Yes, light ones about habits or quirks everyone recognises. Avoid jokes about age, health or anything that suggests they slowed down.

### What do I write in a toast for a boss I didn't like?

Keep it short and true. Thank them for something real, such as an opportunity or a lesson, and wish them well. You don't need to invent warmth.

### What do I say if I'm the manager hosting the party?

Welcome everyone, give a short toast with one story, and then invite others to speak. Hand the last word to the retiree.
