## Who is speaking changes the speech

| You are | What the audience wants from you | Typical length |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A student (class speaker, valedictorian, chosen by peers) | To hear their shared experience said out loud | 5–8 minutes |
| A teacher or staff member | Pride in the class, a few memories only staff saw | 5–8 minutes |
| A guest or commencement speaker | One lesson from your life, linked to where they are now | 10–15 minutes |

Always ask the organisers for your exact time slot and stick to it. Ceremonies run on tight schedules, and the families are waiting to hear names read. If you're the valedictorian or salutatorian, our [valedictorian speech guide](/speaking-coach/speeches/valedictorian-speech/) covers the extra things that role involves.

## Step 1: choose one theme

The speeches people remember are about one thing. Not "the future, friendship, perseverance and gratitude". One.

A theme is easier to find from the bottom up. List ten things your class went through together, then look for what connects them.

| What happened to your class | A theme it could point to |
| --- | --- |
| Your first year was disrupted, the building was being repaired, or the timetable kept changing | Making things work when the plan falls apart |
| The class team, play or project nobody expected to succeed | Doing something before you're sure you can |
| The teacher everyone feared and later loved | People who push you are often the ones who believe in you |
| Group chats, late-night study sessions, the library at exam time | You didn't get here alone |
| A tradition the class started | Leaving things better than you found them |

Write your theme as one sentence you could say to a friend: "We spent four years improvising, and we got good at it." Every paragraph should earn its place by supporting that sentence.

## Step 2: build it from shared memories

Class memories are what make a student speech land. The rule is that the memory must be shared by most of the room, not just your friends.

- **Good:** the fire alarm during finals, the cafeteria's mystery pasta, the first week when nobody could find room 214.
- **Risky:** something that happened on your sports team's bus, a teacher's private moment, a joke about one student.

Give each memory a detail people can see. Not "we had some hard times" but "we sat our chemistry mock in coats because the heating broke, and Ms Patel taught the whole lesson in a scarf."

For guest speakers, the equivalent is one story from when you were about their age, told honestly, including the part where you didn't know what you were doing.

## Step 3: the send-off

The close is what graduates carry out of the room. Make it concrete: something to do, not just something to feel.

Example send-offs (made up), each tied to a theme:

> "So here's my ask. Next time the plan falls apart, and it will, remember the chemistry mock in coats. We've done harder things in worse rooms. Improvise."

> "Somebody in this room helped you get here. Before you leave today, find them and tell them."

> "You don't need to know what you want to be. You need to know what you'll try next. Pick one thing, and try it before the summer ends."

## A shape you can follow

| Part | What to include | Time (student, 6 min) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Opening | A hook: a memory, a question or a picture, not a dictionary definition | 30–45 seconds |
| Thanks | Families, teachers, staff, in one or two sentences | 30 seconds |
| Theme, through memories | Two or three shared moments | 3 minutes |
| What it means now | The theme said plainly, linked to what's next | 1 minute |
| Send-off | One concrete thing to do or remember | 30–45 seconds |

See [how to start a speech](/speaking-coach/guides/how-to-start-a-speech/) for openings that grab a crowd's attention in the first ten seconds.

## Writing for a big room

A graduation is outdoors or in a hall with echo, a microphone, and families fanning themselves with programmes. That changes how you write and speak.

- **Short sentences.** Long ones get lost in the echo.
- **Leave room for reactions.** After a funny line or a shout-out, stop and wait. If you talk through the laughter, nobody hears the next line.
- **Slower than normal.** In large rooms, a pace of around 120 words per minute is easier to follow than your normal conversational pace. Our guide on [slowing down](/speaking-coach/guides/how-to-slow-down-when-speaking/) has drills for this.
- **Look at the graduates for the memories, at the families for the thanks.**

## Mistakes to avoid

- Starting with "Webster's dictionary defines success as…".
- Thanking 20 teachers by name and forgetting three.
- Jokes that only your friend group understands.
- Advice that sounds borrowed ("follow your passion", "the sky's the limit") instead of earned.
- Going over time. Ending a minute early is always welcome.

## Rehearse it out loud

Read it out loud, standing, at least five times, and time it. Your written draft will run longer spoken than you expect, especially with pauses for applause. If you want feedback before the ceremony, you can practise with Speaking Coach in the app, which shows your pace and filler words after each run.

## Questions people ask

### How long should a graduation speech be?

Student speakers usually get 5 to 8 minutes, guest speakers 10 to 15. Your school or university will tell you the exact slot; stay within it.

### Do I need to get my speech approved?

Many schools ask student speakers to submit a draft in advance. Ask early so you have time to make changes.

### Can I use a quote?

One short quote is fine if it connects to your theme. Put it in the middle, not as the first line, and say why it matters to your class.

### Should I memorise my graduation speech?

Know the opening and send-off by heart, and keep printed notes for the rest. Looking up from your page matters more than full memorisation.

### What if I get nervous in front of that many people?

Pick three friendly faces in different parts of the crowd and speak to them in turn. Breathe out slowly before you start.
