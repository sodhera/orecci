## How a GD round works

The group discussion (GD) is a common round in campus placements and MBA admissions, especially in India. The format varies, but often:

- A group of around 8–12 candidates sits together.
- A moderator announces a topic, sometimes with a minute or two to think.
- The group discusses for roughly 10–20 minutes, with no leader appointed.
- Assessors watch silently and may ask someone to summarise at the end.

There's no right side to take. Assessors usually score how well you contribute: the substance of your points, how clearly you say them, whether you listen and build on others, and whether you help the group move forward.

## Types of GD topics

| Type | Example topic | What helps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Current affairs | "Should India adopt a four-day work week?" | Reading news regularly; knowing both sides |
| Business | "Is quick commerce sustainable?" | Thinking in stakeholders: customers, companies, workers |
| Abstract | "Red is the colour of…" | Interpreting creatively, then making it concrete |
| Social | "Social media does more harm than good" | Balanced examples, not just opinion |
| Case-based | A short scenario to solve | Structure: problem, options, recommendation |

## Opening the discussion

Opening gives you visibility, but only if it's good. A weak or rambling opening is worse than not opening. Open only if you have a clear structure in the first few seconds.

A strong opening does three things: frames the topic, offers a structure, and leaves room for others. Example for "Should India adopt a four-day work week?", made up for illustration:

> "I think the real question is whether a four-day week can keep output the same. I'd suggest we look at it from three sides: employees, employers, and sectors where it simply may not work, like hospitals. My own view is that it could work for some office jobs, but not as a single national rule. I'd like to hear what others think about the employer side."

About 70 words, around 30 seconds. It invites the group in rather than lecturing.

If someone else opens, that's fine. Your aim then is to make the first useful addition.

## Adding points that move the GD forward

Most of the discussion is people adding points. The candidates who stand out bring a new angle and connect it to what was said:

| Move | Example line |
| --- | --- |
| Build on a point | "Adding to Rahul's point on productivity, there's also the question of how it's measured in service jobs." |
| Bring a new stakeholder | "We've talked about companies and employees. What about customers who need weekend service?" |
| Give an example | "During my internship, our team worked compressed hours in exam season and still met deadlines. The question is whether that scales." |
| Respectfully disagree | "I see it differently, Neha. A national rule would hit small businesses hardest." |
| Move the group on | "We seem to agree on the benefits. Can we spend a few minutes on how it would be implemented?" |

Only use a fact or figure if you're sure of it. A wrong statistic, confidently stated, is easy for others to challenge and hard to recover from.

## Getting in when it's crowded

GDs often become loud, with several people talking at once. Shouting over others usually costs marks.

- **Wait for a breath** at the end of someone's sentence, then come in with a name: "Priya, I agree, and…"
- **Start with a short, strong first phrase,** so the group turns to you: "One thing we've missed…"
- **Use a hand gesture** slightly raised to signal you want to speak.
- **If two people start at once,** let the other go first: "Go ahead." You'll get the next turn, and it looks composed.

The guide to [politely interrupting](/speaking-coach/small-talk/how-to-politely-interrupt/) has more lines.

## Summarising

Sometimes the moderator asks one person or everyone to summarise. Even if not, offering a summary near the end shows you listened. A good summary:

1. Takes 30–45 seconds.
2. Covers the main views fairly, including ones you disagreed with.
3. Names where the group agreed and where it didn't.
4. Adds no new points.

Example summary, made up for illustration:

> "To sum up, we looked at the four-day week from three sides. Most of us felt it could raise wellbeing and help keep staff, especially in office jobs. The main concerns were small businesses, essential services, and whether output would really stay the same. Where we largely agreed: trials by sector make more sense than one national rule."

## Mistakes to avoid

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Opening without a structure | A weak first impression for everyone | Only open if you can frame it clearly |
| Talking over others | Signals poor teamwork | Wait for a breath, use their name |
| Repeating what was said | Adds nothing | Build on it with a new angle |
| Inventing statistics | Easy to challenge, damages credibility | Use examples you're sure of, or reason without numbers |
| Speaking only once | Not enough to assess | Aim for three or four short, useful points |
| Speaking constantly | Looks dominating | Quality over volume; include quieter people |

## How to prepare

1. **Read every day for three to four weeks.** One national paper, one business source. Note both sides of the big debates.
2. **Practise with four to six friends** using a timer. Rotate who opens and who summarises.
3. **Record the practice** and listen for interruptions, long-winded points and how often you used names.
4. **Practise speaking for 30 seconds on a random topic** with no preparation. The [PREP method](/speaking-coach/guides/prep-method/) gives you a quick shape: point, reason, example, point.

You can also practise openings and summaries in Speaking Coach by describing the GD; the partner can push counterpoints and the feedback shows your pace and filler words.

## Questions people ask

### Should I start the group discussion?

Only if you have a clear, structured opening ready. A good opening gives visibility; a weak one hurts. Making the first useful addition after the opener is just as effective.

### How many times should I speak in a GD?

There's no fixed number, but three or four short, substantive points in a 15-minute GD is a reasonable aim. Quality matters more than count.

### What if I don't know anything about the topic?

Listen for the first minute, then contribute with reasoning rather than facts: ask a question, bring in a stakeholder nobody has mentioned, or summarise the views so far.

### Is a GD the same as a group interview?

They overlap. A GD is a leaderless discussion of one topic. A group interview can include introductions, shared questions and team tasks as well.

### Can I speak in a language other than English in a GD?

Follow the moderator's instructions. Most campus and MBA GDs are held in English, so practise speaking clearly in English.
