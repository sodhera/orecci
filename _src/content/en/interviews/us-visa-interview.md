## What this page does and doesn't cover

Visa rules, eligibility, documents, fees and appointment processes are set by the US government and change over time. This page doesn't cover any of that and is not legal or immigration advice. For rules, use:

- **The US Department of State's visa site**, travel.state.gov, for visa types and the application process.
- **The website of the US embassy or consulate** where you'll apply, for local instructions, appointments and document lists.
- **An immigration lawyer** or accredited adviser if your situation is complicated.

USAGov, the US government's information site, describes the visitor visa process as including the online DS-160 application form, required documents, fees and interview preparation, and points applicants to their embassy or consulate for how to prepare. It also notes that B-1 visas cover business travel and B-2 visas cover tourism and medical treatment, and that some people renewing a visa may be eligible for an interview waiver. Check the current official pages for your own case.

What this page does cover: how to speak clearly, briefly and honestly when you're nervous and talking to an official, often in a second language.

## What the interview feels like

For many applicants, the interview is short and happens at a counter or window, sometimes with a glass screen and a microphone. The officer has your application in front of them and asks questions to understand your situation. You may be standing, there may be noise from other windows, and you may have queued for a long time first.

That setting makes clear speech harder. People speak too quietly, rush, give long answers to simple questions, or recite something they memorised that doesn't match what was asked.

## The kinds of questions officers ask

The exact questions depend on the visa type and your situation. Broadly, they tend to be about:

| Theme | Example question | How to answer clearly |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Purpose | "Why are you going to the United States?" | One sentence naming the main reason |
| Plans | "How long will you stay?" "Where will you stay?" | Specific dates and places, as on your application |
| Your situation at home | "What do you do?" "Who do you live with?" | Plain facts about your job, study or family |
| Funding | "Who is paying for the trip?" | Name the person or source directly |
| Study or work details (for those visas) | "Why this university?" "What will you do in this job?" | A short, specific answer in your own words |
| Past travel | "Have you been to the US before?" | Yes or no, then the basic detail |

These are general examples, not a list of guaranteed questions.

## How to answer: short, true, in your own words

1. **Answer only the question asked.** If the question is "How long will you stay?", the answer is "Two weeks, from the 3rd to the 17th of June." Not your whole itinerary.
2. **Lead with the direct answer.** Put the fact first; add a detail only if it helps.
3. **Tell the truth, always.** Your answers should match your application. If something has changed since you applied, say so plainly.
4. **Use plain English.** Simple, short sentences are easier to understand through a glass screen than complex ones.
5. **Don't memorise speeches.** Memorised answers sound rehearsed, and they break when the question is phrased differently. Know your facts instead.
6. **If you don't understand, ask.** "Sorry, could you repeat the question?" is completely normal.
7. **Speak up and slow down.** Speak slightly louder and slower than feels natural, pointed towards the microphone if there is one.

## Example exchanges

These are made-up examples of clear, brief answers, not scripts. Your answers must be your own true facts.

> **Officer:** What is the purpose of your trip?
>
> **Applicant:** I'm going to my sister's graduation in Boston, and then I'll travel to New York for a few days.

> **Officer:** What do you do for work?
>
> **Applicant:** I'm an accountant at a manufacturing company in Chennai. I've worked there for four years.

> **Officer:** Who is paying for your trip?
>
> **Applicant:** I am, from my savings. My sister is giving me a place to stay.

Each answer is one or two sentences, gives the fact first, and stops. If the officer wants more, they'll ask.

## Managing nerves at the window

- **Breathe out slowly** while you wait in line; a long out-breath settles a shaky voice. More in [how to calm nerves before an interview](/speaking-coach/guides/calm-nerves-before-interview/).
- **Pause before you answer.** A one-second pause is normal and helps you answer the actual question.
- **Keep your documents organised** in the order your embassy's instructions list, so you're not searching while being asked something.
- **If you make a mistake, correct it:** "Sorry, I meant June, not July."

## How to practise

1. Read through your own application so you know your dates, addresses and details without looking.
2. Ask a friend to read simple questions in a random order, and answer each in one or two sentences.
3. Record yourself and listen: are you clear, loud enough, and stopping when the answer is done? The [how to stop rambling](/speaking-coach/guides/how-to-stop-rambling/) guide helps if answers run long.
4. If English isn't your first language, practise the same answers at a slower pace. Clarity matters more than accent; see [speaking clearly with an accent](/speaking-coach/guides/speaking-clearly-with-an-accent/).

## Questions people ask

### What questions are asked at a US visa interview?

It depends on the visa type and your situation. Questions are often about the purpose of your trip, your plans, your work or study, and who is paying. Official guidance is on travel.state.gov and your embassy's website.

### How long is a US visa interview?

It is often short, but it varies by embassy, visa type and your situation. Check your embassy or consulate's website for what to expect locally.

### Should I memorise my answers for a visa interview?

No. Know your facts well instead. Memorised answers can sound rehearsed and may not fit the exact question asked. Answer truthfully in your own words.

### Can I ask the officer to repeat a question?

Yes. "Sorry, could you repeat that?" is normal and much better than guessing at a question you didn't hear.

### Where can I find the official rules for a US visa?

The US Department of State's travel.state.gov site and the website of the US embassy or consulate where you'll apply. USAGov also has an overview of the visitor visa process.
