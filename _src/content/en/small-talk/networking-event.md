## Change the goal

Many people hate networking because it feels like selling yourself to a room. A smaller, clearer goal helps:

- **Have three real conversations.** Not twenty business cards. Three people you actually talked to for five minutes or more.
- **Learn one useful thing.** A company that's hiring, a tool, an event worth going to.
- **Leave when you've done that.** You don't have to stay to the end.

That turns a vague, draining evening into something you can finish.

## Before you go: prepare three things

1. **Your one-sentence answer to "What do you do?"** (see below).
2. **Two questions for other people,** such as "What brought you to this one?" and "What are you working on at the moment?"
3. **Your exit line,** so you never feel trapped.

Say them out loud a few times on the way. Lines you've only thought about tend to come out jumbled the first time you say them to a stranger.

## Who to approach first

Arrive in the first 15 to 20 minutes. Rooms are quieter, people are still arriving alone, and nobody is locked into a group yet.

| Who | Why | Opening line |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Someone standing alone | They're often relieved to be approached | "Hi, I'm Tom. Mind if I join you? I don't know anyone here yet." |
| People at the food or drinks table | Shared, low-pressure moment | "Have you tried these? I can't tell what's in them." |
| A pair standing at an angle, open to the room | They've left space for a third person | "Mind if I join? You two look like you know how these work." |
| The organiser or host | Their job is to connect people | "Is there anyone here you think I should meet? I work in logistics." |
| A tight, closed circle | Hardest to join | Wait, or find someone at its edge |

For joining a group that's already talking, see [how to join a group conversation](/speaking-coach/small-talk/how-to-join-a-group-conversation/). The short version: stand at the edge, listen, nod, and wait for a natural pause or for someone to turn to you.

## Opening lines that don't sound like networking

The event is your easiest topic. Everyone there has it in common.

> "What brought you to this one?"
> "Have you been to one of these before? Is it always this busy?"
> "Did you catch the talk earlier? I missed the start."
> "I'm Ana, I'm new to the area. How long have you been coming to these?"

Save "So, what do you do?" for a minute or two in. It lands better once you've already exchanged a few friendly lines.

## Talking about your work

When someone asks what you do, the worst answers are a job title alone ("I'm an analyst") or a long CV. Aim for one sentence that explains the work plus one detail they can ask about.

Examples:

> "I'm a nurse on a children's ward, and lately I've been training the new staff on night shifts."
> "I run payroll for a group of restaurants, which is mostly solving problems at 6 a.m."
> "I'm between jobs at the moment. I was in marketing for eight years and I'm looking at roles in sustainability."

The detail ("at 6 a.m.", "sustainability") gives them a natural question to ask. Then turn it around: "What about you?" For more, see [how to answer 'What do you do?'](/speaking-coach/small-talk/what-do-you-do/) and the longer [elevator pitch](/speaking-coach/presentations/elevator-pitch/) for when someone asks for more detail.

## Keep it a conversation, not a pitch

Networking goes best when you're curious about them. Good follow-up questions:

- "How did you get into that?"
- "What's the hardest part of it right now?"
- "What are you working on that you're excited about?"
- "Who else here should I talk to?"

If you can help them with something small (an introduction, an article, a name), offer it. That's what people remember.

## Exchanging contact details

Only swap details when there's a reason to follow up. It feels natural when it's tied to something you discussed:

> "I'd love to send you that article about route planning. What's the best way to reach you?"
> "Are you on LinkedIn? I'll connect so I can let you know when we're hiring."
> "Can I introduce you to my colleague by email? She's working on the same problem."

Then follow up within a couple of days, and mention what you talked about: "Great to meet you at the logistics meetup on Tuesday. Here's that article on route planning I mentioned."

## How to leave a conversation

Every networking conversation should end, usually after five to ten minutes. A clean exit lets both of you meet other people.

> "It's been great talking to you. I'm going to grab a drink and say hi to a couple more people. Enjoy the rest of the evening."
> "I don't want to keep you all night. Let's stay in touch."
> "Have you met Raj? He works in the same area. Raj, this is Mei."

The last one, introducing them to someone, is the most generous exit. More lines in [how to end a conversation politely](/speaking-coach/small-talk/how-to-end-a-conversation/).

## If you're an introvert

- **Take short breaks.** Step out for five minutes between conversations.
- **Stand somewhere with flow,** like near the drinks, so people come to you.
- **Go with one goal and leave when it's done.**

## Questions people ask

### What do you say when you walk up to someone at a networking event?

Introduce yourself and ask about the event: "Hi, I'm Tom. What brought you to this one?" It's simple, and gives them an easy first answer.

### How long should a networking conversation last?

Usually five to ten minutes. Long enough to learn something about each other, short enough that you both meet other people.

### How do I network if I don't have a job right now?

Say so briefly and point forward: "I'm between roles. I was in marketing for eight years and I'm looking at sustainability now." People often want to help when they know what you're looking for.

### Should I bring business cards?

Bring a few if your industry still uses them. Many people now connect on LinkedIn or swap numbers instead. What matters is the follow-up.

### How do I follow up after a networking event?

Send a short message within a couple of days that mentions what you talked about and anything you promised to send.
