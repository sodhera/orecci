## Three kinds of opener

Almost every good opener is one of three types. None of them needs to be clever.

| Type | How it works | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The shared situation** | Comment on something you're both experiencing | "This queue hasn't moved in ten minutes, has it?" |
| **An observation** | Notice something about the place or what they're doing | "That looks good. Is it from the stall by the door?" |
| **A simple question** | Ask for a small piece of help or an opinion | "Have you been to one of these before? I don't know how it works." |

The shared situation is the safest, because it's about the moment, not about them. Observations about objects and places are friendly. Observations about someone's body or looks usually aren't, so keep to what they're holding, reading, eating or doing.

## The opener formula: comment, plus a bit of you, plus a question

An opener on its own often gets a one-word reply. "Busy tonight, isn't it?" "Yeah." Then you're stuck.

Add a little of yourself and a question, and the other person has something to work with:

> "Busy tonight, isn't it? I came straight from work and didn't realise it'd be this packed. Are you here for the band?"

That's three short sentences: the comment, a small detail about you, and an easy question. It takes about eight seconds to say. It tells them you're friendly, gives them a fact to respond to, and asks something they can answer without thinking.

## Opening lines by setting

These are example lines, not scripts. Change them to fit what's actually in front of you.

| Setting | Opening line |
| --- | --- |
| Work event | "Hi, I'm Priya. I don't think we've met. Which team are you on?" |
| Party | "How do you know Sam? I'm a friend from his old job." |
| Class or course | "Did you manage the reading for today? I only got halfway." |
| Conference | "Which session did you just come out of? I'm trying to decide what to see next." |
| Coffee shop queue | "Have you tried anything here besides coffee? I'm tempted by the pastries." |
| New neighbour | "Hi, I'm in number 12. Did you just move in? Let me know if you need anything." |
| Waiting room or gate | "Do you know if this is the line for the 9:40? The sign keeps changing." |
| Video call, waiting for others | "Morning! How's your week going so far?" |

## How to approach someone

The words matter less than the approach. A few small things make any opener land better:

1. **Pick someone who looks available.** On their own, not on the phone, not mid-task, body turned slightly outward.
2. **Catch their eye first** and smile briefly. If they look away fast, it might not be the moment.
3. **Come from the front or side,** not from behind, and stop at a normal talking distance.
4. **Say your line at normal volume and speed.** Nerves make people mumble or rush. Slow down a little.
5. **Give them a second to reply.** Don't fill the gap with a second question straight away.

## What to do after they reply

The first reply tells you a lot. If they answer with more than a word and ask something back, they're open to talking. Follow up on whatever detail they gave you.

> You: "How do you know Sam?"
> Them: "We climb together on Thursdays."
> You: "Oh, at the place on Mill Road? I've always wanted to try it. Is it hard to start?"

If they give a short polite answer and look away, that's fine too. Say "Enjoy the evening" and move on. Not every opener should become a conversation. For keeping the good ones going, see [how to keep a conversation going](/speaking-coach/small-talk/how-to-keep-a-conversation-going/).

## Openers to avoid

| Opener | Why it falls flat | Try instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "So, what do you do?" as the very first line | Feels like a networking script | Start with the shared situation, ask about work later |
| A rehearsed pickup line | Sounds like a performance, not an opener | A real comment on where you are |
| A complaint about the event or host | Puts them in an awkward spot | Comment on something neutral or good |
| A personal comment about their looks | Can feel intrusive from a stranger | Comment on something they're doing or holding |
| Starting with a long story | They don't know you yet | Three short sentences, then a question |

## If you freeze before you start

Freezing usually happens in the two seconds before you speak, while you search for the perfect line. Two fixes:

- **Decide your first line before you walk over.** It only needs to be one of the three types above.
- **Practice saying openers out loud.** Ordinary lines feel strange the first time you say them, and easy by the fifth. ## Questions people ask

### What's a good universal conversation starter?

A short comment about what you're both experiencing, followed by a question: "This place is packed. Have you been here before?" It works almost anywhere because it's about the moment, not the person.

### How do I start a conversation without it being weird?

Keep it short, keep it about the shared situation, and give them an easy way out. Weirdness usually comes from saying too much too soon, not from speaking at all.

### Should I introduce myself first or ask a question first?

Either works. At events and at work, your name first is normal. In everyday places like a queue or a park, a comment first feels more natural, and names can come later if you keep talking.

### What if they don't respond much?

Give it one more easy question. If the answers stay short, smile, say something like "Enjoy your day," and leave it. That's a normal outcome.

### How do I start a conversation with someone I already know a bit?

Refer to the last thing you talked about: "How did the interview go?" or "Did you end up getting the bike?" It shows you remembered and gives them an easy start.
