## First, check your questions

The most common reason for one-word answers is the question itself. "Good weekend?" can only be answered with "Yeah" or "Not bad". That's a [closed question](/speaking-coach/glossary/closed-question/). It asks for a yes or no, and you'll get one.

Try turning closed questions into [open ones](/speaking-coach/glossary/open-ended-question/): questions that start with what, how, which or tell me.

| Closed (gets one word) | Open (invites more) |
| --- | --- |
| "Good weekend?" | "What did you get up to at the weekend?" |
| "Busy at work?" | "What's taking up most of your time at work at the moment?" |
| "Do you like it here?" | "What do you like most about living here?" |
| "Have you been to this café before?" | "How did you find this place?" |
| "Are you enjoying the party?" | "How do you know the host?" |
| "Was the film good?" | "What did you think of the ending?" |

The open versions are still easy to answer. They just leave room for a sentence or two.

## Share first, then ask

Being asked a string of questions can feel like an interview, especially for shy people. Many people find it much easier to respond to something you've shared than to come up with something from nothing.

> Instead of: "What do you do for fun?"
>
> Try: "I've just started a pottery class. I'm terrible at it, but it's fun. Do you have anything like that on the go?"

Your share gives them a model of the kind of answer you're hoping for, and something easy to react to ("Pottery? I've always wanted to try that").

A good rhythm: **share, ask, listen, follow up.** If you only ask, the other person has to do all the work.

## Pick up the smallest detail

One-word answers often carry a tiny thread. Catch it.

| They say | Follow-up that pulls the thread |
| --- | --- |
| "Fine. Tired." | "Long week? What's been going on?" |
| "Yeah, I went to my brother's." | "Oh nice, does he live nearby?" |
| "Work." | "What's the work at the moment, a big project?" |
| "It was okay." | "Just okay? What would have made it better?" |
| "Not really." | "Fair enough. What are you into instead?" |

Asking about *how* something felt or *why* gets more than asking *what*. "What made you pick that?" is easier to answer at length than "What was it?"

## Why they might be giving short answers

It's not always about you or your questions. Common reasons:

| Possible reason | Signs | What helps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shy or anxious | Little eye contact, but stays nearby | Share first, ask easy questions, be patient |
| Tired, stressed or distracted | Looking at their phone, sighing | Keep it brief and kind, try another day |
| Not a big talker | Friendly face, just brief | Enjoy the short exchange; don't push for more |
| Language barrier | Pauses, searching for words | Slow down, simple questions, more time to answer |
| Not interested in this chat | Body turned away, checking around | End warmly and move on |
| Busy right now | Working, heading somewhere | "I'll let you go." |

Body language helps you read which one it is. Someone who stays, faces you and makes some eye contact is often happy to keep going, even if they're brief. Someone angled away, glancing at the exit, probably isn't.

## Give them time

Many people need a few seconds to think before answering a question. If you jump in to fill the silence, they never get to say more. After an open question, count to three in your head before saying anything else. A short pause often draws out the second sentence you were hoping for. The [awkward silence guide](/speaking-coach/small-talk/awkward-silence/) covers what to do with longer pauses.

## Know when to stop

If you've tried two or three open questions, shared something of your own, and you're still getting "Yeah" and "Fine", it's okay to let it go. Some people don't want to chat right now, and that's not a failure on your part.

End it warmly so it doesn't feel awkward for either of you:

> "Well, I'll let you get on. Nice to chat."

> "I'm going to grab another drink. Good to meet you."

Next time you see them, try again. Many quiet people open up once you're a familiar face.

## Mistakes to avoid

| Mistake | Why it makes it worse | Try instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Firing question after question | Feels like an interrogation | Share something, then ask |
| Yes/no questions | Invite one-word replies | What, how, which, tell me |
| Filling every pause | They never get to add more | Count to three before speaking |
| Taking it personally | Makes you tense, and it shows | Assume shy or tired first |
| Pushing on after clear signals | Uncomfortable for both | End warmly, try another day |

## Questions people ask

### How do you talk to someone who gives one-word answers?

Use open questions that start with what or how, share something about yourself first, and follow up on any small detail they give.

### Does a one-word answer mean they're not interested?

Not always. They may be shy, tired or not a big talker. Look at their body language: if they stay and face you, they may just need easier questions.

### What questions get people talking?

Questions about how they feel, why they chose something, or what they enjoy: "What do you like most about…?" or "How did you get into…?"

### How many times should I try before giving up?

Two or three genuine tries is enough. If answers stay short, end warmly and try again another day.

### What if my partner or friend always gives one-word answers?

Ask about specific things, such as "What was the best part of your day?", and share first. If it's a pattern that bothers you, talk about it directly at a calm moment.
