## What recruiters at a booth are looking for

A recruiter at a busy booth may talk to many people in a few hours. In each short conversation they're asking: Is this person clear about what they want? Do they fit any roles we're hiring for? Would I remember them? A short, specific introduction and one good question answer all three.

## The booth visit, step by step

| Step | Time | What you do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Approach | 5 seconds | Wait for a gap, step up, smile, handshake if offered |
| Introduce yourself | 20 seconds | Name, field, target role, why them |
| Conversation | 2–3 minutes | Ask questions, answer theirs |
| Close | 30 seconds | Next steps, their name and contact, thanks |
| Notes | 1 minute, after you walk away | Write down names and what you discussed |

## Your 20-second introduction

Use this four-part shape:

1. **Name:** "Hi, I'm Aisha Rahman."
2. **What you do now:** "I'm a final-year mechanical engineering student at Westfield."
3. **What you're looking for:** "I'm looking for a graduate role in product design."
4. **Why them:** "I saw you're expanding your EV battery team, and my final-year project was on battery cooling."

At a normal speaking pace, that's around 50 words and roughly 20 seconds. Then stop and let them respond. The "why them" line is what most people skip, and it's what makes you memorable.

### Example introductions

**A final-year student**

> "Hi, I'm Aisha. I'm finishing my mechanical engineering degree at Westfield and looking for a graduate role in product design. I saw you're growing your EV battery team, and my final-year project was on battery cooling, so I wanted to come and ask about it."

**A career changer**

> "Hi, I'm Marcus. I've spent six years as a high school maths teacher, and I'm moving into data analysis. I've just finished a certificate in SQL and Python. I noticed you hire analysts for your education products, which seems like a natural fit."

**An early-career candidate with an internship**

> "Hi, I'm Lena. I graduated last year in marketing and interned on the social team at a small agency. I'm looking for a junior marketing role. I really liked your recent campaign for the city library and wanted to hear what the team's like."

These are examples of the structure. Use your own details. For a longer version of the same idea, see [how to write an elevator pitch about yourself](/speaking-coach/presentations/elevator-pitch/).

## Smart questions to ask the recruiter

Ask questions the website doesn't answer. They show you've done some homework and want real information.

- "What do people in your graduate programme usually work on in their first six months?"
- "What do the strongest candidates for this role tend to have in common?"
- "What skills are you finding hard to hire for right now?"
- "How does the application process work after today?"
- "Is there anyone on the team I could speak to about the design side?"
- "What do you personally enjoy about working there?" (People like answering this one.)

Avoid "So what does your company do?" and leading with salary or holiday questions.

## How to close

Closing well turns a nice chat into a lead.

> "Thanks, this was really helpful. What's the best next step for me? Should I apply online and mention we spoke?"

> "Could I take your card, or connect with you on LinkedIn?"

Then thank them by name and move on. If there's a queue behind you, keep it short. Recruiters notice candidates who respect their time.

## Mistakes to avoid at the booth

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "What jobs do you have?" as your opener | Shows no preparation | Name the role type you want |
| Reciting your whole CV | Too long, and they can read it | 20-second intro, then let them ask |
| Grabbing free stuff before talking | Looks like you came for the freebies | Talk first |
| Staying ten minutes with a queue behind you | Annoys recruiter and others | Three to five minutes, then close |
| Not writing down names | You can't follow up properly | Notes right after each booth |
| Saying "I'm open to anything" | Hard for them to place you | Pick one or two directions |

## Follow up within 48 hours

Send a short message or email: who you are, where you met, one thing you talked about and that you've applied (or will). "We spoke at the Westfield career fair about your battery team" helps them place you.

## Rehearse your intro out loud

Your 20-second intro should sound natural, not memorised. Say it out loud ten times over a few days and time it. ## Questions people ask

### What should I say to a recruiter at a career fair?

A 20-second introduction (name, what you do, what role you want, why them), then one or two specific questions, then ask about next steps.

### How long should I talk to a recruiter at a career fair?

About three to five minutes, less if there's a queue. Long enough for an introduction, a couple of questions and a clear close.

### What questions should I ask at a career fair?

Ask about the role, the first months, what strong candidates have in common and the next steps. Avoid questions the website already answers.

### Should I bring my resume to a career fair?

Yes, bring several printed copies in case they ask, but don't lead with it. Introduce yourself first.

### How do I follow up after a career fair?

Within 48 hours, send a short message mentioning where you met and what you discussed, and say you've applied or plan to.
