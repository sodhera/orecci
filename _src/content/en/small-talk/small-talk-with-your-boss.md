## Why it feels awkward (and why it matters)

Talking casually with your boss feels strange because there's a power gap. You worry about saying something unprofessional, or looking like you're trying too hard. So many people say nothing, which can come across as distant.

The fix is to treat your boss like a slightly senior coworker: friendly, curious, a bit more careful about what you complain about. Small, regular moments of normal conversation make the bigger conversations (feedback, raises, problems) much easier later.

## Lines for each moment

These are examples to adapt. Most of these moments last under two minutes.

| Moment | What to say |
| --- | --- |
| Waiting for a meeting to start | "How's your week going? Busy?" |
| Start of a one-to-one | "Did you get away at the weekend? You mentioned the coast." |
| Passing in the corridor | "Morning! Good luck with the board meeting today." |
| In the lift or at the coffee machine | "Have you tried the new café downstairs yet?" |
| At a team lunch | "How did you end up in this industry? I've always wondered." |
| After a big presentation they gave | "The part about next year's plan was really clear. Is the team growing?" |
| On a video call before others join | "Is that your dog I heard earlier? What's its name?" |

## Topics that feel natural

**Safe with almost any manager**
- The week, the workload, the weather (as a starting point)
- Weekend plans and holidays
- Food, local places, the commute
- Shared projects, framed positively or neutrally
- Things they've mentioned before: a trip, a child's exam, a marathon

**Good for building a real connection**
- How they got into the field: "What did you do before this?"
- What they're excited about: "What are you most looking forward to this quarter?"
- Advice: "If you were starting in my role now, what would you focus on?"

Asking for their view or their story is respectful and interesting to most people. It's also one of the best ways to learn what matters to them.

## The callback: the most useful habit

Remember one thing your boss tells you, and ask about it next time.

> Monday: "My daughter has her driving test on Thursday."
> Friday: "Did your daughter pass her test?"

It's small, but it shows you listen and care, without flattering. Most people don't do it, so it stands out. If you have a lot of one-to-ones, jot a note after each one.

## How not to sound like you're sucking up

The difference between friendly and fawning is mostly about what you praise and how often.

| Sounds like sucking up | Sounds genuine |
| --- | --- |
| "That was such a brilliant decision." | "That plan made my week a lot clearer, thanks." |
| Laughing hard at every joke | Laughing when something's actually funny |
| Suddenly loving their hobby | Asking about it if you're curious |
| Praising them in front of the team | Thanking them for something specific, one-to-one |
| Agreeing with everything | Asking a real question about their view |

Thanks for something specific is fine. Constant praise for decisions isn't, because it's not your role to grade them, and coworkers notice.

## Topics to avoid with your boss

- **Complaints about coworkers.** Raise real problems in a proper conversation, not in passing.
- **Gossip** about the company, other teams or other managers.
- **Salary and money,** outside a planned conversation about pay.
- **Your job search,** obviously, or how bored you are.
- **Very personal topics** (health, relationship trouble), unless you choose to share something that affects your work, in private.
- **Politics,** even if you think you agree.

## If your boss is quiet or hard to read

Some managers don't do small talk. That's not a sign they dislike you. Signs to keep it short: one-word answers, getting straight to the agenda, looking at their screen. Match their style:

> "Morning. Ready when you are."

You can still use the callback now and then. Quiet people often appreciate it more than chatty ones.

## If your boss is much more senior

With a skip-level manager or someone in senior leadership, keep it shorter and lighter, and come with one thoughtful question in case you get a minute: "What's the thing you're most focused on this year?" See [small talk with your CEO or senior leaders](/speaking-coach/small-talk/talking-to-senior-leaders/).

## Remote teams

On video, small talk usually happens in the first minute of a call. Ask about their week, something in their background, or the weather where they are. Keep it to a minute, then let them start. More in [what to say at the start of a Zoom call](/speaking-coach/small-talk/video-call-small-talk/).

## Practice the first minute

The first sixty seconds of a one-to-one set the tone. If they usually feel stiff, say a couple of openers out loud before the meeting. ## Questions people ask

### Is it okay to make small talk with your boss?

Yes. Short, friendly chats make working together easier and make harder conversations, like feedback or pay, feel less formal.

### What should I talk about with my manager?

The week, weekend plans, shared projects, their background and things they mentioned before. Ask about them and follow up later.

### How do I avoid looking like a suck-up?

Thank them for specific things instead of praising decisions, and be curious about them rather than agreeing with everything.

### What if my boss never does small talk?

Match their style and keep it short. A quick "How's your week?" is enough; don't push for more.

### Should I add my boss on social media?

Follow your workplace's norms. Many people keep work contacts on professional networks only. Let your boss lead if you're unsure.
