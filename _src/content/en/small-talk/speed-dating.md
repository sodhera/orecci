## What a few minutes is actually for

Most speed-dating rounds run somewhere between three and five minutes. That is not enough to learn someone's life story, and trying to is what makes so many rounds feel like a job interview: "What do you do? Where are you from? Do you have siblings?" By round six, everyone is giving the same answers on autopilot.

The real question each round is simpler: **would I enjoy a longer conversation with this person?** You find that out from how they talk, what lights them up, and whether the back-and-forth feels easy. Choose questions that show you that.

## A minute-by-minute plan for a four-minute round

| Time | What to do | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0:00–0:20 | Warm hello and name | "Hi, I'm Dev. How's your night going so far?" |
| 0:20–1:30 | One question that invites a story | "What's been the best part of your week?" |
| 1:30–3:00 | Follow their most interesting detail, share yours | "Wait, you're learning the cello? What made you start?" |
| 3:00–3:40 | One lighter or playful question | "Mountains or beach for a holiday?" |
| 3:40–4:00 | Close warmly | "That was fun. Good luck with the cello recital." |

For a three-minute round, drop the playful question. For five minutes, give the follow-up section more time. Don't fill every second; one good thread beats five topics.

## Openers that start faster than "So, what do you do?"

You don't need a clever line. You need one that lets them talk about something they enjoy:

- "What's been the highlight of your week?"
- "What made you come tonight?" (honest and often funny)
- "What are you looking forward to at the moment?"
- "How many rounds in are you? Has anyone asked you something good yet?"
- "What's something you've got really into recently?"

If they ask "What do you do?" first, answer with a hook rather than a title. The [what do you do guide](/speaking-coach/small-talk/what-do-you-do/) has examples, and the shape works well under time pressure.

## Questions that reveal more than small talk

These are [open questions](/speaking-coach/glossary/open-ended-question/) that ask for a story or an opinion, not a fact. Pick two or three you like and keep them ready.

| Instead of | Ask | What you learn |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "What do you do?" | "What's the best part of your job, and the worst?" | What they value, and whether they can laugh about work |
| "Where are you from?" | "What do you miss most about where you grew up?" | Family, roots, what matters to them |
| "Do you like travelling?" | "What's the best place you've been that most people haven't?" | Curiosity, how they tell a story |
| "What are your hobbies?" | "What could you talk about for an hour without getting bored?" | Real enthusiasm |
| "Are you a morning person?" | "What does a really good Sunday look like for you?" | Lifestyle fit |
| "Do you like your friends?" | "What would your friends say you're always the one to do?" | How they see themselves |

Then follow up. The question is only the door; the follow-up ("What made you choose that?" "How did that go?") is where you actually learn something.

## Lighter questions for the last minute

A quick playful question changes the energy and gives you something to remember them by:

- "What's your most unpopular food opinion?"
- "What's a skill you'd love to have overnight?"
- "Which would you pick: a year without music or a year without films?"
- "What's the last thing that made you laugh properly?"

Use one, not a quiz. A string of quick-fire questions feels like a game show.

## Sharing your side

The other person is also deciding whether they'd enjoy talking to you. Make it easy for them:

- **Answer your own question** briefly after they do. "Mine's walking the coast path near my mum's."
- **Give a detail, not a list.** "I teach Year 5, and this week a kid asked me if I lived at school" beats "I'm a teacher, I like hiking, reading and travel."
- **Let your face show what you enjoy.** You don't need to be loud. Just talk about something you actually care about.

## What to skip in a short round

| Skip | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Exes and past relationships | Heavy, and it eats the round |
| "Why are you single?" | Puts them on the defensive |
| Salaries, house prices, career rank | Feels like screening, not chatting |
| A long monologue about your job | Uses their time, tells them little |
| Checking your phone or the clock | They notice instantly |
| Complaining about the event | Sets a flat tone |

## Ending each round well

When the bell or signal goes, close cleanly. The close is the last impression they have before marking their card:

> "I really enjoyed that. Good luck with the half-marathon."

> "Thanks, that was the best question anyone's asked me tonight."

Then, if the event uses cards or an app, make a note straight away: their name and one detail. After eight rounds, they blur.

If you match and meet again, the next conversation has more room. The [first date guide](/speaking-coach/small-talk/first-date-conversation/) picks up from there.

## Practising a few rounds before the night

Speed dating rewards being relaxed in the first 20 seconds, which is the hardest part when you're nervous. Running a couple of practice rounds out loud helps. In the Speaking Coach app, the "First date" scene gives you a partner who plays the other person and responds to what you say, and you can choose a Gentle or Realistic pressure level. Afterwards you see your pace and filler words, which is useful if nerves make you speed up.

## Questions people ask

### What should I say first at speed dating?

A warm hello and your name, then an easy open question such as "What's been the best part of your week?" Skip the clever line.

### How many questions should I prepare?

Two or three good open questions and one playful one are enough. Most of the round should be follow-ups to what they say.

### Is it okay to ask what someone does for work?

Yes, but ask about the best and worst parts, not just the title. That gives you something real to talk about.

### What if there's an awkward silence in my round?

Ask about something they mentioned earlier, or use your playful question. A short pause is normal; you don't need to fill every second.

### How do I remember everyone after eight rounds?

Write each person's name and one detail on your card or phone straight after the round.
