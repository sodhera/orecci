## The three moves

Most conversations don't die because people run out of topics. They die because each reply closes a door instead of opening one. Three moves keep doors open:

| Move | What it does | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Follow up** | Shows you listened and asks for more of the same | "You said the move was chaotic. What went wrong?" |
| **Share back** | Gives them something of yours to react to | "I moved last year and the van showed up a day late." |
| **Thread** | Picks up a detail they mentioned earlier but didn't explore | "Earlier you said you're new to cycling. Did you ride here?" |

Use them in a rough rotation. Only follow-ups, and it becomes an interview. Only sharing, and it becomes a monologue. Only threading, and it feels like you're jumping around.

## Listen for free information

People almost always say more than you asked. Those extras are the easiest place to go next.

Ask "How was your weekend?" and you might hear: "Good, quiet. Went to my sister's for her kid's birthday and then mostly slept."

There are at least three threads in that one answer:

- The sister ("Do you see her a lot? Does she live nearby?")
- The kid's birthday ("How old? Was it a chaos party with twenty kids?")
- Sleeping all Sunday ("Busy week before that?")

Pick the one that sounds most alive to you. Keep the others in your back pocket for later. If the conversation slows, you can come back: "Wait, how old was your nephew turning?"

## Dead-end replies and how to open them

The same moment can end a conversation or keep it going, depending on your reply.

| They say | Dead-end reply | Reply that keeps it going |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "I just started learning Spanish." | "Oh nice." | "What made you pick Spanish? Are you planning a trip?" |
| "Work's been crazy." | "Same." | "Crazy busy or crazy chaotic? What's going on?" |
| "I'm a nurse." | "Cool." | "What kind of ward? Do you do night shifts?" |
| "We saw that new film." | "I haven't seen it." | "Is it worth seeing? I keep hearing mixed things." |
| "I'm from a small town up north." | "Okay." | "Small like everyone knows your business?" |

"Same" and "Oh nice" are not rude. They just put the work back on the other person. Add a question or a detail and you've given them somewhere to go.

## How to share back without taking over

Sharing is what makes a conversation feel two-way. The trick is keeping it short and connected.

- **Keep it to two or three sentences.** Then stop, or turn it back with a question.
- **Match, don't top.** If they had a bad flight, a similar story helps. A worse story turns it into a competition.
- **Connect it to their point.** "That reminds me of…" works when the link is clear.

Example of sharing back:

> Them: "I've been trying to cook more instead of ordering in."
> You: "I tried that in January and lasted about three weeks. The only thing I can make well is a curry my flatmate taught me. What are you making?"

You gave a short, honest story and handed the conversation back with a question.

## Questions that open things up

Open questions start with *how*, *what*, or *what's it like*. They invite longer answers than yes-or-no questions. A few that work in most settings:

- "How did you get into that?"
- "What's the best part of it?"
- "What's that like day to day?"
- "What happened next?"
- "Would you do it again?"

"Why" can be useful, but it can sound like a challenge ("Why would you do that?"). "What made you…" asks the same thing more gently. The [open-ended question](/speaking-coach/glossary/open-ended-question/) entry has more examples.

## When to change the thread

Every thread runs out. Signs it's time to move on: answers get shorter, you're both repeating yourselves, or the energy dips. Three easy ways to change direction:

1. **Go back to an earlier thread.** "You mentioned your sister's in Lisbon. Have you visited?"
2. **Use the setting.** "This place is busier than I expected. Do you come here often?"
3. **Zoom out.** "Anything fun coming up this month?"

If even that falls flat, it might be a natural end, which is fine. A short, warm conversation is better than a long, strained one. See [how to end a conversation politely](/speaking-coach/small-talk/how-to-end-a-conversation/).

## Common mistakes

- **Planning your next line while they talk.** You miss the free information that would make your next line easy.
- **Asking a second question before they finish answering the first.** Let them land.
- **Treating every pause as an emergency.** A pause of two or three seconds is normal. People often add more if you wait.
- **Correcting or one-upping.** It ends threads fast.

## Questions people ask

### How do I keep a conversation going over text?

The same three moves work: follow up on a detail, share something back, and pick up earlier threads. End messages with something they can reply to, not a statement that closes the topic.

### What if I can't think of a follow-up question?

Repeat the last few words they said with a question in your voice: "Three weeks in Japan?" People usually explain more. It also buys you a second to think.

### How do I stop talking too much about myself?

After you share something, end with a question back. If you notice you've been talking for over a minute, pause and ask what they think.

### What if the other person isn't making any effort?

Try two or three open questions. If you still get one-word replies, they may be busy, shy or not in the mood. See [what to do with one-word answers](/speaking-coach/small-talk/one-word-answers/), and don't take it personally.

### Is it okay to have a pause in a conversation?

Yes. Short pauses are normal and often lead somewhere. Only longer, uncomfortable silences need a restart line.
