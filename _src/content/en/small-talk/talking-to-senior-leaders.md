## What a senior leader actually wants from this chat

Senior people often get approached with requests, complaints or pitches. A short, friendly chat that asks nothing of them is a nice change. They're usually curious about what's happening on the ground, and they'll remember someone who was easy to talk to and clear about their work.

You don't need to impress. You need to be clear, warm and brief.

## Your one-sentence introduction

Leaders may not know your name or team. Give both, plus what you work on, in one sentence:

> "Hi, I'm Tara. I'm on the payments team, working on the new checkout."

> "Hi, I'm Felix, I joined the Dublin sales team in March."

If they already know you, skip it and go straight to a question.

## Openers by situation

| Situation | Opener |
| --- | --- |
| Lift or elevator | "Morning. Busy day?" or "How was the trip to the Singapore office?" |
| Lunch queue or kitchen | "Have you tried the new curry? It's surprisingly good." |
| After a talk or all-hands they gave | "I liked what you said about focusing on fewer projects. It's made a difference for our team." |
| At an event or party | "Thanks for tonight. Are you taking a break over the holidays?" |
| Visiting your office | "Welcome to Manchester. Is this your first time at this site?" |

Referring to something they said in a recent meeting is the strongest opener. It shows you listen and gives them something to talk about. Keep any compliment specific and brief, not gushing.

## Good questions to ask a senior leader

- "What's keeping you busy this week?"
- "What are you most excited about for next year?"
- "What did you think of the offsite?"
- "How did you end up in this role? I know you started in engineering."
- "Any good book or podcast recommendations lately?"

Then follow their answer with one more question. Let them do most of the talking.

## If they ask about your work

This is your chance to be clear, not to pitch. Answer in two or three sentences: what you're working on, one thing going well, and what's next.

> "We're rebuilding the checkout flow. The first version went live last week and fewer people are dropping off at the payment step. Next we're working on mobile."

If you have a real number you're allowed to share, use it. Avoid jargon only your team understands. A habit worth practicing is putting the main point first, sometimes called [BLUF](/speaking-coach/glossary/bluf/), or bottom line up front.

## What not to do

| Mistake | Why it backfires | Instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pitching your idea in the lift | Feels like an ambush | If it's good, ask for time properly later |
| Complaining about your manager or team | Puts them in an awkward spot | Raise issues through the right channels |
| Asking for a promotion or raise | Wrong time, wrong person | Talk to your own manager |
| Heavy flattery | Sounds insincere | One specific, honest comment |
| Talking about yourself for minutes | They have little time | Two or three sentences, then a question |
| Freezing and saying nothing | Misses a good chance | A simple "Morning, busy week?" is enough |

## How to end it

Senior people have packed days, so a clean exit is a courtesy:

> "I'll let you go. Good to chat."

> "Thanks, and good luck with the board meeting."

If they say "Let's talk more about that," follow up with a short message within a day or two, and keep it short.

## If you're nervous

It's normal to feel a jolt of nerves near senior people. Two things help: prepare your one-sentence intro and one question in advance, and slow down your first sentence. Nerves usually show as speed, so speaking a little slower reads as calm. For the wider skill, see [executive presence](/speaking-coach/glossary/executive-presence/).

## Questions people ask

### What do you say to your CEO in the elevator?

A greeting, your name and team in one sentence, and a light question, such as "Busy week?" or something about a recent all-hands. Keep it under a minute.

### Should I pitch an idea if I meet a senior leader?

Not on the spot. If they ask what you're working on, answer clearly. If you have an idea, ask your manager how to share it properly, or ask the leader for a short meeting later.

### How do I make a good impression on senior leadership?

Be brief, clear about your work, positive and genuinely curious about them. A short, easy chat stands out more than a long one.

### Is it okay to talk about non-work topics with the CEO?

Yes. Travel, books, sport and weekend plans are fine. Leaders are people too, and a normal chat is often welcome.

### What if the CEO asks me about problems at work?

Be honest but constructive. Describe the issue briefly and factually, and if you can, mention what might help. Avoid blaming individuals.
