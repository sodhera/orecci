## The worry behind the question

When an interviewer sees four jobs in five years, they do a quick calculation: hiring and training you costs time and money, and if you leave in a year, they'll pay it again. They aren't assuming you're a bad worker. They're asking: **will you leave us too, and why?**

So your answer has two jobs:

1. Make your past moves look like they had a reason, not like restlessness.
2. Give them a believable reason to think this time is different.

Most people only do the first, and then the interviewer is left with the same worry.

## Step 1: map your moves before the interview

Write each short job in a table like this. It turns a messy history into a story you can tell.

| Job | Length | Why it ended | What you gained |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Junior designer, agency | 11 months | Wanted in-house work, not client turnover | Speed, handling feedback |
| Designer, startup | 9 months | Startup ran out of money | Built a design system from scratch |
| Contract designer, retailer | 6 months | Fixed-term contract ended | Large-scale e-commerce work |
| Product designer, fintech | 14 months | Team restructured | User research, working with engineers |

Now look at the last column. What links it? Here, the thread is "I've moved from making things look good to designing products end to end." That sentence is the core of your answer.

## Step 2: sort your reasons

Not every move needs the same explanation. Group them:

- **Outside your control:** layoffs, a company closing, a contract ending, a partner's relocation, a health or family need. Name these in a few words each, with no apology. Interviewers discount them almost completely.
- **Your choice, for growth:** a bigger role, a skill you couldn't build where you were, a move from agency to in-house. These need the thread.
- **Your choice, for fit:** the job wasn't what was described, or the culture didn't suit you. Keep these short, own the misjudgment, and say what you now check before accepting.

If most of your moves were outside your control, say that early: "Two of those were contracts and one was a closure, so I've actually only chosen to leave once."

## Step 3: the commitment signal

This is the part that answers the real worry. Specific reasons beat promises. "I'm looking for somewhere to stay long term" is what everyone says. Instead, say why *this* job is different:

- "This role has a path from designer to lead, which none of my last three had."
- "You're a stable company with a product that's been around for ten years. After two startups, that matters to me."
- "It's permanent, and I've been on contracts. I'm ready to stay in one team and build something over time."
- "The work here is the thing my last four jobs were leading towards."

Then add something you want to do over the next two or three years. It shows you're imagining yourself there.

## Example answers

These examples use invented details. Fit the structure to your own history.

### Mixed reasons (product designer)

> "Looking back, each move took me a step closer to designing whole products rather than screens. Two of them weren't my choice: the startup ran out of money, and the retail role was a six-month contract. The other two I chose because I couldn't do user research where I was. What's different here is that this role includes research and has a clear path to lead designer. I'd like to spend the next few years building your design system and growing into that lead role."

### Early-career exploration (graduate with three jobs in two years)

> "In my first two years after university I tried three quite different roles: recruitment, events and customer support. I'll be honest, I was working out what I was good at. What I found is that my best days in all three were when I was solving a customer's problem one-to-one. That's why I'm applying for customer success roles now, and why this one, with a long-term account portfolio, fits. I'm not looking to try anything else. I've found the thing."

### Contract-heavy career (IT support)

> "Most of my roles have been fixed-term contracts, which is common in IT support. Each was renewed at least once, and I left only when the projects ended. I've enjoyed the variety, but I want to stay in one place long enough to see systems I've set up through their full life, which is why I'm only applying for permanent roles now."

### Moves for family reasons (teacher)

> "My partner is in the armed forces, so we've moved three times in five years, and each school move followed that. My references from each school will tell you I left on good terms. We've now settled here for at least the next four years, which is why I'm looking for a permanent post."

## Mistakes to avoid

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Explaining every move in detail | Takes two minutes and sounds defensive | Give the thread, then let them ask |
| Blaming bad managers each time | A pattern of blame becomes the story | Own any choice you got wrong |
| "I get bored easily" | Confirms their worry | Talk about what you were building toward |
| Promising to stay forever | Nobody believes it | Give specific reasons this role is different |
| Leaving out contracts or layoffs | Makes your history look worse than it is | Name outside reasons early |

It helps to have your whole history clear in your head before you're asked, because this question often comes after [walk me through your resume](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/walk-me-through-your-resume/). If you can tell your career as one story there, this question becomes easy.

## Questions people ask

### How short is "too short" for a job?

There's no fixed rule, and it varies by industry. Interviewers tend to ask when there are several roles under a year or two in a row. One short role among longer ones rarely needs much explanation.

### Should I leave short jobs off my CV?

That depends on the job and how relevant it is. If you leave one off, don't deny it if asked, and make sure your dates don't mislead. A gap can raise as many questions as a short job.

### What if I left a job after a few weeks?

Say it was a poor fit, in one sentence, and what you learned about choosing roles. "The job turned out to be mostly cold calling, which I hadn't asked enough about. I ask about a typical day now."

### Is job hopping still a problem?

Many employers are used to shorter tenures than in the past, especially in tech and in early careers. But you'll still be asked, so prepare the answer whatever your field.

### Can I turn the question around?

Lightly. Asking "What does a typical career path look like here?" near the end of the interview reinforces that you're thinking long term.
