## Why interviewers ask about hobbies

This is usually a lighter moment. The interviewer wants to see you relax and talk like a person. Sometimes they are also checking for a few things: whether you have a life that keeps you balanced, whether you stick with things, and whether you'd be easy to work beside.

They are not looking for the "right" hobby. Nobody is hired because they play chess. They are listening for whether you can describe something you care about with some energy and detail.

## How to choose which hobbies to mention

Most people have more than two. Choose with these questions:

1. **Do I actually do it?** Something from the last few months, not something you did at school.
2. **Can I give a specific detail?** A distance, a dish, a project, a team, a number.
3. **Would I enjoy two follow-up questions about it?** If not, drop it.
4. **Is it safe for a stranger at work to hear?** See the list of risky hobbies below.

You don't need to make the hobby sound useful for the job. A light link is fine if it is real, but most strong answers just show you enjoy something and take it seriously.

| Hobby type | What it can quietly show | Detail that makes it land |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Team sport | Commitment, working with others | "Sunday league, I've played centre-back for the same team for five years" |
| Endurance sport | Discipline, long-term effort | "Training for my second 10K, aiming to beat 55 minutes" |
| Making things (cooking, woodwork, knitting) | Patience, attention to detail | "I'm working through regional curries, one new one every weekend" |
| Music or performing | Practice, nerve in front of people | "I play keyboard in a covers band, we do about one gig a month" |
| Volunteering | Reliability, care for others | "I tutor maths at a community centre on Saturday mornings" |
| Games and puzzles | Problem solving, strategy | "I play online chess, mostly rapid games, and review my losses" |
| Learning a language | Curiosity, steady effort | "I'm learning Japanese, about 20 minutes a day for a year now" |
| Reading | Curiosity, depth | Name the last book and one thing you took from it |

## How much detail to give

The right length is about 30 to 45 seconds, roughly 70 to 110 spoken words. A useful shape:

- **What** you do. One sentence.
- **How** you do it, with one specific detail. One or two sentences.
- **Why** you like it. One sentence.

Then stop and let them ask more. If they are interested, they will. If they move on, you haven't taken up time they wanted for other questions.

Too little ("I like reading and hiking") sounds evasive or bored. Too much (a five-minute account of your fantasy football league) makes them wonder about your sense of the room.

## Example answers

These are made-up examples of the shape. Use your own hobbies.

### Short and warm

> "I bake bread, mostly sourdough. I started during a long winter and got a bit obsessed, I keep notes on every loaf. What I like is that it's slow, and you can't rush it, which is a nice change from my day."

### With a light link to the job

> "I referee youth football on Saturday mornings. You make maybe a hundred decisions in an hour, and someone's always unhappy with one of them. It's taught me to make a call, explain it calmly and move on, which honestly helps in customer-facing work too."

### For someone who thinks they have no hobbies

> "Honestly, with a young child my free time is limited, but my one thing is a weekly walk with two friends, about ten kilometres on a Sunday morning. We've done most of the trails near the city now. It's the time I do my best thinking."

## Hobbies that can backfire

| Hobby or answer | The risk | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Netflix and hanging out with friends" | Sounds like you have nothing to say | Pick something more specific, or name the show and why you like it |
| Political or religious activity | Invites judgement and debate | Keep it for people who know you |
| Gambling or heavy drinking culture | Raises concerns about judgement | Leave it out |
| Dangerous sports, mentioned with injury stories | Can make them think about sick days | Mention the sport, skip the hospital stories |
| A hobby that takes lots of weekday time | Can sound like it competes with the job | Talk about the activity, not the hours |
| A hobby you invented for the interview | Collapses under one follow-up | Only mention what you really do |
| Saying you read, without a recent book | "What are you reading now?" is the next question | Have a title ready, see [the last book you read](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/what-is-the-last-book-you-read/) |

## Be ready for the follow-up

Expect "How did you get into that?", "What's the hardest part?" or "What's next with it?" Have one small story ready, such as the first time you tried it, or a moment that went wrong. The same story can carry a related question, [tell me something that's not on your resume](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/tell-me-something-not-on-your-resume/).

Saying your answer out loud matters here, because hobby answers either come out flat or ramble. A two-minute run with someone who asks one follow-up is enough. In Speaking Coach the interviewer follows up on what you actually said, so you get used to the second question, not just the first.

## Questions people ask

### Do I have to have hobbies?

No. You can talk about how you spend free time, even if it is simple: walking, cooking for family, a weekly game with friends. Specific and honest beats impressive.

### Should my hobbies relate to the job?

They don't have to. A link can help if it is natural, but a forced one ("I do puzzles, which is why I'm great at analysis") can sound rehearsed.

### Is it okay to say I play video games?

Yes, if you describe it the way you would any other hobby: which games, what you enjoy, maybe a team or community aspect. Avoid making it sound like it takes all your free time.

### Should I list hobbies on my resume too?

Only if there is space and they are specific. If they are on the CV, expect to be asked about them.

### What if the interviewer shares my hobby?

Enjoy the moment, but keep it short and let them steer back to the interview. A minute of shared interest is good. Ten minutes may push out questions they needed to ask.
