## What this question is really testing

This is a behavioural question: the interviewer believes how you handled something hard in the past predicts how you'll handle it in their job. They're listening for:

- **What you count as a challenge.** It tells them your level of experience.
- **What you did, personally.** Not the team, not your manager.
- **How you think under difficulty.** Did you plan, ask for help, adapt?
- **Whether it worked.** A result, ideally with a number.

## Choose a challenge of the right size

The most common mistake isn't the telling; it's the choice. Too small and it sounds trivial. Too big and you were a passenger, or it raises worries.

| Too small | Right size | Too big or risky |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "My laptop broke before a deadline." | "Two of our five pickers quit during peak season and I had to keep orders going out." | "The company nearly went bankrupt." (What did you do?) |
| "A customer was a bit rude." | "Our biggest client threatened to leave after three late deliveries." | A conflict that ended with someone fired |
| "I had a lot of emails." | "I had to learn a new booking system in a week and train the team on it." | A personal tragedy or health crisis |
| "Group project was boring." | "A team member dropped out of our final project two weeks before the deadline." | Anything involving safety incidents, legal trouble or broken rules |

Good sources of challenges: a deadline with missing people, a broken process, a difficult customer, a technical problem, learning something fast, a change you didn't choose.

## Tell it with STAR, and time it

[STAR](/speaking-coach/guides/star-method/) gives you a shape. The timing matters as much as the order.

| Part | What to say | Time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Situation | Where, when, and what made it hard | 10–15 seconds |
| Task | What you were responsible for | 5–10 seconds |
| Action | The steps you took, in order, with "I" | 40–60 seconds |
| Result | What changed, with a number if you have one, and what you learned | 15–20 seconds |

That's 70–105 seconds in total. If your situation takes longer than your action, cut the background.

## Making the result measurable

Interviewers remember numbers. Look for any of these:

- **Time:** "We got it done two days early," "cut the wait from 20 minutes to 8."
- **Money:** "saved about 3,000 a month in overtime."
- **Volume:** "kept 98 out of 100 orders on time."
- **Errors:** "complaints dropped from twelve a week to three."
- **People:** "trained all 14 staff," "the client renewed for another two years."

If you don't have exact numbers, give an honest estimate ("about half") or a clear before-and-after ("we went from missing the weekly deadline to hitting it every week for the rest of the year").

## Example answers

Made-up examples to show the structure. Use your own story and real numbers.

### Warehouse supervisor: short-staffed at peak

> **Situation:** "Last November, two of the five pickers on my shift left in the same week, right at the start of our busiest period."
>
> **Task:** "I was responsible for getting every order out by the 6 pm courier collection."
>
> **Action:** "First I looked at which orders actually had to go that day and which could go the next morning without breaking a promise to customers, and I got my manager to agree. Then I reorganised the pick routes so the three remaining pickers weren't crossing each other, which saved a lot of walking. I asked two people from goods-in to cover the last hour of each shift, and I picked orders myself during the peak. Meanwhile I worked with HR to get two agency staff in by the second week."
>
> **Result:** "We missed the courier only once in four weeks, and that day we got the orders out first thing the next morning. The new pick routes worked so well that we kept them after the agency staff left."

### Graduate: a team member dropped out

> "In my final year, one of the four people in our group project left the course two weeks before the deadline. I was the one who'd been keeping our project plan, so I took responsibility for sorting it out. I split her section into three smaller pieces, matched each to what the rest of us were already doing, and emailed our tutor the same day to explain the change, so there were no surprises. I also took on the data analysis myself, since that was the part closest to mine. We submitted on time and got the second-highest mark in the year. What I learned is to tell people early when plans change."

## Follow-up questions to prepare

Behavioural interviewers almost always dig deeper. Have a sentence ready for each:

- "What would you do differently?"
- "What did your manager or team do?"
- "What was the hardest part for you personally?"
- "How did you know it had worked?"
- "Has anything like it happened since?"

## Mistakes to avoid

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Try instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "We" all the way through | They can't tell what you did | Say "I" for your actions, "we" for the team's |
| A long setup | You run out of time before the action | Keep situation and task under 25 seconds |
| No result | The story just stops | End with what changed, even if imperfect |
| Blaming others for the challenge | Sounds like you're dodging | Describe the problem neutrally |
| A challenge you didn't solve | Leaves them worried | Use the [failure question](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/tell-me-about-a-time-you-failed/) for that story instead |

## How to practise it

Write your story as four lines, one per STAR part, then tell it out loud and time it. Most people discover their situation runs long and their result is one rushed sentence. Practise the follow-ups too, out loud. Speaking Coach's AI interviewer asks follow-ups based on what you actually said, and after the session shows tips that quote your words, so you can see where the story lost focus.

## Questions people ask

### What's a good example of a challenge for an interview?

One that was hard but solvable, where you personally acted and got a result: a deadline with too few people, a broken process, a difficult client, or learning something new fast.

### Can I use a challenge from university or my personal life?

From university, yes, especially if you're early in your career. Personal challenges are riskier; use them only if they show a work-relevant skill and don't involve sensitive details.

### How long should a STAR answer be?

About 90 seconds to two minutes. Shorter feels thin; longer and the interviewer loses the thread.

### What if my challenge didn't have a happy ending?

Then it's a better fit for the failure question. For "a challenge you faced", choose one where your actions improved things, even if not perfectly.

### Is "a challenge you faced" the same as "a problem you solved"?

Similar, but a challenge can be a situation you managed, like short staffing, while a [problem you solved](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/tell-me-about-a-time-you-solved-a-problem/) is usually something you diagnosed and fixed.
