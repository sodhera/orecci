## What makes this question tricky

The obvious answer is a story about staying late or working through a weekend. That can backfire. It may suggest you can't manage your time, that you'll burn out, or that you'll expect others to do the same. Some interviewers also hear "I did something nobody asked for" as "I ignore what I'm told".

A strong answer avoids all of that. It shows **extra effort with a purpose**: you noticed a need nobody had asked you to meet, you met it sensibly, and the result mattered to someone.

## What "above and beyond" can look like

Effort isn't only time. The best stories often involve noticing something.

| Type of extra effort | Example | Why it works |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Noticing what others missed | Spotted a billing error that affected 40 customers | Shows attention and care |
| Going further for one person | Called a customer back after their issue was "closed" | Shows ownership of the outcome |
| Building something reusable | Wrote a guide so the next person wouldn't struggle | Benefit lasts beyond the moment |
| Learning a skill to solve a problem | Learned basic SQL to pull data the team kept waiting for | Shows initiative and growth |
| Covering a gap in a crisis | Stepped in when a colleague was suddenly off | Shows reliability |
| Small touches that mattered | Remembered a regular's allergy and told the kitchen | Shows care, works for any level |

## The structure: need, extra step, result

1. **The normal expectation (one sentence).** What your job required. This makes the "extra" visible.
2. **The need you noticed (one or two sentences).** What was going wrong or missing.
3. **What you did beyond the expectation (30–40 seconds).** The steps, including any check with your manager. Mention that your regular work was covered.
4. **The result (15 seconds).** What changed, who noticed, ideally with a number.

About 75–90 seconds in total, roughly 180–220 words.

## Example answers

These are examples with made-up details. Use your own story.

### Example answer for a hotel or hospitality role

> "My job on the front desk was check-ins and check-outs. One evening a guest arrived for a job interview the next morning and realised his suit had been crushed in his bag. Our laundry service had closed for the day. My shift had another hour, and once the check-in rush was over and my colleague could cover the desk for 15 minutes, I asked my manager if I could take the suit to the dry cleaner two streets away, which offered a same-night service. I picked it up on my way home and left it at the desk. The guest emailed the hotel afterwards to say he got the job, and my manager added a list of late-night local services to our front desk folder so anyone could help the next guest."

### Example answer for an office or admin role

> "As an administrator, my job was processing supplier invoices. While doing that, I noticed we were paying two different prices to the same supplier for the same item, depending on which team ordered. It wasn't my area, but I pulled together six months of invoices in a spreadsheet during quiet afternoons and showed my manager. She took it to purchasing, who renegotiated a single price. The savings in the first year were significant enough that I was asked to check two other suppliers the same way."

### Example answer for a software or technical role

> "I was assigned a bug fix in our booking system. While fixing it, I noticed the same error message was confusing customers in three other places, and support had a pile of tickets about it. I finished my fix first, then asked my lead if I could spend half a day rewriting those messages. She agreed. I worked with someone from support on the wording. Tickets on that topic dropped by about half over the next month."

### Example answer for a graduate or student

> "At my campus library job, I was expected to reshelve books and help at the desk. I noticed international students kept asking the same questions about the printing system, which was confusing. I wrote a one-page guide with screenshots in my free time and asked my supervisor if we could put it by the printers. She had it translated into three languages, and desk questions about printing dropped noticeably that term."

## Mistakes to avoid

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Try instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "I worked 80 hours that week" | Suggests poor boundaries or planning | Focus on what you noticed and did |
| Neglecting your own job to do it | Raises reliability concerns | Show your normal work was covered |
| Going against instructions | Sounds like you don't follow process | Mention who you checked with |
| A story that's just your job done well | Not "beyond" anything | Say what was expected, then what you added |
| No result | Effort without impact is just busyness | End with what changed |
| Implying others didn't care | Sounds critical of your team | Describe the gap, not the people |

## When they follow up on boundaries

A thoughtful interviewer may ask, "Do you do that often?" or "How do you know when to stop?" A good reply:

> "When I see something that affects customers or the team and I can fix it without dropping my own work, I'll usually offer. If it's bigger than that, I raise it with my manager rather than taking it on alone."

This shows you care, and that you won't burn out or step on toes. If the conversation turns to hours, see [are you willing to work overtime?](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/are-you-willing-to-work-overtime/)

## Practise saying the "extra" clearly

In this answer, the line between your normal job and the extra step is what makes the story. When you practise out loud, check that the listener can hear that line: "My job was X. What I noticed was Y. So I did Z." Speaking Coach's interviewer can follow up with "Did your other work suffer?", which is the question that tests whether you've set the boundary clearly.

## Questions people ask

### Is staying late a good example?

Only if the late hours solved a specific problem and weren't a regular pattern. Even then, lead with what you noticed and achieved, not the hours.

### What if I haven't done anything dramatic?

Small, thoughtful actions count: a guide you wrote, a customer you followed up with, a problem you noticed. Care and judgement matter more than scale.

### How is this different from taking initiative?

Initiative is about starting something without being asked. Going above and beyond is about doing more than expected. The stories can overlap, but prepare separate ones. See [took initiative](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/tell-me-about-a-time-you-took-initiative/).

### Should I mention who thanked me?

Yes, briefly. A manager, customer or colleague noticing is good evidence that it mattered.

### Can I use a volunteering example?

Yes, especially early in your career, as long as there's a clear expectation, an extra step and a result.
