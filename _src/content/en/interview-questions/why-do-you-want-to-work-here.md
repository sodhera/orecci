## What a good answer proves

The interviewer is checking whether you chose them or just applied everywhere. Both are normal, but people who have a real reason to want this employer tend to stay longer and settle in faster. Your answer should show:

- You know what the company actually does, beyond its slogan.
- Something about it genuinely connects to your experience or your goals.
- You'd be glad to be there, not just employed.

The test: if you could swap in a competitor's name and your answer still worked, it's too generic.

## What to research (and where)

You don't need hours. You need two or three specific hooks.

| Where to look | What you're looking for |
| --- | --- |
| The company's own site: about, products, customers | What they sell or do, and for whom, in one sentence |
| News or press releases from the last six months | A launch, expansion, contract, merger or new direction |
| The job post itself | Words they repeat; what they say about the team |
| Their careers page or team pages | How they describe their values, with examples, not just the words |
| Their product, shop or service, used yourself | A first-hand detail nobody else will mention |
| Reviews from customers | What customers praise or complain about |

For a step-by-step version with timings, see [what do you know about our company](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/what-do-you-know-about-our-company/).

## The three-part link

Each reason should follow this pattern:

1. **What you noticed:** a specific fact about the company.
2. **Why it matters to you:** connected to your past work, your values or your goals.
3. **What you'd do with it:** how it shows up in the role.

Two of these, spoken, take about 45–60 seconds. That's enough.

## Generic praise vs. a real reason

| Generic | Specific |
| --- | --- |
| "You're a leader in the industry." | "You're the only one of the big three still manufacturing in the UK, and I've spent four years in supply chains where that kind of control was the difference between on-time and late." |
| "I love your culture." | "Your engineering blog talks about how every new hire ships something in the first week. That's how I learn best." |
| "It's a great company to work for." | "You moved all your customer support in-house last year. I've worked in outsourced support and I've seen how much better it is when the team owns the customer." |
| "I've always admired your brand." | "I've used your app for budgeting for two years, and the thing I'd change is the onboarding, which is exactly what this team works on." |

## Example answers by kind of employer

These are made-up examples of the method. Use real details from the company you're interviewing with.

### A large public-facing organisation (hospital network, admin role)

> "Two things. First, you've just opened the new outpatient centre, which I read means a lot of new booking processes being set up at once. I spent three years in a busy GP surgery handling appointments, so I know where those processes break and how much it matters to patients. Second, I like that your admin staff are part of the patient experience training. I want to be somewhere that treats the front desk as part of care, not just paperwork."

### A startup (customer success role)

> "I've been following your product since you launched your free plan last year. What stands out is that your customers are mostly small accountancy firms, which is who I've supported for the last three years at my current job. I know their busy seasons and what they panic about in January. And at your size, I'd be helping shape how customer success works, rather than following a playbook someone else wrote."

### A small local business (family-run bakery chain, shift supervisor)

> "I've been a customer at your high street shop for years, and what I notice is that the staff know the regulars by name, even at the morning rush. That's how I've tried to run my shifts at the coffee shop I'm at now. I also saw you're opening a third shop next spring, and I'd like to grow with a business that's growing, and help train the new team."

### A non-profit (programme coordinator)

> "Your literacy programme works with adults, not just children, and that's rare. My mother learned to read properly in her forties, so I know how much it changes someone's life. On the practical side, I've run volunteer schedules for a food bank for two years, so I'm comfortable keeping 50 volunteers organised. I want to do that work full-time for a cause I care about."

## Mistakes to avoid

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Try instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Leading with salary, benefits or location | Sounds like any job would do | Mention them only if asked, and never first |
| Pure flattery | Shows no research and no connection to you | Pair every compliment with why it matters to you |
| Getting a fact wrong | Worse than no fact | Use only details you checked on their own site or recent news |
| Talking only about what you'll gain | Feels one-sided | Include what you'd contribute |
| Reciting their mission statement | They wrote it; they don't need it read back | Say what the mission looks like in practice and why it fits you |

## How this differs from "Why do you want this job?"

"Why here?" is about the employer. "[Why this job?](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/why-do-you-want-this-job/)" is about the work you'd do every day. Interviewers sometimes ask both, or one question that covers both. Prepare one company reason and one role reason, and you can answer either version.

## How to practise it

Say your answer out loud and listen for phrases that would fit any company. Replace each one with a detail. Then practise with a follow-up: a good interviewer will ask "What else did you look at?" or "Which of our products do you know?" Speaking Coach's AI interviewer follows up on what you actually said, and in a mock interview you can add the job post so it asks about this company and role.

## Questions people ask

### What if I honestly just need a job?

That's fine, and common. Find one or two real things about this company that you'd genuinely prefer over others, even small ones like the team size or the kind of customers. Lead with those.

### What if the company is hard to research?

Use what you can: the job post, their shop or product, customer reviews, and the people you meet in the process. You can also say what you noticed during the interview itself.

### How long should my answer be?

About 45–60 seconds: two reasons, each with a link to you. Then stop and let them respond.

### Can I mention pay or benefits?

Not as your main reason. If they have a standout benefit like training budget or a four-day week, you can mention it as a secondary point, linked to how it helps you do good work.

### Should I mention people I know who work there?

Yes, briefly, if they've told you something specific about the work. "My friend on the data team says your releases are planned, not rushed" is a real reason.
