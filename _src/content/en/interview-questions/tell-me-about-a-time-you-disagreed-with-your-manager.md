## What the interviewer wants to find out

This question has two traps, and the interviewer is watching for both.

- **The yes-person.** "I've never really disagreed with my manager" sounds like you either don't think for yourself or won't tell the truth. Every manager wants people who will say "I think this is a mistake" before it costs money.
- **The rebel.** A story where you went around your boss, complained to the team, or quietly did it your own way tells them you'll be hard to manage.

The answer they hope for sits in between: you raised it the right way, and then you backed the decision.

## The four moves of a good upward disagreement

Your story should show each of these, in this order.

1. **Private and early.** You raised it one-to-one, or in a way that didn't embarrass your manager, and before the work was locked in.
2. **Evidence, not opinion.** Numbers, customer feedback, a past result, a risk you could point to. "I think" is weaker than "last time we did this, 12 orders were delayed."
3. **An alternative.** You didn't just say no. You offered a different option or a smaller test.
4. **Commit.** Once your manager decided, you put full effort behind it, even if they didn't take your view.

The fourth move is the one most people leave out, and it's the one that matters most. Interviewers often follow up with "And what if they'd said no?" Have that answer ready.

## Three possible endings, and how to tell each

Not every disagreement ends with you being right. All three endings can make a strong answer.

| How it ended | What to stress | Closing line to use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Manager changed their mind | How you made it easy for them to say yes | "She took the smaller version, and it worked, so we expanded it." |
| Manager kept their plan, and it worked | That you committed and learned something | "I was wrong about the timing, and I learned how she reads the client." |
| Manager kept their plan, and your concern came true | That you helped fix it without saying "I told you so" | "When the issue came up, I already had the backup ready, and we sorted it in a day." |

The third ending is the riskiest. Tell it without any hint of triumph. Focus on how you helped, not on being right.

## Example answers

The details below are invented to show the shape. Use your own story.

### Pushing back on a deadline (project coordinator)

> "My manager wanted to move a client launch forward by two weeks to impress them before the contract renewal. I thought it would mean skipping user testing, and on our last project skipped testing led to three weeks of fixes. I asked for 15 minutes with her that afternoon rather than raising it in the team meeting. I showed her the bug list from last time and suggested a middle option: launch early to the client's internal team only, then go public on the original date. She agreed. The internal launch caught two serious bugs, and the public launch went out clean. The client renewed."

### When the manager said no (retail supervisor)

> "Our store manager decided to cut the Saturday morning shift from four people to three. I disagreed, because Saturday mornings were when we had the most returns, and I'd tracked queue times for a month that showed it. I took him the numbers and suggested cutting a Tuesday shift instead. He listened, but head office had set the Saturday cut, so it stayed. I said I understood and then reorganised the Saturday tasks so one person handled returns only. Queues stayed under ten minutes most weeks. I also learned to ask earlier where a decision came from before building my case."

### A technical disagreement (software engineer)

> "My lead wanted to build our own scheduling system rather than use an existing library. I thought the library would save us about a month. I wrote a one-page comparison with the time estimates and the risks on both sides, and sent it to him before our planning meeting so he wasn't surprised. He read it and still chose to build, because he wanted full control over a feature the library didn't have. That was a fair reason I hadn't weighed enough. I took on the hardest part of the build and we shipped it in six weeks."

## Mistakes to avoid

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "My boss was just wrong" | You sound like you'll criticise this manager too | Describe the decision, not the person |
| Disagreeing in front of the team | Signals poor judgment about timing | Show you chose a private moment |
| Going over their head | Suggests you'll go around your next boss | Only mention escalation if it was about safety, ethics or law |
| A trivial disagreement (font choice) | It doesn't show anything | Pick something with real consequences |
| No commitment at the end | The interviewer worries you'll sulk | Say what you did after the decision |

## Practising the tone

The words in this answer matter less than how you sound saying them. Any edge in your voice when you describe your manager will be heard. Say the answer out loud and listen for it: does the manager sound like a reasonable person you worked with, or like an obstacle? Speaking Coach has a *Disagree in a meeting* scene where an AI partner plays the other side and pushes back, which is useful for hearing how your disagreement lands before you tell the story in an interview.

## Questions people ask

### What if I really have never disagreed with a manager?

Think smaller: a process, a priority, a deadline, a tool. Most people have suggested a different approach at least once. If you're a graduate, a disagreement with a professor or a placement supervisor works.

### Can I say I was right?

You can say what happened. Let the facts show you were right, and don't say "I told you so" in any form. Interviewers notice the difference.

### Should I name my former manager?

No. "My manager" or "my team lead" is enough. Naming them adds nothing and can sound personal.

### What if the disagreement was about something unethical?

That's a different and serious story. It's fine to tell, but keep it calm and factual, and say how you escalated through proper channels. The page on [ethical dilemmas](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/tell-me-about-a-time-you-faced-an-ethical-dilemma/) covers it.

### How is this different from a conflict question?

A [conflict question](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/tell-me-about-a-time-you-had-a-conflict/) is usually about a peer and how you repaired the relationship. A disagreement with your boss adds a power gap, so it's about how you raise a view upward and then accept the decision.
