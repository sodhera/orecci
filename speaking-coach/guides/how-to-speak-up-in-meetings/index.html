How to Speak Up in Meetings When You're Quiet or New

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

To speak up in meetings, prepare one point before you walk in, say something in the first ten minutes, and use a short starter like "Can I add one thing?" to get in. Keep your contribution under 30 seconds: your point, one reason, and a question or next step. The longer you wait, the harder it gets.

Why it gets harder the longer you wait

The first time you speak in a meeting is the hardest. Every minute you stay quiet, the bar in your head rises: now your first comment has to be really good to justify the silence. So the most useful rule is simple: say something early, even if it's small. Once you've spoken once, speaking again is much easier.

Prepare one point beforehand

You don't need to be quick on your feet if you arrive with something ready.

  1. Read the agenda or invite. If there isn't one, ask the organizer: "Anything you'd like me to think about before Thursday?"
  2. Pick one topic where you know something. Your own work, a customer you spoke to, a number you've seen.
  3. Write one sentence. "I think we should test the new form with five customers before launch, because two people on support raised the same confusion last week."
  4. Say it out loud once. Hearing it before the meeting makes it much easier to say in the room.
  5. Note one question too. A good question is a full contribution, especially when you're new.

On video calls, use the raise-hand button or unmute and say the person's name: "Sam, could I add something here?" Typing your point in the chat also counts if speaking feels too big today, but aim to say it next time.

Sentence starters that get you in

Short starters give you a way in without having to compose your whole point first. Keep a few in your notes.

You want toSay
Add to someone's idea"Building on what Lena said…"
Agree with substance"I agree, and one reason is…"
Ask a question"Can I ask a quick question about the timeline?"
Disagree gently"I see it a bit differently. My concern is…"
Share a fact"One thing I've seen on the support side…"
Summarize"So it sounds like we're agreeing to… Is that right?"
Bring someone in"Priya, you worked on this last year, what do you think?"

Summarizing is underrated, especially for new people. It shows you're following closely, helps the group and doesn't require you to have an opinion yet.

How to interrupt politely

In fast meetings, waiting for a perfect gap can mean never speaking. A polite interruption is fine if it's short and you hand the floor back.

  • Wait for the end of a sentence, not the middle of one.
  • Use the person's name and a soft signal: "Sorry, Mark, can I just add one thing on that?"
  • Make your point in one or two sentences.
  • Hand it back: "Go ahead, sorry to cut in."

If someone interrupts you, you can hold your place calmly: "Let me just finish this point, then I'd love to hear yours." More lines are in how to politely interrupt.

Keep it short

Quiet people often worry they'll sound unprepared, then over-explain once they start. Aim for under 30 seconds:

Example, in a planning meeting: "I'd suggest we move the launch a week. Support is still getting questions about the new form, and a week gives us time to fix the wording. Could we decide that by Friday?"

That's a point, a reason and a next step. The PREP method is a reliable shape for this, and the rambling guide helps if you tend to keep going.

If you're new

  • Your first contributions can be questions: "How was that decided last time?" New people asking good questions is expected.
  • Say what you're seeing with fresh eyes: "Coming in new, one thing I noticed was…"
  • Follow up in writing after the meeting if you thought of something later. It still counts.
  • Ask your manager what they'd like you to contribute in team meetings. Then you know the bar.

Questions people ask

How often should I speak in a meeting?

There's no set number. Aim for at least one useful contribution in each meeting you're invited to. Quality matters more than quantity.

What if someone already said my point?

Agree and add something specific: "I agree with Sam, and the customer feedback backs it up." Supporting with evidence is a real contribution.

How do I speak up when one person dominates?

Use their name and a short signal at the end of their sentence, or ask the chair: "Could we hear from a few more people on this?" Raise-hand features help on video calls.

Is it okay to read my point from notes?

Yes. Glancing at a one-line note is normal. Say it in your own words rather than reading a long paragraph.

How do I stop my voice from shaking when I speak up?

Take a breath before you start, speak your first sentence slowly, and keep it short. It gets easier the more often you speak early in meetings.
