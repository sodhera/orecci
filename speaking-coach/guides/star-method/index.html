STAR Method: How to Answer Behavioral Interview Questions

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

STAR stands for Situation, Task, Action, Result. Spend about 15 seconds setting the scene, 10 on what you had to do, 60 on what you personally did, and 20 on the result and what you learned, for an answer of 90 seconds to two minutes. Most of the time belongs to the Action.

How long each part should take

Behavioral questions start with "Tell me about a time…" or "Give me an example of…". The interviewer wants one real story, told so they can follow it and judge what you did. STAR gives the story a shape. The timing is where most people go wrong.

PartWhat it answersSecondsRough words
SituationWhere were you, and what was going on?10–2025–50
TaskWhat were you responsible for, or what was the problem?5–1515–35
ActionWhat did you do, step by step?45–70110–170
ResultWhat happened, ideally with a number, and what did you learn?15–2535–60

That adds up to roughly 90 seconds to two minutes, or 200–300 spoken words. If your Situation is longer than your Action, the answer is upside down.

Build an answer in six steps

  1. Pick the story before the interview. Write down five or six stories from work, study or volunteering: a problem you fixed, a conflict, a deadline, a mistake, a time you led. One good story can answer several questions.
  2. Write the Result first. Knowing where the story ends stops you wandering. If you can't name a result, pick another story.
  3. Cut the Situation to two sentences. Who, where, and the one fact that made it hard.
  4. State the Task in one sentence. "My job was to…" or "The problem I had to solve was…"
  5. List three or four actions as verbs. "I called… I rebuilt… I asked… I tested…" Use "I", not "we". The interviewer is hiring you, not your team.
  6. Say it out loud and time it. Writing a STAR answer and saying one are different. Your first spoken try will probably run long; trim it until it fits.

A worked example, from rough to ready

Here is how one story changes as you shape it. The details are made up for illustration.

The rough version (what most people say first, about 3 minutes):

"So this was at my last job, I was in operations at a mid-sized online furniture company, we'd been growing a lot, and there were lots of changes going on with management, and the warehouse team had a new system, and basically customers were complaining about late deliveries, and we had a meeting about it, and then we kind of looked into it, and it turned out…"

Ninety seconds in, the interviewer still doesn't know what you did.

The STAR version (about 100 seconds):

Situation: "At my last job in operations at an online furniture retailer, late-delivery complaints had doubled in two months."

Task: "My manager asked me to find the cause and fix it before our busy season, which was six weeks away."

Action: "I pulled three months of order data and matched each late order to the warehouse it shipped from. Almost all of them came from one site, which had switched to a new picking system. I spent two days there watching the shift and found that large items were being queued behind small ones. I worked with the shift lead to split large items into their own queue, and I set up a daily report so we would see delays the same day instead of a week later."

Result: "Within four weeks late deliveries from that site were back to normal, and we went into the busy season without a backlog. What I took from it is to go and watch the work before guessing at the cause."

Notice what changed: the company history went, the "we" became "I", and the Action got the most words.

Mistakes that make STAR answers run long

MistakeWhat it sounds likeFix
Too much backgroundTwo minutes on the company, the team, the reorgTwo sentences of Situation, then move on
Telling it in order of events"And then… and then… and then…"Group into three or four actions, each starting with a verb
"We" all the way through"We decided, we built, we launched"Say "I" for your part; mention the team once
No clear resultThe story just stopsPlan the last line before you start
Explaining every detailTool names, dates, side charactersKeep only what the listener needs to follow
Adding a second story"Another time, something similar…"One story per question; offer more if they ask
Starting with "So, um…"A slow, uncertain openingOpen with the Situation sentence you prepared

If your answers keep running long, try the shorter CAR method, which folds Situation and Task into one Context line.

How to land the Result

The Result is the part interviewers remember, and the part people rush. Three things make it work:

  • A number, if you have a real one. Time saved, complaints down, money made, people trained. Never invent one; "the client renewed" is fine without a figure.
  • What changed for others. "The team stopped working weekends" says more than "it went well".
  • One line of learning. Especially for failure and mistake questions, where what you learned is the result.

Then stop talking. A clean stop invites a follow-up question, and follow-ups are where good interviews happen.

Practice it out loud

Say each of your stories out loud at least three times, timing each one. Check your speed with the pace checker or run a full answer through the interview answer review, which shows your words per minute and filler words.

The harder test is the follow-up: "What would you do differently?" or "What did your manager say?" In the Speaking Coach app, the interviewer follows up on what you actually said, so you find out which parts of your story are thin before a real interviewer does.

Questions people ask

How long should a STAR answer be?

About 90 seconds to two minutes, or 200–300 words spoken. Under a minute usually skips the Action; over three minutes loses the listener.

Can I use a story from school or volunteering?

Yes. If you have little work experience, a group project, a part-time job, a club or caring for a family member all count. What matters is that you can describe what you did.

What if my story didn't have a happy ending?

Use it anyway for questions about failure or mistakes. Make the Result about what you learned and what you now do differently.

Should I say "Situation", "Task" and so on out loud?

No. Use the structure silently. Natural links like "the problem was" and "so what I did was" do the same job.

How many STAR stories should I prepare?

Five or six strong ones. Most behavioral questions, from conflict to leadership to deadlines, can be answered from that set with a different emphasis.
