How to End a Presentation Strongly (Not With Any Questions)

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

End a presentation with a short summary of your main message, a clear ask (what you want the audience to do, decide or remember), and a final line you've written in advance. Take questions before that final line, not after, so the last words people hear are yours rather than the last question or 'so, yeah, that's it'.

Why "that's it" and "any questions?" fall flat

Audiences remember the end of a talk more than most of the middle. Yet many presentations end like this:

"…and that's the last slide. So, yeah, that's it really. Any questions?"

Three problems in one line. "That's it" sounds like you ran out rather than finished. "So, yeah" drains energy just when you want it highest. And "Any questions?" hands the final moment to whoever speaks next, which might be the person with the awkward question, or no one at all, leaving you in silence.

The fix is to plan the last minute as carefully as the first.

The summary-plus-ask closing

A strong close has three parts and takes 30 to 90 seconds, depending on the length of the talk.

PartWhat it doesExample line
SignalTells people you're wrapping up, so attention comes back"Let me bring this together."
SummaryYour main message, in one or two sentences, not a list of every slide"Our support queue doubled because of three onboarding gaps, and all three are fixable in a month."
AskWhat you want them to do, decide or remember"I'm asking for two people from product for four weeks to fix them."

Then your final line. More on that below.

The ask is the part people skip. Every presentation should ask for something, even if it's small: approve this, try this, remember this, talk to me after. Without it, the audience nods and forgets. See call to action.

Where Q&A goes

Put questions between your summary and your final line, not after it.

  1. Signal and summary.
  2. "Before I finish, what questions do you have?"
  3. Questions and answers.
  4. "Thanks for those. To bring it back to where we started…" and your ask and final line.

This way the room hears your message twice, and the last thing they hear is your close, not the tail end of a tangent. If time runs out during questions, you can still deliver a 15-second final line.

Ask "What questions do you have?" rather than "Any questions?". It assumes there are some, which makes people more likely to ask. Then wait a full five seconds before filling the silence. For handling the questions themselves, see how to handle Q&A.

Example last lines

These are made-up examples to show the kinds of endings that work. Your final line should be one or two sentences, said slowly, followed by a pause before "thank you".

SituationFinal line
Proposal to managers"If we start on Monday, the queue is back to normal before the busy season. I'd like a yes by Friday."
Team update"So: on track for launch, one risk on payments, and I need Ana's sign-off by Wednesday. That's the one thing to remember."
Class presentation"Next time you buy a bottle of water, think about where the plastic goes. Now you know."
Sales or client pitch"You told us onboarding takes your team three weeks. We'd like to get it to three days. Let's pick a pilot team."
Conference talk"Go back to work tomorrow and cancel one meeting. Just one. See what happens."
Talk that opened with a story"Remember the server that went down on that Monday? It hasn't happened since. That's what this plan is for."

The last example uses a callback: it returns to the opening story or question. It's one of the most satisfying endings because it makes the talk feel complete. If you plan your opening and closing together, a callback is easy to build in. See how to start a speech.

Endings to avoid

EndingProblemInstead
"That's it." / "That's all I've got."Sounds like you ran outSignal, summary, ask
"Sorry, I went over time."Ends on an apologyCut a section and end on time
Reading the last slideYou're talking to the screen at the key momentTurn to the audience; say your final line from memory
A new point at the endConfuses the messageNew points go in the body or nowhere
"Any questions?" as the last wordsHands the ending to someone elseQ&A before the final line
Trailing offVoice drops, nobody knows if you've finishedSay the final line, pause, then "Thank you."

How to deliver the last 30 seconds

  1. Slow down. Most people speed up near the end because they're relieved. Do the opposite.
  2. Step forward or look up from your notes. The final line should be said to people, not read.
  3. Land the last word. Let your voice go down, not up.
  4. Pause for two seconds. Then say "Thank you." The pause tells people the talk is over and gives them a moment before they clap or respond.
  5. Don't move straight to packing up. Stay still for a beat.

Know your final line word for word, even if you speak everything else from notes. If everything goes wrong in the middle, you can still end well.

Questions people ask

How do you end a presentation without saying "any questions"?

Summarise your message, make your ask, then say "Before I finish, what questions do you have?" After Q&A, deliver your final line and say thank you.

Should I say "thank you" at the end of a presentation?

Yes, but after your final line, not instead of it. "Thank you" signals you've finished; it shouldn't be the content of your ending.

How long should the conclusion of a presentation be?

About 10% of your time: 30 seconds for a five-minute talk, one to two minutes for a 20-minute one.

Should I have a final slide?

A simple slide with your main message or ask works better than a "Thank you" or "Questions?" slide. It stays up during Q&A and reminds people of your point.

What if there are no questions?

Wait five seconds. If nobody asks, say "A question I often get is…" and answer it, then go to your final line.
