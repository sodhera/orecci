How to Be Understood Clearly When You Speak With an Accent

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

An accent isn't a problem to fix; being understood is the goal, and it usually depends more on pace, stress and clear word endings than on your accent. Slow down a little, stress the key word in each phrase, finish the last sound of each word, pause between ideas, and spell or write names and numbers. Check understanding with a quick question instead of hoping.

Your accent isn't the problem

Everyone has an accent, including native speakers. Accents carry where you're from and the languages you speak, and many listeners understand a wide range of them without effort. When people do struggle to follow someone, the cause is often not the accent itself but a few things that happen on top of it: speaking very fast, flat stress, dropped word endings, or long runs without pauses.

Those are all things you can adjust without trying to sound like someone else. This guide is about clarity, not accent reduction.

1. Slow down a little, especially for new information

Many non-native speakers speak fast in a second language, sometimes because they're nervous, sometimes to sound fluent. Speed makes any accent harder to follow, because the listener has less time to adjust.

You don't need to speak slowly everywhere. Slow down on:

  • Names, numbers, dates and places
  • Technical terms and acronyms
  • The main point of each answer

A typical conversational range in English is about 120–160 words per minute. If you measure yourself well above that with the pace checker, slowing slightly is the easiest clarity win.

2. Stress the key word in each phrase

English listeners rely heavily on stress, which words sound longer, louder or higher, to find the meaning. If every word gets equal weight, a sentence can be hard to follow even when each word is pronounced well.

"We need the report by Friday." (when)

"We need the report by Friday." (which thing)

Pick the one word in each phrase that carries the new information and give it a little more length and volume. Word stress inside longer words matters too: "de-VEL-op", "PHO-to-graph", "pho-TOG-ra-pher". If you're unsure, a dictionary with audio shows which syllable is stressed.

3. Finish your word endings

Final consonants carry grammar and meaning in English: "plan" vs "planned", "work" vs "works", "can" vs "can't". In many languages words end in vowels or the last sound is soft, so these endings get dropped.

A drill: read a paragraph aloud and exaggerate every final sound, especially -t, -d, -s and -ed. Then read it normally. The exaggerated version trains your mouth; the normal version will keep a bit of it.

4. Speak in chunks, with pauses

Group your words into short phrases of four to eight words, with a brief pause between them. Pauses give listeners time to process, which matters more when the accent is unfamiliar to them.

"Last quarter / our team in Jakarta / handled most of the new accounts. // This quarter / we're adding two people."

5. Make names and numbers easy

These are where most real misunderstandings happen, because there's no context to guess from.

  • Numbers: say them slowly and repeat key ones. "Fifteen, one-five."
  • Teens vs tens: "fifteen" and "fifty" are easily confused. Add the digits: "fifty, five-zero."
  • Names: say your name, then spell it or give a hint. "I'm Siobhan, S-I-O-B-H-A-N, said 'shi-VAWN'."
  • On calls, type it. Put names, dates and figures in the chat while you say them.
  • Use the 24-hour clock or say "in the morning" if times could be misheard.

6. Check understanding, not just hope

Asking "Did you understand?" can feel awkward for both people. These work better:

  • "Let me know if I should go over the dates again."
  • "Just to confirm, we're meeting on the 14th, is that right?"
  • "I'll send that in writing too."

And if someone asks you to repeat, don't say the same thing faster. Say it more slowly, or in different words.

Practice that helps

The most useful practice is speaking with listeners who respond, not reading aloud alone. Record short answers and listen for speed, stress and endings, then try the same answer again. Questions people ask

Should I try to lose my accent?

That's your choice, and it isn't needed to be understood. Most people get further by working on pace, stress, word endings and pauses, which make any accent easier to follow.

Why do people ask me to repeat myself?

It's often speed, stress or a dropped ending rather than the accent itself, and sometimes it's background noise or a poor connection. Slowing down on key words usually solves most of it.

Is it rude if people say they can't understand my accent?

Usually they're just asking for help to follow you. Repeat more slowly or rephrase. If someone mocks your accent, that's about them, not your speaking.

How can I practice speaking English clearly at home?

Record yourself explaining something for one minute, listen for speed and dropped endings, then record again with pauses between ideas. Practice with a partner who asks questions when they miss something.

Does an accent affect job interviews?

Interviewers need to follow your answers, so clarity matters. Speak a little slower than usual, stress your key points and check in if you're on a video call with a poor connection.
