How to Slow Down When You Talk Too Fast, Especially When Nervous

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Measure your pace first: record a minute of speech and count the words, or use a pace checker. Presentations commonly land around 130–150 words per minute and conversation around 120–160. If you're well above that, slow down by adding pauses rather than stretching words: pause at every full stop, break long sentences into short ones, and slow down most on your first sentence and key points.

Step 1: Measure your pace

"You talk too fast" is a feeling. Words per minute is a number you can track. Measure before you change anything.

Two ways to do it:

  1. Use the pace checker. Speak for a minute and it shows your words per minute, or type in a word count and a time.
  2. Count by hand. Record yourself talking for exactly one minute. Transcribe it, or play it back and count the words. That's your words per minute.

Measure three situations, because pace changes with pressure:

SituationHow to test
RelaxedTell a friend about your weekend
ExplainingDescribe what you do at work for one minute
Under pressureAnswer an interview question, or give the opening of a presentation, while recording

Most fast talkers find the third number is much higher than the first. That's the one to work on.

Step 2: Know your target

These are commonly cited ranges for English, not hard rules. Different speakers, languages and styles vary.

ContextTypical range (words per minute)Notes
Everyday conversationabout 120–160Faster is fine with friends who know you
Presentation or talkabout 130–150Slower for big rooms and complex content
Interview answeraim for the middle of the conversational rangeNerves usually push it up
Toast or emotional speechtoward the slower endPauses for laughs and moments matter
Video callsimilar to a talkAudio lag and compression make fast speech harder to follow

If you're 20–30 words per minute above the range under pressure, that's normal nerves and a few drills will fix it. If you're far above it even when relaxed, it's a habit, and it takes a few weeks of practice. More on the measure itself in words per minute.

Why you speed up when nervous

  • Adrenaline. Your body speeds up generally: heart, breath, speech.
  • Wanting it to be over. Part of you is rushing to the end.
  • Fear of silence. Pauses feel like failure, so you fill them.
  • Shallow breathing. Short breaths mean short bursts of fast speech between gasps.

Knowing this helps: slowing down isn't about talking like a slow-motion video. It's about adding pauses and breathing between phrases.

Drills to bring your pace down

Do one of these for five minutes a day. Measure again after a week.

Drill 1: Marked pauses

Take a 150-word paragraph from any article.

  1. Put a / after every phrase where you'd naturally breathe, and a // at every full stop.
  2. Read it out loud. Pause briefly at each /, and for a full second at each //.
  3. Time it. It should take around a minute. If it takes 45 seconds, your pauses are too short.
  4. Read it again without the marks, keeping the same rhythm.

"When I started this job / I thought the hardest part would be the software. // It wasn't. // The hardest part / was getting people to use it."

Drill 2: Sentence breaks

Fast talkers often link everything with "and", "so" and "but", which leaves no natural place to stop. Break the chain.

Fast: "So we launched in March and it went well but then sales dropped in April so we looked at the data and found it was the pricing page."

Broken up: "We launched in March. It went well. Then sales dropped in April. We looked at the data. The problem was the pricing page."

Take a minute of your own recorded speech, write out one long chain, and rewrite it as short sentences. Then say the short version out loud, stopping fully at each full stop.

Drill 3: Slow start

Nerves are highest in the first 30 seconds, so that's where speed is worst. Practice your first sentence at a deliberately slow pace, almost too slow. Your normal pace will pick up naturally after it, but you'll have set a calmer baseline.

Drill 4: Breathe at every full stop

Take a small breath at each full stop, through the nose if you can. You can't speak while breathing in, so this builds in a pause automatically. See how to breathe while speaking.

Drill 5: Emphasis words

Pick one word per sentence that matters most and say it slightly slower and with a little more weight. Stretching one key word per sentence slows the whole sentence without making you sound sluggish.

What not to do

MistakeWhyInstead
Stretching every wordSounds unnatural and tiring to listen toKeep words at normal speed; add pauses between phrases
Trying to be slow the whole timeYou'll forget halfway throughFocus on the start and on key points
Cramming content into the timeForces speedCut content, not pauses
Only practicing when relaxedYour pace is fine thenPractice under a bit of pressure

The biggest single cause of rushing in talks is too much material. If your talk is 10 minutes and your script is 1,800 words, you will rush. Cut to around 1,300–1,500.

Check your progress

Re-measure every week, in the same three situations. A good sign is when your "under pressure" number gets close to your "relaxed" number.

Questions people ask

What is a good speaking pace?

For English, conversation is commonly around 120–160 words per minute and presentations around 130–150. Clarity and pauses matter more than hitting an exact number.

Why do I talk so fast?

Often habit, nerves, or trying to fit too much in. It's usually worst at the start of a talk or answer, when nerves are highest.

How do I slow down when I'm nervous?

Breathe out slowly before you start, say your first sentence deliberately slowly, and pause at every full stop. The pauses do most of the work.

Does talking fast make you seem less confident?

It can make you harder to follow and sound rushed. A steady pace with clear pauses usually comes across as more composed.

Can talking too slowly be a problem?

Yes. Very slow speech can lose attention. The aim is a steady pace with pauses, not slow words.
