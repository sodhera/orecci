# How to Give an Impromptu Speech With No Preparation

When you're asked to speak with no warning, pick one quick framework before you open your mouth: past-present-future, PREP (point, reason, example, point) or what-so what-now what. Open with a thank-you or a line about the occasion to buy a few seconds, make one point with one example, and finish with a clear closing line. Aim for about one minute.

## The first five seconds

The moment someone says "Would you like to say a few words?", your brain wants to say no or start talking immediately. Do neither.

1. **Stand up or turn to face the room.** Moving gives you two or three seconds.
2. **Pick your framework** (below) while you move. Just the name: "past-present-future".
3. **Pick your one point.** One thing you want people to remember. Not three.
4. **Start with a buying-time line** you already know by heart.

### Opening lines that buy time

- "Thank you, I wasn't expecting this, so I'll keep it short."
- "I'd love to. Let me start with how I met Dana."
- "Before we eat, just a minute from me."
- "What a question. Here's what comes to mind."

These are honest, warm and give your brain a few seconds to line up the next sentence.

## Three frameworks for no-notice speaking

### 1. Past, present, future

The most flexible framework for occasions: farewells, birthdays, project wrap-ups, welcomes.

| Part | Say | Example: a colleague's leaving drinks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Past | Where it started, or a memory | "When Dana joined, our reports were three weeks late." |
| Present | Where things are now | "Now they go out on time, and half the team uses the template she built." |
| Future | A wish or what comes next | "Her new team doesn't know how lucky they are. To Dana." |

### 2. PREP

Best when someone asks for your opinion: "What do you think, Sam?" in a meeting or a panel.

Point, Reason, Example, Point. "I'd support it. The main reason is cost. Last year the same switch saved the Leeds office a month of overtime. So yes, let's try it." The [PREP method guide](/speaking-coach/guides/prep-method/) has templates.

### 3. What, so what, now what

Best for surprise updates: "Can you tell everyone where the project is?"

- **What:** "We finished testing on Friday."
- **So what:** "That means we can launch on time."
- **Now what:** "Next week we need sign-off from finance. Priya, I'll send that today."

## The one-minute shape

Whichever framework you use, the speech has the same outline:

| Section | Length | Words (approx.) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Buying-time opener | 5 seconds | 10–15 |
| Your one point, through the framework | 40 seconds | 90–100 |
| Closing line | 10 seconds | 15–25 |

At a conversational pace of about 120–160 words per minute, that's 120 to 150 words. Short is a gift to the room, especially at social events where people are standing with a drink.

## How to end

The ending is where impromptu speeches fall apart. People finish their point, feel the silence, and keep adding "and also…". Decide your ending before you start:

- **A toast:** "So please raise your glasses to Dana."
- **A thank-you:** "Thank you all for being here."
- **A call back to the start:** "Three weeks late to right on time. Thank you, Dana."
- **A handoff:** "That's all from me. Over to you, Mark."

Say it, smile, stop. Don't add "Yeah, so, that's it."

## What not to do

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "I'm terrible at this" | Lowers expectations and makes people uncomfortable | A warm thank-you |
| Trying to be funny on the spot | Off-the-cuff jokes often misfire | Warm and sincere works every time |
| Covering three or four points | You lose the thread and run long | One point, one example |
| Inside jokes | Half the room is left out | A story everyone can follow |
| Trailing off | Weak ending, awkward silence | A closing line chosen in advance |

## Practice prompts

Impromptu speaking improves fast with practice, because what you're really practicing is starting without a plan. Set a timer for one minute, pick a prompt at random, give yourself five seconds, and speak out loud.

1. Your team just hit a goal. Say a few words.
2. A colleague is leaving after five years. Say a few words.
3. You're asked to welcome a new team member at a meeting.
4. Someone asks: "What's the best advice you've ever had?"
5. You're asked to thank the host at a dinner.
6. Your manager asks what you think about a four-day week.
7. You have to introduce the next speaker, whose name you only just learned.
8. A friend's birthday dinner: "Go on, say something!"
9. At the end of a workshop: "Any final thoughts?"
10. "Tell everyone about the project you're working on."

Record two of these a day for a week and listen back. You'll hear your openers and endings get faster and cleaner. ## Questions people ask

### How long should an impromptu speech be?

At a social event, about one minute. In a meeting, 30 to 60 seconds. Contests and classes sometimes set longer limits, such as two minutes, but the shape is the same.

### What if I can't think of anything to say?

Fall back on past-present-future. There's always a "how it started", a "now" and a "what's next". Saying the first one usually brings the rest.

### Can I say no when asked to speak?

Yes. "I'll leave this one to Sam, but cheers to Dana" is a perfectly polite answer. But if it's someone you know well, a 30-second thank-you is usually appreciated.

### Should I tell a joke?

Only if it's gentle and about the occasion, not about a person's flaws. Sincerity works more reliably than humor when you haven't prepared.

### How do I get better at impromptu speaking?

Practice short one-minute speeches on random prompts, out loud, several times a week. The [think on your feet guide](/speaking-coach/guides/how-to-think-on-your-feet/) has more drills.
