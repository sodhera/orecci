How to Overcome Fear of Public Speaking and Stage Fright

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Fear of public speaking shrinks with repeated, gradual exposure: speak out loud to one person, then a few, then a small group, then a room, moving up only when the current step feels manageable. On the day, use the last 10 minutes to breathe slowly, say your first lines out loud, and focus on one friendly face rather than the whole room.

Why avoiding it makes it worse

Every time you skip the presentation, hand the toast to someone else or stay quiet in a meeting, the relief teaches your brain that speaking was dangerous. The fear grows a little. Every time you speak and nothing terrible happens, it shrinks a little.

The aim isn't to stop feeling nervous. Most experienced speakers still feel a jolt before they start. The aim is to get the fear down to a level where it doesn't stop you or take over your voice. That happens through repetition at a level you can handle, not through one big brave leap.

The exposure ladder

Work up these rungs in order. Stay on each rung until you've done it two or three times and your nerves before it are noticeably lower than the first time. Then move up.

RungWhat you doHow longExample
1Speak out loud to no one, recorded1–2 minExplain your job to your phone's voice recorder
2Speak to an AI or video-call partner2 minAnswer a question out loud and hear a reply
3One person you trust, in person2–3 minTell a friend about a trip, standing up, without being interrupted
4Two or three people you know3 minExplain a plan to housemates or family at dinner
5Speak up once in a group setting30 secOne comment or question in a team meeting or class
6A short prepared update to a group2–3 minGive the weekly update in a meeting
7A small group of strangers3–5 minA club, a community meeting, a workshop where you introduce a topic
8A room5+ minA presentation, a toast, a talk

A few rules make the ladder work:

  • Speak out loud every time. Running it in your head doesn't count. Your body has to hear your voice under a little pressure.
  • Stay until the nerves dip. Nerves tend to be worst at the start and ease as you keep going. If you stop at the peak, you only practice the worst part.
  • Repeat before you climb. One success on a rung is a start. Three in a row is when it starts to feel ordinary.
  • Drop back if you need to. A bad day on rung 6 isn't failure. Do rung 5 twice and try again.

Rungs 1 and 2 matter more than they look. Recording yourself is mildly uncomfortable, which is the point. An AI partner adds the feeling of being listened to without any social risk. The 10 minutes before you speak

This is the routine for the day itself, once you're at the venue or about to join the call.

  1. 10 minutes out: find somewhere quiet. A corridor, a bathroom, an empty room. Stand rather than sit.
  2. Breathe slower than feels natural. Breathe in through your nose for about four counts, out through your mouth for about six. The longer out-breath is what helps. Do this for two minutes.
  3. 8 minutes out: say your first three sentences out loud. Quietly is fine. The opening is where nerves hit hardest, and saying it once means your mouth has done it before. See how to start a speech.
  4. 6 minutes out: loosen your body. Roll your shoulders, shake out your hands, open your jaw wide a few times. Tension in the jaw and shoulders tightens the voice.
  5. 5 minutes out: have water nearby. Take a sip, not a gulp.
  6. 3 minutes out: rename the feeling. Tell yourself "I'm ready" rather than "I'm scared". Your heart racing is the same signal either way.
  7. 1 minute out: pick a friendly face. When you start, deliver your first lines to one person who looks receptive. Widen your gaze after the first minute.
  8. First 10 seconds: slow down on purpose. Nerves speed you up. Take a breath, look up, and say your first sentence slower than you think you need to.

What to do if fear hits mid-talk

  • Pause. Two seconds of silence feels long to you and normal to the audience.
  • Look at your notes for the next point. Having one-line notes is a safety net, not a weakness. See what to do when your mind goes blank.
  • Ground your feet. Feel both feet on the floor. It stops swaying and gives you a second to reset.
  • Don't announce it. "Sorry, I'm really nervous" draws attention to something most people hadn't noticed.

Common thinking traps

ThoughtWhat's usually true
"Everyone can see I'm shaking."People notice much less than you feel. A shaky hand rarely reaches the back row.
"If I make a mistake, it's ruined."Audiences forget small slips within seconds, especially if you carry on.
"I have to be as good as the last speaker."You only need to be clear. Clear beats polished.
"I'll be fine if I just wing it."Unprepared speaking is the fastest way to raise anxiety. Rehearse out loud.

Questions people ask

Is fear of public speaking normal?

Yes, it's very common. It even has a name, glossophobia, and most people who speak well have learned to manage it rather than lost it.

How long does it take to get over stage fright?

It varies. Many people notice the first rungs of the ladder getting easier within a few weeks of regular practice. Climbing to a full room usually takes longer, and that's fine.

Should I imagine the audience in their underwear?

It doesn't help most people. Focusing on one friendly listener and on your message works better than trying to trick yourself.

What if my fear is so bad I avoid work or school?

If fear of speaking is affecting your job, studies or daily life, talk to a doctor or a qualified therapist. This guide covers practice, not treatment.

Does practicing alone actually help?

Yes, as a first step. Speaking out loud alone builds familiarity with your material and your voice. It works best when you then move up to real listeners.
