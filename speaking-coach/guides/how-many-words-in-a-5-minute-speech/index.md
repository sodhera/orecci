# How Many Words Is a 5-Minute Speech? (With a Timed Sample)

A 5-minute speech is about 700 words at an average speaking pace of 140 words per minute, and between 550 words (slow) and 850 words (fast). For a speech with pauses, laughs or slides, write about 560 to 630 words, which is 80 to 90 percent of 700. That is enough for one message and three short points.

## The word count for 5 minutes

| Pace | Words in 5 minutes |
| --- | --- |
| Slow (110 words per minute) | 550 |
| Average (140 words per minute) | 700 |
| Fast (170 words per minute) | 850 |
| **Write this many** (80–90% of average) | 560–630 |

Conversational English is commonly cited at about 120 to 160 words per minute, and presentations often land around 130 to 150, so 140 is a sensible middle. Over five minutes, slow and fast end up 300 words apart. The same 700-word draft runs 6 minutes 22 seconds for a slow speaker and 4 minutes 7 seconds for a fast one. The [pace checker](/speaking-coach/tools/pace-checker/) tells you which you are, and the [speech length calculator](/speaking-coach/tools/speech-length-calculator/) turns any draft into minutes.

**In pages,** 700 words is about one and a half single-spaced pages, or two and a half to three double-spaced. That rests on a rough rule of thumb of 500 words to a single-spaced page of 12-point text, which fonts and margins change.

## What fits in 5 minutes

One message with three short points under it. The points are not three topics but three reasons, scenes or steps that hold up the same sentence. Each gets about a minute: enough for a claim and one example. A [3-minute speech](/speaking-coach/guides/how-many-words-in-a-3-minute-speech/) has room for two points; the extra two minutes buy the third, plus time to say where you are going.

Five minutes suits:

- a **class presentation**, alone or as your part of a group's ([presenting at school](/speaking-coach/presentations/school-presentation/))
- a **pitch**: a pitch competition, a demo day slot, or an idea pitched to your manager ([what to say in a startup pitch](/speaking-coach/presentations/investor-pitch/))
- a **best man speech**, at the long end of the usual three to five minutes ([with examples and timing](/speaking-coach/speeches/best-man-speech/))

Made-up examples of the message and its three points:

| Speech | The message | Three points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Class presentation | "The bus isn't slow, it's stuck at one junction" | What we measured; why it happens; what we propose |
| Pitch | "We help small cafés stop pouring away unsold milk" | The problem; how it works; what we need from you |
| Best man speech | "He's the friend who turns up" | A story from before; what changed when he met his partner; a wish for them both |

What doesn't fit is a second message. If you need "and also" to say what your talk is about, you have two talks. For presentations, [how to structure a 5-minute presentation](/speaking-coach/presentations/5-minute-presentation/) has a version built around slides.

## A timing plan for 5 minutes

| Part | Time | Words | What goes in it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Opening | 30 seconds | 70 | The message in one sentence, then "We'll cover three things" |
| Point one | 70 seconds | 165 | Your strongest point, with one example |
| Point two | 70 seconds | 165 | A different kind of support: a story if point one was numbers |
| Point three | 60 seconds | 140 | The shortest point, or the one that leads into your ask |
| The close | 30 seconds | 70 | The message again, and what you want people to do |
| Pauses and slide changes | 40 seconds | 0 | Laughs, a click, a glance at the screen |
| **Total** | **300 seconds** | **610** | |

Words are counted at 140 per minute and rounded to the nearest 5. Start each point with a one-line signpost, such as "The second thing we found surprised us." It costs three or four seconds, and it is how listeners keep count. More on that in [signposting](/speaking-coach/glossary/signposting/).

## A 140-word opening to time yourself

This made-up opening of a class presentation is exactly 140 words, the first minute of a five-minute talk at an average pace. It does what the plan's opening asks: the message, then the three points in order. Read it out loud with a timer.

> Good morning. Last month, our group stood at the bus stop outside the school gate every morning for two weeks, with a notebook and a stopwatch. The number 12 bus is supposed to arrive at 8:05. It came on time twice. On the other eight days, it was between six and nineteen minutes late. Here is what we found: the bus isn't slow, it's stuck, and almost all of the delay happens in one place. We'll cover three things. First, where the time is actually lost, because it isn't where we expected. Second, what three of the drivers told us, because they were very happy to talk. And third, our proposal, which would cost the town less than a new bench. Then we'll take your questions. So let's start where we did, at 8:05, with a stopwatch and no bus.

If that took 60 seconds, you speak at 140 words per minute. If not, your pace is 8,400 divided by your time in seconds. Here is what your pace does to the 610-word plan above:

| Your time for the sample | Your pace | The 610-word plan at your pace |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 50 seconds | 168 words per minute | 3:38 |
| 55 seconds | 153 words per minute | 3:59 |
| 60 seconds | 140 words per minute | 4:21 |
| 65 seconds | 129 words per minute | 4:44 |
| 70 seconds | 120 words per minute | 5:05 |

Times are talking only. At 120 words per minute, the plan runs past five minutes before anyone laughs, so a slow speaker should write about 510 words, which is 85% of 600. At 168, the plan leaves over a minute spare. Don't fill it with a fourth point; slow down, because nerves usually speed you up on the day.

## Slides for a 5-minute presentation

Three to five suits most five-minute talks: a title, one slide per point and a close that shows the message again. It depends on the slides. A pitch of single photos or numbers can use more, because each is seen rather than read; a slide with a table can need a whole minute. Every change costs a few seconds, which is part of why the plan keeps 40 seconds free. Formats where slides advance on their own set the number for you; see [how to give a lightning talk](/speaking-coach/presentations/lightning-talk/).

## What to cut when you're over

How much to cut depends on how far over you are. At 140 words per minute:

| You're over by | That's about | Cut |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 50 words | 20 seconds | The warm-up, and any "before I start" |
| 100 to 150 words | 45 seconds to a minute | Point three's example; keep its one-sentence claim |
| 200 words or more | nearly a minute and a half | A whole point, or rebuild around one message |

Then cut what each kind of five-minute speech tends to carry too much of:

- **Class presentation:** the history and the definitions. One sentence of background, then what you found.
- **Pitch:** the market-size slide and the team biographies. One line on the team, unless someone asks.
- **Best man speech:** the second story, before anything about the couple.

Don't fix an overrun by speaking faster. At 170 words per minute a 700-word draft fits on paper, but the room has to work to keep up.

## Questions people ask

### How many words is a 5-minute speech?

About 700 words at an average pace of 140 words per minute, and between 550 and 850 depending on your pace. For a speech with pauses, laughs or slides, write 560 to 630.

### How many pages is a 5-minute speech?

About one and a half single-spaced pages, or two and a half to three double-spaced, if a single-spaced page holds roughly 500 words. Count words to be sure.

### Is 800 words too long for a 5-minute speech?

Yes, for most speakers. 800 words takes 5 minutes 43 seconds at 140 words per minute, and still 4:42 at a fast 170, which leaves almost no time for pauses. Cut it to about 600.

### How many words is a 6-minute speech?

About 840 words at 140 words per minute, and between 660 and 1,020 depending on your pace. For a real six-minute slot, write about 670 to 755.

### How many slides for a 5-minute presentation?

Three to five for most talks: a title, one per point and a close. Formats with slides that advance on their own set their own number.
