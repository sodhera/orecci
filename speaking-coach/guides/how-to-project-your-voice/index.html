How to Project Your Voice Without Shouting or Straining

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Projecting is carrying your voice to the furthest listener without forcing it from your throat. Breathe low so your belly moves, let the sound resonate by opening your mouth and keeping your throat relaxed, and aim your words at the back wall. If your throat feels tight, scratchy or sore, you're pushing too hard; back off and use more breath instead.

Projecting is not shouting

Shouting comes from the throat. It sounds strained, goes up in pitch, and tires your voice within minutes. Projecting comes from breath and resonance. It sounds fuller rather than just louder, stays at your normal pitch, and you can keep it up for an hour.

You can feel the difference: shout "Hey!" and your neck tightens. Call "Hello!" to someone across a field, as if greeting them warmly, and most of the effort comes from your belly and your open mouth.

The three parts of projection

PartWhat it meansWhat goes wrong without it
Breath supportAir from low in your body, released steadilyYou run out of air and push from the throat
ResonanceSound that fills your mouth and face, not squeezedThin, quiet voice that doesn't carry
AimSpeaking to the furthest listenerVoice drops off a few metres in front of you

Work on them in that order. Breath first.

Breath support exercises

Most quiet speakers breathe high, into the chest and shoulders. Projection needs low breath. This is often called diaphragmatic breathing.

  1. Find the low breath. Lie on your back with a book on your belly. Breathe in through your nose; the book should rise. Your chest and shoulders should barely move. Do this for a minute.
  2. Stand and repeat. Put one hand on your belly, one on your chest. Breathe in so the lower hand moves out.
  3. Long hiss. Breathe in low, then let out a steady "sssss" for as long as is comfortable. Keep it even, not a burst then a trickle. Repeat three times.
  4. Count out loud. Breathe in low and count "one, two, three…" at a firm speaking volume on one breath. Stop before you strain. Over a week, you'll find you get a little further.

Resonance exercises

Resonance is what makes a voice sound full. You get it by giving the sound space.

  1. Humming. Hum "mmm" on a comfortable pitch with your lips lightly closed. You should feel a buzz on your lips and nose. Slide the pitch up and down slowly.
  2. Hum into words. Hum "mmm", then open into a word: "mmm-many", "mmm-morning", "mmm-moon". Try to keep the buzzing feel as you open.
  3. Open the mouth. Say "far, car, star" with your jaw dropping fully. A barely open mouth is like a speaker with a cushion over it.
  4. Yawn-sigh. Start a yawn, and as your throat opens, sigh out "aahh" from high to low. This relaxes the throat, which is where strain comes from.

Aiming exercises

Your voice goes where your attention goes. If you're looking at your notes or the front row, your volume tends to drop to that distance.

  1. The back wall. Stand at one end of a room. Pick a spot on the far wall. Say "Good morning, everyone" so the words land on that spot.
  2. Three distances. Say the same sentence to an imaginary person one metre away, then five, then fifteen. Notice what changes: mostly breath and mouth opening, not throat effort.
  3. Throw a ball. Mime tossing a ball to the back of the room as you say a sentence. It sounds silly; it works, because your body naturally sends energy that far.
  4. Keep the last word up. Most people's volume fades at the end of a sentence, exactly where the key word often is. Read a paragraph making sure the final word of each sentence reaches the back.

Checks for different rooms

SettingTargetQuick check
Meeting table, 4–8 peopleNormal conversation, a notch upCan the person at the far end hear you without leaning in?
Meeting room, 10–25 peopleClearly raised, still conversationalAim at the back row; slow down slightly
Large room, 30–100 people, no micFull projectionAsk early, "Can everyone at the back hear me?" and watch for nods, not just words
Large room with a microphoneNormal, steady voiceKeep the mic a fist's width from your mouth; don't project into it
OutdoorsFull projection, slowerSound disperses; slow down and pause more
Video callNormal voiceLet the mic do the work; projecting can distort

A useful trick in any room: ask a friend to sit at the back and give you a signal if they can't hear.

Warning signs you're straining

Stop and change approach if you notice:

  • A tight or aching throat, or neck muscles standing out.
  • Your pitch rising as you get louder.
  • Hoarseness or a scratchy feeling after speaking.
  • Needing to clear your throat often.

The fix is almost always more breath, more mouth opening and less throat effort. Sip water during long talks. If hoarseness lasts more than a couple of weeks, or speaking is painful, see a doctor or voice specialist rather than practicing through it.

A 5-minute warm-up before you speak

  1. Three slow, low breaths (30 sec).
  2. Long hiss, twice (30 sec).
  3. Hum and slide up and down (1 min).
  4. "Mmm-many, mmm-morning, mmm-moon" (30 sec).
  5. Yawn-sigh, twice (30 sec).
  6. Say your first two sentences to the back wall (1 min).
  7. One more low breath, then go.

Questions people ask

How can I make my voice louder without shouting?

Breathe low, open your mouth more, and aim your words at the furthest person. The extra volume should come from breath and resonance, not from tightening your throat.

Why does my voice get tired when I speak loudly?

Usually because you're pushing from the throat instead of supporting with breath. Strain also comes from speaking at a pitch that's higher than your natural one.

Can a quiet voice be trained to project?

Yes. Breath and resonance exercises done for a few minutes a day make a clear difference for most people within a few weeks.

Should I project when using a microphone?

No. Speak at a steady, normal volume and keep the mic a consistent distance from your mouth. Projecting into a mic can sound harsh.

How do I project my voice when I'm nervous?

Nerves make breathing shallow. Take two slow, low breaths before you start, and aim your first sentence at the back wall.
