How to Write an Elevator Pitch About Yourself (With Examples)

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

A personal elevator pitch answers three things in 30 to 60 seconds: who you are, one specific thing you've done that proves it, and what you're looking for from this person. Build it from short blocks you can drop or add, end with a question, and practice it out loud until you can say it a slightly different way each time.

Write it as blocks, not a script

Most pitch advice gives you a single paragraph to memorise. The trouble is that real moments vary: sometimes you get 20 seconds before someone's coffee is ready, sometimes a recruiter says "tell me more". A pitch built from blocks stretches and shrinks without falling apart.

BlockWhat it saysSecondsKeep it when…
1. WhoYour role, field or what you're studying5Always
2. ProofOne specific thing you did, with a detail or number10–15Always
3. AngleWhat makes your mix unusual, or why you care10You have 45+ seconds
4. Second proofAnother example, from a different area10–15You have a full minute
5. AskWhat you're looking for, ending in a question5–10Always

Blocks 1, 2 and 5 make a 25-second pitch. Add 3 and 4 for a full minute. At a normal speaking pace, 30 seconds is about 70 words and 60 seconds about 140. The speech length calculator converts any draft for you.

Fill-in template

Copy this and fill each line with your own facts:

  1. Who: "I'm a ___ who ___." (or "I'm studying ___ at ___.")
  2. Proof: "Recently I ___, which ___."
  3. Angle: "What I bring that's a bit different is ___." (or "I got into this because ___.")
  4. Second proof: "Before that, I ___."
  5. Ask: "I'm looking for ___. Do you know anyone who ___?" (or "How does your team handle ___?")

Write each block as one spoken sentence. If a block needs two, the detail is probably too technical for a first conversation.

Three example pitches

These are made-up examples to show the structure. Swap in your own facts.

A student (about 35 seconds)

"I'm in my final year studying environmental science. For my dissertation I measured air quality at twelve bus stops around the city and presented the results to the local council's transport team. I'd love to do that kind of work full time, turning field data into something decision-makers can use. I'm looking for graduate roles in sustainability consulting. Is that the kind of work your team does?"

A career changer (about 50 seconds)

"I'm a primary school teacher moving into learning design. For eight years I've been building lessons for thirty kids with thirty different attention spans, so I've learned to make hard things simple and to test whether they actually stick. Last spring I built an online onboarding course for new teaching assistants at my school, and the head now uses it for every new hire. What I bring is that I've seen what happens when training meets a real, distracted audience. I'm looking for a junior instructional design role, ideally in a company that trains frontline staff. Do you know teams like that?"

A founder (about 45 seconds)

"I run a small company that makes scheduling software for physiotherapy clinics. I started it after five years as a physio, watching receptionists spend half their day on the phone moving appointments. We now have twenty clinics using it, and the main thing they tell us is that their no-show rate dropped. Right now I'm looking to talk to clinic owners outside our region to see whether the same problem exists there. Do you know anyone who runs a practice?"

Notice that each one ends with a question. A pitch that ends in a statement leaves the other person to think of something to say. A question hands them the next line.

How to say it without sounding rehearsed

A pitch sounds rehearsed when every word is fixed. It sounds natural when the points are fixed and the words change a little each time.

  1. Memorise the blocks, not the sentences. Know your five points in order. Let the wording drift.
  2. Practice out loud in different lengths. Say a 20-second version, then a 60-second one, then a 40-second one. Switching lengths forces you to think instead of recite.
  3. Look at the person, not into the middle distance. People who recite tend to look up and away, as if reading from a screen in their head.
  4. Pause after the Who. Half a second after "I'm a nurse moving into health tech" lets the listener catch up and makes the rest sound unhurried.
  5. Drop the pitch voice. Speak at the volume and pace you'd use telling a friend about your job. If your voice rises and speeds up, slow down for the proof block.
  6. Let them interrupt. If they jump in after block 2, answer them. The pitch did its job: it started a conversation.

Mistakes to avoid

MistakeWhy it hurtsInstead
Opening with your job title onlyTitles mean different things at every companySay what you actually do or make
A list of adjectives"Passionate, driven, detail-oriented" proves nothingOne example that shows it
Jargon from your fieldThe listener nods and forgetsExplain it as you would to a friend
No askThe conversation stallsEnd with what you want and a question

Questions people ask

How long should an elevator pitch about yourself be?

Thirty seconds is the classic length, about 70 words. Have a 60-second version ready for when someone asks you to say more.

Is an elevator pitch the same as introducing yourself?

No. A self-introduction in a meeting is about ten seconds: name, role and one hook. A pitch adds proof and an ask. See how to introduce yourself.

Should my pitch mention what I'm looking for?

Yes, in most settings. People can only help with a need they know about. At a purely social event, swap the ask for a question about them.

How do I pitch myself if I'm unemployed?

Lead with what you do and what you've done, not your status. "I'm a data analyst; most recently I…" is accurate and stronger than "I'm currently looking for work."

What if I'm nervous and forget it?

Keep only blocks 1 and 5 in your head: who you are and what you're looking for. Those two sentences alone still start a useful conversation.
