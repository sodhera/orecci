How to Use Pauses in a Speech for Emphasis and Control

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Pause before your key point (about 1 second) and after it (2 to 3 seconds), after asking the audience a question (3 to 5 seconds), between sections (about 2 seconds) and once before your first word. Mark pauses in your script with / for short and // for long, and count them silently in rehearsal. A pause always feels longer to you than to the audience.

The pause types, with timings

Treat these as starting points. Larger rooms and more serious moments can take longer pauses; casual updates take shorter ones.

PauseWhereLengthWhat it does
Opening pauseAfter you reach the front, before your first word2–3 secondsSettles you and the room; you start in control
Breath pauseAt commas and natural phrase breaksUnder 1 secondKeeps you from running out of air; helps listeners follow
Before a key pointJust before the line you most want rememberedAbout 1 secondSignals "this matters"
After a key pointRight after that line2–3 secondsGives people time to absorb it

Why pauses work

A pause does several jobs at once:

  • It replaces fillers. Most "um"s happen where a pause should be. Learning to stay silent for one second is one of the most effective fixes for fillers, as the stop saying um guide explains.
  • It controls your pace. Speakers who say they talk too fast often speak at a normal word rate but never stop. Pauses slow the overall pace without making the words drag.
  • It creates emphasis. Silence is the frame around a key line. Without it, the important sentence sounds like all the others.
  • It gives you thinking time. You can find your next point during a pause that looks intentional.

Pauses feel longer to you than to them

When you're speaking, a two-second silence can feel like an age. To the audience, it usually sounds calm and deliberate. Your sense of time speeds up when you're nervous, so a pause that feels uncomfortable to you is usually about right.

The fix is to count rather than judge by feel. In your head: "one-and, two-and." Count every planned pause during rehearsal until the length feels normal.

How to mark pauses in a script

Mark your script or notes before rehearsing:

MarkMeaningCount
/Short pause"one"
//Long pause"one-and, two-and"
[P]Question pause, wait for the roomUp to five
Line breakNew section, transition pause"one-and, two-and"

Here is a made-up example of a marked opening for a team talk:

"Last quarter / we answered every customer email / within a day. // This quarter / we didn't. // So today / I want to show you why, / and one change that fixes it. // How many of you / have waited more than a day for a reply / from a company you pay? [P]"

Read it out loud, honoring every mark. Then read it again ignoring them. The difference is usually obvious.

Drills to get comfortable with silence

  1. The count drill. Read a paragraph aloud. At every full stop, count two seconds silently before going on. It will feel too slow. Record it and listen: it usually sounds confident.
  2. The key-line drill. Pick one sentence from your talk. Say the sentence before it, pause one second, say the key line, pause three seconds. Repeat until it feels natural.
  3. The filler-swap drill. Talk for one minute about your day. Every time you feel an "um" coming, close your mouth and pause instead. A pause is the replacement, not a new word.
  4. The question drill. Ask an imaginary audience a question and count to five before you continue. In real talks, people need that time.

Check your speed before and after with the pace checker. Adding pauses should bring your overall words per minute down even if you say each phrase at the same speed.

Pauses vs freezes

A pause is chosen. A freeze is when you've lost your place. They can look the same from outside, which is good news: if you blank, holding still for two seconds looks like a pause. The difference is what happens next. After a pause you continue with your point; after a freeze you need a way back in.

Questions people ask

How long should a pause be in a speech?

Most pauses are between one and three seconds. Breath pauses can be under a second, and a pause after a question to the audience can be up to five.

Do pauses make you sound less confident?

Usually the opposite. Calm, planned pauses tend to sound confident and in control. Fillers and rushing are what make speakers sound unsure.

How do I stop filling pauses with "um"?

Close your mouth at the end of each phrase and breathe in through your nose. It's hard to say "um" with your mouth closed. Practice it in everyday conversation first.

How many pauses should a speech have?

Every sentence ends with a short pause, and each section or key point gets a longer one. In a five-minute speech that might mean five to ten deliberate longer pauses.

What's the difference between a pause and dead air?

A pause has a purpose and a clear end. Dead air is silence where the speaker seems lost. If you look calm and continue with a clear line, it reads as a pause.
