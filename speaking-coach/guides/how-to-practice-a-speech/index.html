How to Practice a Speech: A Rehearsal Plan That Works

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Practice a speech out loud, standing up, about eight to twelve times spread over several days, not all the night before. Start with slow read-throughs, then timed full runs, then targeted drills on the opening, transitions and ending, and finish with one dress rehearsal in conditions close to the real thing.

A rehearsal schedule you can follow

The plan below is for a five- to ten-minute speech with about a week to go. Shorter notice? Squeeze it: keep the order, drop some runs, but keep the dress rehearsal.

WhenRunsWhat you're doing
7 days out2Read the draft out loud, sitting down. Mark every sentence that is hard to say.
6 days out2Fix the marked sentences on paper, then read it again. Time the second run.
5 days out2Stand up. Speak from a short outline, not the full text.
4 days out1 full + drillsOne timed full run, then 10 minutes on the opening and the transitions only.
3 days out2Full runs from the outline, out loud, recorded once.
2 days out1 full + drillsOne full run, then drill whatever went wrong on the recording.
1 day out1Dress rehearsal (see below). Then stop.
The day0–1Say your opening and closing out loud twice. No full run.

That is roughly eleven full run-throughs plus focused drills. You don't need more than that, and the night before is the wrong time to add five extra runs. Tired rehearsal teaches you to sound tired.

Out loud, not in your head

Running a speech silently feels productive, but it skips the part that goes wrong. In your head you never trip over a long sentence, never run out of breath and never hear yourself rush. Out loud you do, and you find out while there is still time to change the words.

Silent runOut-loud run
Checks you remember the orderChecks you can actually say it
Fast, so it hides how long the speech isGives you a real time
Easy to skip the hard partsMakes you say every word
Fine for a last look on the trainNeeded for every run that counts

Use silent runs only for quick reviews, for example reading your outline on the way there.

Timing runs

Time every full run and write the number down. A speech you planned at five minutes can come in at four when you rush or seven when you add asides. Typical presentation pace in English is around 130–150 words per minute, so a 700-word speech is close to five minutes. The speech length calculator converts your word count either way.

Rules for timing:

  1. Time from your first word to your last, including pauses you plan to take.
  2. If you're over, cut a whole point or example, not a few words from every sentence.
  3. Aim to finish 10–15 percent under your limit. Laughter, applause and nerves eat time on the day.
  4. If your runs keep getting shorter, you're speeding up. Slow the opening first.

Fixing the hard parts

Most speeches fail in the same three places: the first 30 seconds, the joins between sections, and the last line. Full run-throughs give each of those only one try per run. Drill them separately.

  • The opening. Say your first two sentences out loud five times in a row. You should be able to start without looking at anything.
  • Transitions. Say only the last line of one section and the first line of the next, for every section, in order. This is where people blank, because the next idea isn't connected to the words they just said.
  • The closing. Know your final sentence word for word, and the pause before it.
  • Tongue-twister sentences. If you trip on the same sentence three times, rewrite it. Shorter sentences are easier to say and easier to hear.

A useful drill: start at a random section and keep going. If you can only start at the top, you don't know the speech yet, you know a sequence.

The dress rehearsal

Make one run as close to the real thing as you can:

  • Stand where you will stand, or somewhere similar. If there's a lectern or microphone, mimic it.
  • Wear the shoes and clothes you plan to wear.
  • Use the exact notes or slides you will use, in the final order.
  • Have one person listen, or record yourself on video. Ask the listener for one thing that worked and one thing to change, not a general opinion.
  • Don't stop if you make a mistake. Recover and keep going, because that is what you'll have to do on the day.

Then stop rehearsing. Changes after the dress rehearsal should be small.

Mistakes that waste rehearsal time

MistakeWhy it hurtsInstead
Memorizing word for word from day oneOne missed word and you lose your placeLearn the outline first; memorize only the opening and close
Always starting from the topYou know the beginning well and the end badlyDrill sections out of order
Rehearsing only silentlyYou never hear the speed or the hard sentencesEvery counted run out loud
Editing after every runYou never practice the same speech twiceEdit in batches, on separate days
Skipping the audienceNo one asks the question you're dreadingRun it once for a person, or a partner who asks questions

Questions people ask

How many times should I practice a speech?

For most speeches, eight to twelve full out-loud runs spread over several days, plus short drills on the opening, transitions and close. Big or high-stakes talks may need more, but spacing matters more than the total.

Should I practice in front of a mirror?

It can help you notice posture once or twice, but it splits your attention between speaking and watching. A recording is more useful, because you can review it afterwards with full attention.

Is it bad to practice the night before?

One relaxed run is fine. Cramming many runs late at night tends to make you tired and anxious. Do your dress rehearsal earlier and keep the night before light.

What if I only have one day?

Do two slow read-throughs out loud, one timed run from an outline, ten minutes on the opening and transitions, and one final run for a listener. Then rest.

Should I memorize my speech?

Usually memorize the opening and closing lines and speak the middle from an outline. The notes or memorize guide helps you decide for your situation.
