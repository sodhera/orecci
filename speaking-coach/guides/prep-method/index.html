PREP Method: How to Give a Clear Answer in 30 Seconds

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

PREP stands for Point, Reason, Example, Point. State your answer in one sentence, give the main reason, back it with one short example, then repeat the point in fresh words. It fits opinion and 'what do you think' questions in meetings, class or interviews, and takes about 30 to 45 seconds; for behavioral 'tell me about a time' stories, use STAR or CAR instead.

The four parts and their timing

PartWhat it doesStarterTime
PointYour answer, in one sentence"I think…" / "My recommendation is…"5 seconds
ReasonThe single most important why"The main reason is…"8–10 seconds
ExampleOne concrete case that shows the reason is true"For example…" / "Last month…"10–15 seconds
PointThe answer again, ideally with a next step"So I'd…" / "That's why…"5 seconds

At a conversational pace of around 120–160 words per minute, 30 seconds is about 60 to 80 words. That's enough for a clear answer and short enough that people can respond.

The fill-in template

"I think [your position]. The main reason is [one reason]. For example, [a specific case, number or moment]. So [your position again, or what you'd do next]."

The final Point isn't a lazy repeat. It's your chance to turn the opinion into an action: "So I'd suggest we try it for one month."

When PREP fits, and when it doesn't

Question typeExampleUse
Opinion or judgment"Should we switch tools?"PREP
"What do you think about…" in an interview"What makes a good manager?"PREP
On-the-spot update request"Where are we on the launch?"PREP or BLUF
Class discussion or seminar"Is social media good for democracy?"PREP
Behavioral story"Tell me about a time you failed"STAR or CAR, not PREP
Long presentationA 20-minute talkA full structure; PREP for each section at most

The difference from STAR: STAR tells one story in order. PREP leads with a position and uses a story only as evidence. If someone asks for a story, give a story. If someone asks what you think, give your point first. The STAR method guide covers the story version.

Worked examples

These are made-up examples to show the shape. Swap in your own facts.

In a team meeting

Question: "Should we move our weekly meeting to async updates?"

Point: "I think we should keep one live meeting but make it shorter."

Reason: "The main reason is that the live discussion is where we catch blockers early."

Example: "Two weeks ago the payment bug came up in the meeting and was fixed the same day. In a written update it would have sat for a day or two."

Point: "So I'd keep the meeting, cut it to 20 minutes, and move status updates to writing."

In a job interview

Question: "What do you think makes a good team leader?"

Point: "I think a good team leader makes it easy for people to say what's going wrong."

Reason: "Problems get cheaper the earlier you hear about them."

Example: "My last supervisor started every Monday by asking what was stuck, not what was done. People raised issues in week one instead of week four."

Point: "So that's the kind of leader I'd want to be: someone people bring problems to early."

In a class or seminar

Question: "Should homework be banned in primary school?"

Point: "I don't think it should be banned, but it should be short."

Reason: "Short practice at home helps reading stick, but long assignments mostly measure how much help a child gets."

Example: "Ten minutes of reading aloud to a parent builds the habit without needing anyone to teach new material."

Point: "So keep homework, but cap it at about ten minutes and make it reading."

Making it sound natural

  • Don't announce the structure. Never say "My point is… my reason is…". Use normal starters like "I think" and "for example".
  • One reason, not three. Three reasons turn a 30-second answer into two minutes. If they want more, they'll ask.
  • Make the example specific. A day, a number, a name of a project. "For example, in general…" isn't an example.
  • Change the words in the final Point. Repeating the first sentence word for word sounds robotic.
  • Pause after the first Point. One beat of silence makes your position land before you explain it.

Building the habit

PREP is most useful when you don't have time to plan, so practice it until it's automatic.

  1. Write ten opinion questions from your work or studies on cards.
  2. Pull one, give yourself five seconds to find your Point, then answer out loud.
  3. Time it. Over 45 seconds? You probably gave more than one reason.
  4. Record a few and check: could someone repeat your point after hearing it once?

Questions people ask

What does PREP stand for?

Point, Reason, Example, Point. You state your view, explain why, prove it with an example, and finish by restating the view or a next step.

Is PREP the same as BLUF?

They're related. Both put the main point first. BLUF (bottom line up front) is mostly used for written updates and status reports; PREP adds a reason and an example, which makes it better for spoken opinions.

Can I use PREP in a job interview?

Yes, for opinion and judgment questions like "What makes a good manager?" or "How do you see this industry changing?" For "Tell me about a time…" questions, use STAR or CAR.

How long should a PREP answer be?

About 30 to 45 seconds when spoken, or roughly 60 to 100 words. Stretch it to a minute only if the question is big.

What if I don't have an example?

Use a short hypothetical instead: "Imagine a customer who…". A specific, realistic scenario is still more convincing than a general statement.
