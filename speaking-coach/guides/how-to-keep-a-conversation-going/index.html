How to Keep a Conversation Going When It Starts to Die

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

When a conversation starts to die, go back to something the other person already said and ask a follow-up about it: how it happened, what was hardest, what's next. If that runs dry, share a short related detail of your own to give them something to ask about. If neither works after a couple of tries, exit warmly; not every conversation needs saving.

Signs a conversation is fading

You'll usually feel it before it stops: answers get shorter, both of you look around, someone says "Yeah…" and nods. That's the moment to act, not after the silence has stretched to ten seconds.

The rescue has an order. Try the first move; if it doesn't catch, try the next.

  1. Follow up on something they said.
  2. Thread off an earlier detail.
  3. Share something of your own.
  4. Exit gracefully.

Move 1: Six follow-up patterns

The easiest way to revive a conversation is to go deeper on what's already there. You don't need a new topic. These six patterns work on almost anything someone tells you.

PatternQuestion shapeExample (they said "I've been training for a half marathon")
The start"How did you get into…?""How did you get into running?"
The hard part"What's been the hardest bit of…?""What's been the hardest part of the training?"
The best part"What do you enjoy most about…?""Do you actually enjoy the long runs, or just finishing them?"
The why"What made you decide to…?""What made you pick a half and not a 10k?"
What's next"What's next after…?""Are you thinking about a full one after this?"
The comparison"How does it compare to…?""Is it different from what you expected?"

Pick one and ask it. If it lands, follow the answer with another from a different row. These turn a fact into a story, and stories keep conversations alive. More on the idea in follow-up questions.

Move 2: Thread off an earlier detail

People drop small details all the time and nobody picks them up. Those are threads you can go back to when the current topic runs out.

Here's a made-up example of a fading chat and the threads in it:

They said, five minutes ago: "Sorry I was late, I've just come from my sister's. She's moving to Glasgow next week."

Then you talked about work, and it's dying.

Threads you could pull:

  • The sister: "You mentioned your sister's moving to Glasgow. Is she excited or stressed?"
  • The move: "Have you been helping her pack? Moving is brutal."
  • The place: "Have you spent much time in Glasgow?"

Coming back to something they mentioned earlier shows you were listening, which people notice and like. A useful habit: as you chat, make a mental note of two or three side details (a place, a person, a plan) to use later.

Move 3: Share a bit of yourself

A conversation where only one person asks questions feels like an interview, and the answerer eventually runs out. If your follow-ups aren't catching, give them something to ask you about.

The trick is a short, specific detail with a hook in it:

Too littleToo muchJust right
"Yeah, I like running too."Five minutes on your marathon times"I tried a half once and walked the last three miles. Never again, or maybe again."
"I've been to Glasgow."Your whole trip, day by day"I went to Glasgow last year and got lost looking for one café for an hour."

The "just right" version is two sentences with something odd or funny in it. That's the hook. Then stop and let them pick it up. If they don't, ask them a question back.

Move 4: Exit gracefully

Sometimes a conversation has simply run its course. That's fine. Small talk isn't a test you fail by ending it. A good exit leaves both people feeling it went well.

A graceful exit has three parts: a signal, a warm line about the chat, and a reason or next step.

"Anyway, I'm going to grab another drink. It was really nice to meet you, and good luck with the half marathon."

"I should go and find my team before the next session. Let's catch up at lunch?"

"I'll let you get back to it. Tell your sister good luck with the move."

Avoid fake reasons you'll be caught out on, like saying you're leaving and then standing nearby for an hour. See how to end a conversation for more exit lines.

Questions people ask

What do you say when a conversation dies?

Go back to something the other person said earlier and ask a follow-up: "You mentioned X; how did that go?" It works better than starting a new topic.

How do I keep a conversation going without asking too many questions?

Alternate. Ask a question, then when they answer, share a short related detail of your own before you ask another.

What if they're giving one-word answers?

Try two open questions about different things. If both get one word, they may be busy, shy or not in the mood. Exit kindly and don't take it personally.

How long should I let a silence go?

Two or three seconds is normal and often fine. If it goes past five and you both look uncomfortable, use a thread or exit.

Is it rude to end a conversation first?

No, as long as you do it warmly. Thanking them and naming something you enjoyed about the chat is polite.
