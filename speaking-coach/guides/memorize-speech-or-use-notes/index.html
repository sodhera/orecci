Should You Memorize a Speech or Use Notes? How to Decide

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

For most speeches, don't memorize every word and don't read a full script. Memorize your first and last lines, then speak from a short outline of key phrases. Memorize the whole thing only for very short, polished pieces like a 60-second pitch, and read from a script only when exact wording matters, such as a eulogy or a formal statement.

Four ways to deliver a speech

MethodWhat it meansSoundsRisk
MemorizedEvery word learned by heartPolished if it goes well; can sound recitedForget one line and the rest can collapse
Full scriptRead word for word from paper or screenPrecise, but often flat, with little eye contactHead down, monotone
OutlineA few key phrases per point; words chosen liveNatural and conversationalCan ramble without practice
HybridOpening and closing memorized, middle from an outlineConfident start and finish, natural middleNeeds a few run-throughs

The hybrid is the default for most people and most talks. Speaking from an outline after rehearsing is sometimes called an extemporaneous speech.

The case against memorizing word for word

Memorizing feels safe because you know exactly what you'll say. But it has two hidden costs.

  • It's fragile. A memorized speech is a chain. If one link goes, you may not find the next. Many people's worst blank moments happen mid-memorized speech, because there's no outline to fall back on.
  • It sounds recited. When you're retrieving exact words, your attention is inward. Your eyes drift up, your pace evens out and your voice flattens.

If you do memorize, also memorize the outline, so you can jump to the next point if a sentence disappears.

The case against reading a script

Reading is safe in a different way, but you lose eye contact and the audience feels it. Most people also speak faster and flatter when reading. If you must read, see the tips below for making a script readable.

How to make notes you can glance at

Good notes are designed to be read in one second from arm's length. That means fewer words than you think.

  1. Write your outline first. Your main message, then three or four points.
  2. Cut each point to three to six words. "Queue doubled: 3 onboarding gaps", not a sentence.
  3. Write your first and last lines in full. These are the only full sentences on the card.
  4. Use big text. If you can't read it held at your waist, it's too small. On paper, write large; on a screen, use at least 20-point font.
  5. One card per section, numbered. Index cards are easier to hold than A4 paper and don't shake as visibly.
  6. Mark cues, not words. A star for a story, a number you need, a name you mustn't forget, an arrow for "slide change".
  7. Leave white space. Crowded cards make you hunt.

Here's a made-up example of a card for one section:

2. WHY NOW

- Busy season: 6 weeks - Queue: 2x since March - ★ Story: Monday server crash - → slide 5

That card holds about a minute of speech, and you can read it in a glance. For more on what to put in slide software's notes, see speaker notes.

If you have to read a script

  • Print it large, double spaced, with short paragraphs.
  • Mark pauses with a slash and underline one word per sentence to stress.
  • Look up at every full stop. Read the next sentence silently first, then look up and say it.
  • Hold it at chest height, not on a table, so your head stays up.
  • Rehearse it out loud at least three times, so you're half-remembering rather than fully reading.

How to practice each method

MethodRehearsal approach
MemorizedLearn the outline first, then one section at a time. Practice starting from random sections.
ScriptRead it aloud three to five times, looking up more each time.
OutlineSay it through five times. The words will change each time; that's fine.
HybridDrill the first and last lines ten times; run the middle from the outline three to five times.

Questions people ask

Is it better to memorize a speech or use notes?

For most speeches, use notes: a short outline of key phrases, with your opening and closing memorized. Full memorizing suits only very short pieces.

Is it unprofessional to use notes?

No. Audiences expect notes for anything longer than a couple of minutes. Glancing at a card looks far better than freezing.

How long does it take to memorize a 5-minute speech?

It varies a lot by person. Learning the outline and your first and last lines takes much less time than learning every word, and gives you most of the benefit.

Can I read my speech off my phone?

You can, but a phone's small screen makes text tiny and it can lock mid-speech. If you use it, turn auto-lock off, use large text, and hold it steady at chest height.

Should I memorize my wedding speech?

Memorize the opening and the toast at the end. Keep the middle on a card. Emotional moments often cause blanks, and a card is completely normal at weddings.
