How to Breathe While Speaking So You Don't Run Out of Air

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

You run out of breath mid-sentence when you speak long stretches without topping up, breathe high in the chest, or rush. Fix it by taking a small, quick breath at every phrase break (commas, full stops, after key words), breathing low into your belly, and shortening your sentences. Practice by marking breath points in a paragraph and reading it aloud.

Most people have two or three of these at once. The good news: they're all habits, and they respond to practice.

Breathe at phrase breaks

Speech has natural breaks: commas, full stops, the end of a clause, the pause before a key word. Those are your breathing points. A speaking breath is small and quick, a sip rather than a gulp, taken through the mouth or nose in under a second.

The mistake is waiting until you're out of air. By then your voice has already gone thin and rushed. Top up often instead, even when you don't feel you need to.

Your listeners won't notice these breaths. They hear them as natural pauses, which also make you easier to follow. The pauses guide explains how pause and breath work together.

Breathe low

Breathing into the belly, often called diaphragmatic breathing, gives you a steadier, longer supply of air than breathing high into the chest.

A simple check:

  1. Sit or stand upright. Put one hand on your belly and one on your upper chest.
  2. Breathe in through your nose for about four seconds. The belly hand should move out. The chest hand should stay mostly still.
  3. Breathe out slowly through your mouth for about six seconds. The belly hand moves back in.
  4. Repeat five times.

If your shoulders rise when you breathe in, you're breathing high. Try lying on your back with a book on your belly. It's easier to feel the movement there.

Shorten your sentences

Writing habits often carry over into speaking: long sentences with several clauses. They're hard to say in one breath and hard to follow.

Before: "We've been working on the new onboarding process for the last three months with the support and sales teams and we think it's ready to roll out to all new starters from next month, assuming the IT setup is done in time."

After: "For three months, we've worked on the new onboarding process with support and sales. We think it's ready. We'd like to roll it out to all new starters next month, as long as the IT setup is done in time."

Same content, four breathing points instead of none. If you're giving a prepared talk, read every sentence aloud during rehearsal. Any sentence you can't say comfortably in one breath, split.

Read-aloud drills

Drill 1: Count on one breath

Take a low breath and count out loud at a steady pace: "one, two, three…". Stop when you need air. Don't squeeze. Note the number. Repeat daily for a week. The aim isn't a record; it's noticing what a steady, supported exhale feels like.

Drill 2: Marked paragraph

Copy a paragraph from anything you read for work. Mark a / at every place you'll breathe. Then read it aloud, taking a small breath at every mark and nowhere else. Here's a made-up example:

"Our team handled more calls this month / than any month this year. / Most were about the new billing page. / So next week / we're adding a short guide to the page / and we'll track whether calls drop. /"

Drill 3: Breath ladder

Say a short sentence on one breath. Then a slightly longer one. Then longer. Stop the moment you feel you're pushing. Step back down. This teaches you where your comfortable limit is.

  1. "I'm ready."
  2. "I'm ready to start the meeting."
  3. "I'm ready to start the meeting, so let's look at the numbers."
  4. "I'm ready to start the meeting, so let's look at the numbers from last week first."

Drill 4: Slow first sentence

Say the opening sentence of a talk or answer at half speed, breathe at the full stop, then continue at normal speed. Nervous starts are where most breathlessness begins.

Pace and breath go together

If you speak fast, you'll need more air more often, and you'll skip breaths to keep going. Check your speed with the pace checker. If you're well above the typical conversational range of about 120–160 words per minute, slowing down will help your breathing as much as any exercise. The slow down guide has more on that.

Questions people ask

Why do I run out of breath when I talk?

Usually because you speak in long stretches without taking small breaths at phrase breaks, breathe shallowly into the chest, or speak fast. Nerves can make all three worse.

Should I breathe through my nose or mouth when speaking?

Both are used. Quick breaths during speech often go through the mouth because it's faster. Slower nose breathing is good before you start, to settle.

Can people hear me breathing when I speak?

Small breaths at natural pauses are rarely noticed. Loud gasps happen when you've waited too long, so breathing earlier and more often keeps them quiet.

How do I breathe while giving a presentation?

Take a full, low breath before your first word, then a small top-up at every full stop and most commas. Mark breath points in your notes until it becomes automatic.

What if I get breathless even when I'm not speaking?

If you're short of breath at rest or it's getting worse, talk to a doctor. This guide covers breath control for speaking only.
