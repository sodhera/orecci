What students actually need from a speaking app

Most "best public speaking app" lists are written for executives. A student's week looks different: a graded report on Thursday, a group presentation where you have three slides, a recitation, a thesis defense with a panel, sometimes all of it in English when English isn't your first language. Three things matter more for you than for a manager:

  • Price. Many good tools are free or free through your school. Check those first.
  • Questions after the talk. Teachers and panels grade the Q&A as much as the talk. Most apps only listen; few ask back.
  • Language. If you present in English but think in Filipino, Hindi or Spanish, an app that only works in English can't help you rehearse the version in your head.

The apps, one by one

PowerPoint Speaker Coach. Microsoft's support page says it is in free preview for everyone, even without an Office subscription; on the web and Android you sign in with a personal Microsoft account or a school account. It flags pace, pitch, filler words, informal phrases and reading your slides aloud, then gives a report. It runs on Windows, Mac, the web, iPhone, iPad and Android, in English only. Best for: anyone whose deck is already in PowerPoint. This is the first thing to try if your school gives you Microsoft 365.

The free voice review on this site. Our free speech review listens to you answer out loud, for up to 2 minutes (3 for a speech), and shows your measured pace, filler words and long pauses. You also get feedback that quotes your own words: one thing to keep, one change, and one of your sentences rewritten more clearly. It is English only, allows two free reviews per network per day, and needs no account or install. Your audio goes to Speaking Coach's server to be transcribed and isn't kept. Best for: checking your opening minute the night before.

Orai. Its App Store listing describes feedback on filler words, pace, clarity, energy, confidence and conciseness, with short lessons and scripted or freestyle practice for presentations, speeches and more. The listing doesn't say which features are free; in-app prices shown were $12.99 a month, $49.99 a year or $99.99 lifetime. English only. Best for: practicing a memorized script alone.

Speeko. The App Store listing offers free speaking exercises and tools, with feedback on pacing, filler words, tone and pauses, plus "Convos", AI roleplays for job interviews, difficult conversations and small talk. Full access was listed at $24.99 a month or $99.99 a year. English only. Best for: building a daily habit over a semester.

Speaking Coach (ours). You add your slides as a PDF and swipe through them like a clicker while you speak, then an AI audience asks the questions a class or panel would ask. Afterwards you see your pace in words per minute, filler words, long pauses and tips that quote what you said, and you can retry the moment that went wrong for 90 seconds. It works in 20 languages, Filipino, Hindi, Indonesian and Vietnamese among them, and never rates your accent. It's a subscription with no free tier in the app; see the App Store or Google Play for the price in your country.

Yoodli. Its pricing page lists a free Starter plan with 5 lifetime roleplay sessions, Pro at $8 a month billed annually, and no student pricing. Best for: a few practice sessions in the browser.

VirtualSpeech and Bodyswaps. Both build realistic practice rooms and sell mainly to schools. VirtualSpeech's individual plan was $45 a month or $399 a year, with no student discount listed; education pricing is quoted per cohort or campus. Bodyswaps' home page lists higher education and career readiness, runs in a browser, on mobile or in VR, and offers a free trial. Best for: students whose university already has a licence. Ask your career office before paying.

Toastmasters. Its joining page says visiting a club is completely free. Joining costs $72 every six months plus a one-time $25 fee. Best for: once you've rehearsed alone and want real faces.

A week before a class presentation

  1. Day 1: Time a full run with your notes. If it's over, use the speech length calculator to see how many words to cut.
  2. Day 2: Rehearse with your slides in Speaker Coach or a recording, and fix the slide you keep reading from.
  3. Day 3: Practice only the first 60 seconds, three times. A strong opening carries the nervous part.
  4. Day 4: Write the five questions your teacher is most likely to ask, and answer each out loud in under 45 seconds.
  5. Day 5: Do a full run with someone asking those questions: a classmate, a family member or an app that plays the audience.
  6. Day before: One relaxed run, then stop. Our school presentation guide covers structure and grading.

For a thesis defense, add a second round of questions from a "tough panel" and practice saying "That's outside my study, but here's what my data does show." The thesis defense guide lists the questions panels ask most.

If you get very nervous

Start alone, where nobody is watching: a recording or a delivery app. Then add one listener, then a small group. The order matters more than the app. Our guide to public speaking anxiety explains why this works.

Questions people ask

What is the best free public speaking app for students?

As of November 2026, PowerPoint's Speaker Coach is free in preview and works with a school Microsoft account, in English. For a quick check of pace and filler words with quoted feedback, the free voice review on this site allows two reviews a day without an account. Our free public speaking apps list gives the exact limits of each.

Can an app help me prepare for questions after my presentation?

Yes, but only apps that talk back. Speaking Coach reads your slides and asks the questions an audience would ask; Yoodli, VirtualSpeech and Bodyswaps run roleplays. Delivery apps like Orai and Speaker Coach measure how you spoke but don't ask anything.

Is there a public speaking app in Filipino or other languages?

Most delivery apps listed here are English only. Speaking Coach works in 20 languages, including Filipino, so you can rehearse in the language you'll present in, or in your first language before switching to English.

Do I need a paid app to get better at presenting?

No. A free tool plus a few honest listeners covers most class presentations. Paid apps help with volume: practicing the Q&A ten times at midnight when nobody is free to listen.

Do universities give students free access to these apps?

Some do. VirtualSpeech and Bodyswaps sell licences to campuses, and Big Interview's site quotes a school that gives students free access. Ask your career office or library before you pay for one yourself.
