Rehearsing a deck is three jobs, not one

Most people rehearse a presentation by clicking through it in their head. That catches typos and nothing else. A real rehearsal checks three things, in this order:

  1. Timing. Does it fit the slot, and where does it drag?
  2. Delivery. Pace, filler words, and whether you're reading your slides aloud.
  3. Questions. Can you answer the three things the room will ask, without the slides?

Different tools are good at each step. We make Speaking Coach, which covers step 3 and parts of step 2. We checked every other tool's own help page or site in October 2026.

Step 1: Timing

Keynote. Apple's guide describes Play > Rehearse Slideshow, which opens the presenter display without a second screen. You can show presenter notes, a clock and a timer, and edit notes as you go. There's no speech feedback, but it's the most honest way to time a Keynote deck.

Google Slides. Its presenter view shows speaker notes, and the help page mentions taking audience questions while you present. It doesn't mention a timer or any rehearsal feedback, so use your phone's stopwatch.

Word count first. If you're working from a script or detailed notes, check the length before you rehearse. Presentations often land around 130–150 words per minute, so a 10-minute slot fits roughly 1,300–1,500 words, less if you pause for slides. The speech length calculator does the sum for you.

Mark where you should be halfway through ("slide 6 at 5:00"). If you're behind at the midpoint in rehearsal, cut a slide now rather than rushing on the day.

Step 2: Delivery

PowerPoint Speaker Coach is the best free option if your deck is in PowerPoint. Microsoft's support page says it's in free preview for anyone with a Microsoft account, gives on-screen guidance while you rehearse, and produces a Rehearsal Report afterwards. Depending on the platform it flags pacing, filler words, monotone, and reading the text on your slides. Limits: English only, it sends your speech to Microsoft, and the report disappears when you close it, so take a screenshot.

Yoodli produces a speaking report on pacing and filler words with suggestions, and lets you practice on your own decks. Orai and Speeko are phone apps that analyze a recording of you speaking; they're better for building habits over weeks than for one specific deck.

Whichever you use, fix one thing per run. If the report says you're too fast and use too many fillers, slow down first; fillers often drop with it. The free pace checker is enough if you just want a words-per-minute number.

Step 3: Questions

This is the step most people skip, and where presentations go wrong. You can't rehearse Q&A alone, because you don't know what you'll be asked.

Speaking Coach (ours). You add your slides as a PDF and swipe through them like a clicker while you speak. When you finish, the AI audience asks the questions your audience would ask, follows up on your answers, and then shows your pace, fillers, long pauses and tips that quote your own words. You can retry a moment that went badly, such as a question you fumbled. It runs on iPhone and Android in 20 languages; it's a subscription.

Yoodli's public speaking page describes opting in to AI follow-up questions and a simulated Q&A with tough, skeptical audiences. It runs on the web.

Ovation places you in a virtual boardroom, lecture hall or debate stage, in VR or on a computer, with AI avatars who ask questions based on what you said.

Without any tool, ask a colleague to listen and ask the most annoying question they can think of. Our guide to handling Q&A covers how to answer when you don't know.

Questions people ask

What is the best free tool to rehearse a presentation?

As of October 2026, PowerPoint's Speaker Coach is free in preview and gives feedback on pace, fillers and reading your slides, in English. Keynote's rehearse mode is free with Keynote but only times you.

Does Google Slides have a rehearsal coach?

Google's presenter view help page describes speaker notes and taking audience questions, but no rehearsal feedback or timer. Pair it with a stopwatch and a separate recording analyzer.

How do I practice answering questions after a presentation?

Use a tool that asks you questions, like Speaking Coach, Yoodli's follow-up questions or Ovation, or ask a colleague to play a skeptical listener. Prepare short answers to your five most likely questions first.

How many times should I rehearse?

Enough that the opening and the transitions between slides feel automatic, usually three to five full runs. Rehearse the first minute more than the rest.

Can I rehearse my slides on my phone?

Yes. PowerPoint Speaker Coach works on iOS and Android, and Speaking Coach lets you swipe through a PDF of your slides on iPhone or Android while you speak.
