## Presentation practice tools compared

Most people rehearse a presentation by clicking through it in their head. That catches typos and nothing else. A real rehearsal checks three things in order: **timing** (does it fit the slot?), **delivery** (pace, fillers, reading your slides aloud) and **questions** (can you answer what the room will ask?). Different tools are good at each.

## Short verdicts, by step

### Timing

**Keynote.** Apple's guide describes Play > Rehearse Slideshow, which opens the presenter display without a second screen, with presenter notes, a clock and a timer you can rearrange. No speech feedback, but the most honest way to time a Keynote deck.

**Google Slides.** Presenter view shows speaker notes and lets you take audience questions. The help page mentions no timer or rehearsal feedback, so use a stopwatch.

**A word count.** Presentations often land around 130–150 words per minute, so a 10-minute slot fits roughly 1,300–1,500 words, fewer if you pause for slides. The [speech length calculator](/speaking-coach/tools/speech-length-calculator/) does the sum.

### Delivery

**PowerPoint Speaker Coach** is the best free option if your deck is in PowerPoint. Microsoft's page says it's in free preview, even without an Office subscription, gives on-screen guidance while you rehearse and a Rehearsal Report afterwards. On desktop it covers pace, monotone, fillers and reading slide text. **Skip it if** you present in another language (English only for now) or want to keep the report: it disappears when closed, and your speech is sent to Microsoft.

**Yoodli** gives a speaking report on pacing and filler words, on your own decks. **Pick it if** you rehearse at a computer and also want Q&A practice.

**Orai** and **Speeko** analyze a recording of you on your phone. Orai is $12.99 a month or $49.99 a year; Speeko now has a free tier. They suit building habits over weeks more than one deck.

**The free AI voice review on this site** is built into the English presentation pages and the [free speech review](/speaking-coach/tools/free-speech-review/): say your opening or one section out loud for up to 2 minutes (3 for a speech) and get your pace, fillers and long pauses, plus feedback that quotes your words, with one thing to keep, one change and one sentence rewritten more clearly. English only, two reviews per network per day, no account; audio goes to Speaking Coach's server for transcription and is not kept.

### Questions

This is the step most people skip, and where presentations go wrong. You can't rehearse Q&A alone, because you don't know what you'll be asked.

**Yoodli's** public speaking page describes opt-in AI follow-up questions and simulated Q&A "with tough, skeptical audiences". **Pick it if** you're at a desk with your deck open. It's the strongest web option for this step.

**Ovation** puts you in a virtual boardroom, lecture hall or press room in VR, on desktop or on the web, at $15 a month billed yearly with a 7-day trial. **Pick it if** you want to feel the room as well as hear the questions.

**VirtualSpeech** lets you present your own slides to an audience that asks about what you said. It's aimed at organizations; individuals pay $45 a month or $399 a year.

**The Speaking Coach app (ours).** Add your slides as a PDF and swipe through them like a clicker while you speak. When you finish, the AI audience asks the questions your audience would ask and follows up on your answers. Then you see your pace, fillers, long pauses and tips that quote your words, and can retry a question you fumbled. 20 languages; a subscription. **Skip it if** your deck has animations or video that matter to the talk; it works from a PDF.

Without any tool, ask a colleague to listen and ask the most annoying question they can think of. Our guide to [handling Q&A](/speaking-coach/guides/how-to-handle-q-and-a/) covers how to answer when you don't know.

## How to choose: a rehearsal week

| When | Do this | Tool |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5 days before | Time a full run-through; cut if over | Slide software's rehearsal mode |
| 4 days before | Two runs focused on delivery | Speaker Coach, Yoodli or the free AI voice review |
| 3 days before | Write the five questions you fear most | Pen and paper |
| 2 days before | Present, then take questions | Yoodli, Ovation, the Speaking Coach app or a colleague |
| The day before | One calm run, opening and closing twice more | Any |

Mark where you should be halfway ("slide 6 at 5:00"). If you're behind at the midpoint in rehearsal, cut a slide now rather than rushing on the day.

## Questions people ask

### What is the best free tool to rehearse a presentation?

As of November 2026, PowerPoint's Speaker Coach is free in preview and covers pace, fillers and reading your slides, in English. Keynote's rehearse mode is free but only times you. The AI voice review on this site gives two free English reviews a day.

### Does Google Slides have a rehearsal coach?

Google's presenter view help page describes speaker notes and taking audience questions, but no rehearsal feedback or timer. Pair it with a stopwatch and a separate delivery check.

### How do I practice answering questions after a presentation?

Use a tool that asks you questions, like Yoodli's follow-up questions, Ovation or the Speaking Coach app, or ask a colleague to play a skeptical listener. Prepare short answers to your five likeliest questions first.

### How many times should I rehearse?

Enough that the opening and the transitions between slides feel automatic, usually three to five full runs. Rehearse the first minute more than the rest.

### Can I rehearse my slides on my phone?

Yes. PowerPoint Speaker Coach works on iOS and Android, and the Speaking Coach app lets you swipe through a PDF of your slides on iPhone or Android while you speak.
