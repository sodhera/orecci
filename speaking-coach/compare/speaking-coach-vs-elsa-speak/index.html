Speaking Coach vs ELSA Speak: Accent Drills or Real Conversation?

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Choose ELSA Speak if people misunderstand specific sounds when you speak English: it scores pronunciation and maps your speaking to IELTS, TOEFL and CEFR, and it is the better tool for that. Choose Speaking Coach if you're understood but freeze, ramble or rush in real situations like interviews and meetings: it gives you a partner who talks back and never rates your accent. Many non-native speakers benefit from both.

Two different problems

Non-native speakers usually have one of two problems, sometimes both:

  1. "People don't understand some of my words." That's pronunciation: specific sounds, stress, intonation.
  2. "People understand me, but I freeze, ramble or speed up when it matters." That's conversation under pressure: finding words in real time, structuring an answer, staying calm when someone follows up.

ELSA is built mainly for the first. Speaking Coach, which we make, is built only for the second. Neither fixes the other's problem well.

Where ELSA is the better choice

Be clear on this: if your main problem is pronunciation, use ELSA. It is built to hear individual sounds and tell you which ones to fix, and Speaking Coach does not do that at all. ELSA is also the better pick if:

  • you're preparing for IELTS, TOEFL or TOEIC speaking and want a predicted score;
  • you want to target a specific accent (American, British or Australian);
  • you like short, structured lessons that adapt to your level.

Where Speaking Coach is the better choice

  • You're understood, but you freeze. A partner who asks a follow-up you didn't expect is the practice you need, not another drill.
  • You have a specific situation coming. A job interview in English (the interviewer reads your CV and the job post), a presentation (swipe through your PDF slides as you speak), a weekly update to your manager, small talk with new colleagues.
  • You don't want your accent judged. Speaking Coach focuses on whether you were clear: pace, fillers, pauses, and what you said. It never rates your accent or personality.
  • You want to switch languages. You can practice an interview in Spanish today and English tomorrow; it works in 20 languages.
  • You get stuck mid-sentence. Say "Help me" for a sentence to start from, or "I need a moment" to pause.

ELSA now has conversation features too. The difference is focus: ELSA's feedback centers on how you pronounce; Speaking Coach's centers on how the conversation went.

Using both

A simple routine if you have both problems:

  1. 5 minutes of ELSA on the two or three sounds that cause real misunderstandings.
  2. 10 minutes of conversation practice on a real situation you have coming up, in Speaking Coach or with a person.
  3. Once a week, a real conversation with a colleague, tutor or language partner.

What "clear" means to a listener

Listeners rarely need you to sound native. They need you to slow down slightly, pause between ideas, and stress the important word. Questions people ask

Does Speaking Coach correct pronunciation?

No. It never rates your accent or pronunciation. It gives feedback on pace, filler words, pauses and what you said. For pronunciation, ELSA is the better tool.

Is ELSA Speak free?

ELSA is free to download with in-app purchases. As of October 2026, its App Store listing shows Premium with a 7-day free trial and paid plans starting at $19.99 a month.

Which is better for a job interview in English?

If your pronunciation is clear enough to be understood, Speaking Coach, because the interviewer uses your CV and follows up on your answers. If specific sounds still confuse listeners, do short ELSA drills too.

Can I practice languages other than English?

ELSA is for English. Speaking Coach works in 20 languages, including Spanish, French, German, Hindi, Japanese, Korean and Arabic.

Will I lose my accent with ELSA?

ELSA lets you choose a target accent and practice toward it. Whether you want to change your accent is your choice; being clear matters more to listeners than sounding native.
