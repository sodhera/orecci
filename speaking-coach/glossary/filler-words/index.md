# Filler Words in Speech: Meaning, List and Examples

Filler words are sounds, words and short phrases that fill a gap in speech without adding meaning, such as um, uh, like, you know, so, basically and I mean. Everyone uses some: they buy thinking time and signal that you haven't finished. They become a problem in interviews and presentations when there are so many that listeners notice them more than your point.

## Meaning

A filler fills a gap while your brain catches up with your mouth. There are two kinds:

- **Hesitation sounds** such as *um*, *uh* and *er*. These are sometimes called filled pauses: a [pause](/speaking-coach/glossary/pause/) with a sound in it.
- **Filler words and phrases** such as *like*, *you know* and *basically*. These are real words used for a small job, such as softening a claim or holding your turn, rather than for their meaning.

You'll also hear them called verbal fillers, crutch words or verbal tics.

## A list of common filler words

| Type | Examples | Example in a sentence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hesitation sounds | um, uh, er, ah, hmm | "It was, um, about forty people." |
| Starters | so, well, okay, right, now | "So, I've been in sales for five years." |
| Phrases | you know, I mean, kind of, sort of, you know what I mean | "It's kind of a big change, you know?" |
| Emphasis words | basically, actually, literally, honestly, totally | "I literally just finished it." |
| Tags at the end | and stuff, or whatever, and yeah, right? | "We fixed the bugs and stuff." |

## Filler or real word?

The same word can be a filler in one sentence and carry meaning in the next. Example sentences:

| Sentence | Filler? | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "I like this plan." | No | *Like* is the verb |
| "It was, like, huge." | Yes | Remove it and nothing is lost |
| "Sales fell, so we cut prices." | No | *So* links cause and result |
| "So… um… so yeah." | Yes | It fills time and links nothing |
| "I thought it was Tuesday. Actually, it's Monday." | No | *Actually* corrects a fact |
| "I actually really enjoyed it." | Yes | It adds emphasis the sentence doesn't need |

The test: take the word out. If the meaning stays the same, it was a filler.

## Examples: before and after

Made-up answers, before and after cutting fillers.

### In a job interview

> **Before:** "So, um, basically I've been, like, working in customer service for, you know, about four years."
>
> **After:** "I've worked in customer service for four years."

### In a presentation

> **Before:** "Okay so, um, this slide is kind of about our, uh, results and stuff."
>
> **After:** "This slide shows our results. Three numbers matter."

### In a meeting

> **Before:** "I mean, I sort of think we should, like, maybe wait?"
>
> **After:** "I think we should wait a week. Here's why."

The "after" versions aren't stiff. The point just arrives sooner. Some fillers, like *sort of* and *maybe*, overlap with [hedging language](/speaking-coach/glossary/hedging-language/): softening that is fine once, but sounds unsure when stacked.

## Why speakers use them

- **Planning:** "um" holds the space while you find the next word.
- **Holding the floor:** a filler tells others you haven't finished.
- **Softening:** "kind of" makes a statement less blunt.
- **Nerves:** silence feels longer under pressure, so people fill it.
- **Habit:** fillers used with friends carry over into interviews.

A few fillers in casual talk sound natural. They hurt when one appears in most sentences, or when three arrive in a row before your first point.

## How to cut them

1. Talk for one minute about your day and run it through the [filler word counter](/speaking-coach/tools/filler-word-counter/), or record and tally by hand.
2. Pick your most frequent filler and ignore the rest for now.
3. Plan your first sentence, since fillers bunch up at the start of answers.
4. When you feel the filler coming, close your mouth and pause instead.
5. Re-count after a week on the same topic.

The steps for sounds are in [how to stop saying um](/speaking-coach/guides/stop-saying-um/); for words like *like* and *you know*, see [how to stop saying like](/speaking-coach/guides/how-to-stop-saying-like/). Speaking Coach also counts your filler words after each practice session.

## Questions people ask

### What are filler words?

Sounds and words such as um, uh, like, you know and basically that fill a gap in speech without adding meaning.

### What are examples of filler words?

Um, uh, er, so, well, like, you know, I mean, kind of, sort of, basically, actually, literally and "and stuff".

### Are filler words always bad?

No. A few are normal and can sound natural. Removing every one can make you sound stiff.

### What can I say instead of um?

Nothing: a short silent pause does the same job and sounds more confident.

### Do other languages have filler words?

Yes, every language has its own, such as *euh* in French or *este* in Spanish.
