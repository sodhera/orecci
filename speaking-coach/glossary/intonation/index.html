Intonation: Meaning, Types and Examples in Speech

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated November 17, 2026

Intonation is the rise and fall of your pitch across a sentence: the melody of speech. It tells listeners what your words alone don't, such as whether you're stating or asking, finished or still going, sure or unsure, and which word matters most. In English, a falling pitch at the end usually signals a statement, and a rising pitch often signals a yes/no question.

Meaning

Pitch is how high or low your voice is at any moment. Intonation is the pattern that pitch makes across a phrase or sentence. Every language uses intonation, but the patterns differ, and so do English accents. None of them is "wrong"; what matters is whether listeners can follow what you mean.

The main intonation patterns in English, with examples

PatternWhat the pitch doesOften used forExample
FallingDrops at the endStatements, instructions, questions with who, what, where"I'll send it tomorrow." / "Where do you work?"
RisingGoes up at the endYes/no questions, checking, surprise"Is this seat free?" / "You've finished already?"
Fall-riseDrops, then liftsA "but" left unsaid, polite doubt, partial agreement"The plan's good…" (but I have a concern)
Rise-fallLifts, then drops sharplyStrong feeling: being impressed, surprised or certain"That's amazing."
FlatBarely movesBoredom, reading aloud, sarcasm"Great. Another meeting."

Same words, different meaning

How English pitch commonly moves at the end of a sentence:

SentencePitch at the endWhat it signals
"You finished the report."FallsA statement of fact
"You finished the report?"RisesSurprise, or checking
"Did you finish the report?"Usually risesA yes/no question
"When did you finish the report?"Usually fallsA question asking for information
"I wrote the report, the summary and the slides."Rises on each item, falls on the lastA list, and that it's complete

Pitch also marks which word matters. Say each line, with a small jump in pitch on the bold word:

  • "I didn't say we'd cancel it." (Someone else said it.)
  • "I didn't say we'd cancel it." (We might postpone it.)
  • "I didn't say we'd cancel it." (We might cancel something else.)

Intonation in a presentation or interview

MomentTryExample line
Stating your main pointLet the pitch fall at the end"My recommendation is to launch in May."
Listing pointsRise on each item, fall on the last"We'll cover cost, timing and risk."
Starting a new sectionStart a little higher than you ended"Now, the second question: timing."
Landing a key numberA pitch jump on the number, then a pause"We cut waiting time to four days."
Answering an interview questionFall at the end of each claim"I led the project from start to finish."

Patterns that can confuse listeners

  • Rising at the end of statements. A firm statement can sound like a question. That's called uptalk.
  • Very little movement at all. Every sentence sounds equally important: a monotone delivery.
  • The same tune on every sentence. A sing-song pattern that rises and falls in the same place, whatever the meaning.
  • Trailing off. Pitch and volume drop so far that the last words get lost.

Intonation vs. stress, pitch and inflection

TermMeaning
PitchHow high or low your voice is at one moment
IntonationThe pattern pitch makes across a phrase or sentence
StressExtra weight on a syllable or word: louder, longer or higher
InflectionA change of pitch, often at the end of a phrase, as in "rising inflection"
Vocal varietyChanging pitch, pace, volume and pauses across a whole talk

How to practise intonation

  1. Record five sentences: two statements, a yes/no question, a question starting with "what", and a list.
  2. Listen back. Does each statement end with a fall?
  3. Say "I didn't say we'd cancel it" three times, stressing a different word each time.
  4. Take one paragraph of your talk, underline the key word in each sentence, and give it a small pitch jump.
  5. Hum one sentence with your mouth closed to hear the melody without the words, then say it again.
  6. Record the paragraph again and compare it with the first take.

Questions people ask

What is intonation in simple words?

It's the tune of your voice: how your pitch goes up and down as you speak.

What are the types of intonation?

The main patterns in English are falling, rising, fall-rise and rise-fall, plus flat intonation, where the pitch barely moves.

What is the difference between intonation and stress?

Stress is the extra weight on a syllable or word. Intonation is the overall rise and fall of pitch across the sentence. They work together.

Why does intonation matter in speaking?

It tells listeners whether you're asking or telling, finished or continuing, and which word matters.

Is intonation the same in every language?

No. Languages use pitch differently, which is why intonation patterns can carry over when you speak a second language.

How can I hear my own intonation?

Record yourself saying a few statements and questions, then listen for whether your pitch rises or falls at the end.
