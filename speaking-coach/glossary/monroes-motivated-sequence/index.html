What is Monroe's motivated sequence? Definition and example

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Monroe's motivated sequence is a five-step structure for persuasive speeches: Attention, Need, Satisfaction, Visualization and Action. You grab attention, show a problem, offer your solution, help the audience picture the result, then ask them to act. Alan H. Monroe developed it at Purdue University in the 1930s.

The five steps, with examples

Example topic: a student asking the school to install water refill stations. All details are made up.

StepWhat you doExample line
1. AttentionGrab interest with a hook"This term, the bin outside our canteen collected 3,112 plastic bottles. I counted."
2. NeedShow the problem and why it matters to them"We pay for every one, the vending machine runs out by lunch, and the fountains don't fill a bottle."
3. SatisfactionPresent your solution and how it works"Two refill stations, one by the gym and one in the canteen, paid from this year's student fund."
4. VisualizationHelp them picture the result, or the cost of doing nothing"Imagine next summer: no queue at the machine, cold water after practice, and a bin that's half empty."
5. ActionAsk for a specific step"Vote yes on Friday's student fund proposal."

Why it works

The order follows how people tend to be persuaded: they need to care before they listen to a solution, and they need to picture a better outcome before they act. The visualization step is the one most speakers skip, and it's often what makes the call to action feel natural.

Questions people ask

Who created Monroe's motivated sequence?

Alan H. Monroe, a professor at Purdue University, in the 1930s.

Can I use it outside speeches?

Yes. It fits sales pitches, fundraising appeals and proposals to a manager.

What is the visualization step?

Describing what the future looks like if the audience acts, or what happens if they don't, in concrete detail.

Is it the only persuasive structure?

No. Problem-solution and ethos, pathos and logos are other common approaches. Monroe's adds the visualization and action steps.
