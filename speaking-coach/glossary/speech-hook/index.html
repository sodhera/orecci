Hook in a Speech: Meaning, 7 Types and 14 Examples

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated November 16, 2026

A hook in a speech is the opening line or moment that makes the audience want to keep listening. It can be a question, a surprising fact, a short story, a quote, a bold statement, an "imagine" scene or a remark about the room, and it should lead straight to your main point. Most hooks take under 30 seconds.

What a speech hook is

The hook is your first 10 to 30 seconds: the part designed to earn the rest. People decide quickly whether a speaker is worth their attention, so the hook comes before your name, your thank-yous and your agenda. It is also called an attention getter or attention grabber.

A good hook is short, specific and connected to your message. One that has nothing to do with the talk feels like a trick.

7 types of hook, with 14 examples

Every line below is made up to show the type. The numbers and the people quoted are invented, so swap in your own.

TypeExample hookAnother example
Question"When did you last change your mind about something important?""How many of this week's meetings would you skip if nobody noticed?"
Surprising fact"Last month our support team answered 4,212 emails. Eleven of them said thank you.""Our team spent 1,900 hours in meetings last year. That's one person doing nothing else, all year."
Short story"At 6 a.m. on my first day as a nurse, a patient handed me a list of everything the night shift had got wrong.""On the morning of my driving test, I locked the keys inside the car."
Quote"My first manager had one rule: 'If it takes two minutes, do it now.'""Our coach said one thing before every match: 'Play the next ball, not the last one.'"
Bold statement"Most of our meetings should be emails.""You don't need more time. You need fewer priorities."
"Imagine""Picture the car park at 8:55 on a Monday: forty people circling for twelve spaces.""Imagine your phone is switched off for a whole weekend. What do you miss first?"
Something in the room"Everyone here walked past the same broken lift this morning.""It's four o'clock on a Friday, and I'm the last thing between you and the weekend. So I'll be quick."

Fill-in patterns for the two types that need real material:

  • Fact: "Every [week or year], [a number you have checked] [what happens]. That's [the same number as something people can picture]."
  • Quote: "[Name], who [how the audience knows them], used to say: '[their exact words]'."

The rhetorical question and the short anecdote are the easiest places to start, because they work for almost any topic.

How to write your own hook

  1. Write your main point in one sentence. The hook has to lead there.
  2. Pick a type that fits the room: a fact or bold statement at work, a story or quote for a toast, a question or "imagine" in class.
  3. Draft three versions and say each one out loud. Keep the shortest one that still makes sense on its own.
  4. Add one sentence that links the hook to your point. After "Most of our meetings should be emails", it could be "I tracked ours for a month, and here's what I found."
  5. Learn the hook word for word and time it. Aim for under 30 seconds.

Questions people ask

What is a hook in a speech?

It is the opening line or moment that makes the audience want to keep listening, such as a question, a surprising fact or a short story.

What is a good hook for a speech?

One that is short, specific and leads straight to your point. For most topics, a question the audience answers in their heads or a 20-second story is safest.

How long should a speech hook be?

One to three sentences, usually under 30 seconds.

Can I use a quote as a hook?

Yes, if the quote is real, attributed correctly and quickly tied to your point. A line from someone the audience knows often lands better than a famous one.

Should the hook come before I introduce myself?

Often, yes. If someone has already introduced you, start with the hook straight away.
