Rhetorical Question: Meaning, Definition and Examples

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated November 16, 2026

A rhetorical question is a question asked to make a point or create an effect, not to get an answer: the listener answers it silently, or the speaker answers it straight away. "Who doesn't want more free time?" is one. In a speech it can open a topic, make a point feel obvious or challenge the audience just before you give your own answer.

What a rhetorical question means

A rhetorical question has the form of a question but does the job of a statement. "Who doesn't want more free time?" means "everyone wants more free time". Nobody is expected to reply out loud. The word comes from rhetoric, the craft of persuasive speaking, and that is where it does most of its work: speeches, presentations, persuasive talks and adverts.

Examples of rhetorical questions by purpose

These are made-up examples. The third column is what the speaker hopes each listener thinks.

To open a speech"How many passwords have you reset this month?""Too many."
To open a speech"When did you last read a contract all the way to the end?""Never."
To make a point"Would any of us accept a car that broke down once a week? Then why do we accept it from our software?""We shouldn't."
To make a point"If the process works, why are we all still here at nine at night?""It doesn't."
To challenge"Are we really going to wait for a third complaint before we act?""No."
To ask, then answer"So why did the project stall? It wasn't the budget. It was the handoffs."The speaker gives it
In an interview answer"Could I have fixed it alone? Probably. Would the fix have lasted? No, so I brought the support team in early."The candidate gives it

The "ask, then answer" kind has a formal name, hypophora. It is one of the most useful in presentations and interviews, because it turns a list of facts into a small puzzle and its solution.

How to use one in a speech

A rhetorical question works as a hook when:

  1. Everyone can answer it instantly in their head. "How many passwords have you reset this month?" makes each person count.
  2. It leads straight into your point. The next sentence should use their silent answer.
  3. You pause. Give one or two seconds of silence so people actually think. Rushing on wastes it. More on pauses.

For other ways to open, see how to start a speech.

When not to use one

  • When you need a real answer. If you want the figures, ask plainly and wait for the reply.
  • When someone is upset. "Do you ever listen?" lands as an attack. Say the statement instead: "I don't feel heard."
  • Clichés. "Have you ever wondered…?" is so common that audiences tune out.
  • Several in a row. Three rhetorical questions in a row sound like you're avoiding saying anything.
  • When honest answers would differ. If some people would think "yes" where you expect "no", you've lost them.
  • In place of an interview answer. One is fine as a lead-in. The interviewer still needs your answer straight after it.

Questions people ask

What is a rhetorical question in simple words?

A question you ask to make a point, not to get an answer.

What is an example of a rhetorical question?

"Who doesn't want more free time?" The speaker doesn't expect an answer; the point is that everyone does.

Do you answer a rhetorical question?

Not out loud. You answer it in your head, or the speaker answers it for you. If you're unsure, wait a second: a speaker asking rhetorically carries on.

Is it okay to start a speech with a rhetorical question?

Yes, if it's specific and you pause after it. Avoid generic ones like "Have you ever wondered…?"

What's the difference between a rhetorical question and an audience question?

A rhetorical question expects a silent answer. An audience question, like asking for a show of hands, expects people to respond.

How many rhetorical questions should a speech have?

Usually one to three in a five-to-ten-minute talk, spaced out.
