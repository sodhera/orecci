Stage Fright: Meaning, Signs and Examples in Public Speaking

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated November 17, 2026

Stage fright is the fear and nervous tension a person feels before or while performing in front of others, such as giving a speech, presenting in class, acting or playing music. Common signs are a racing heart, shaky hands or voice, a dry mouth and a mind that goes blank. For speakers it often peaks just before and in the first minute, then eases once you are talking; glossophobia is the narrower term for fear of public speaking itself.

Meaning

Stage fright is what many people feel when others are watching and something feels at stake. It is also called performance anxiety, performance nerves or simply "nerves". It can hit anyone who performs: a speaker at a conference, a student giving a class presentation, a best man raising a toast, a pianist at a recital.

The word is about the moment of performing, not about a person's general character. Plenty of outgoing, confident people get stage fright, and plenty of quiet people don't.

Example of the word in a sentence: "She had stage fright before her first presentation, but it faded by the second slide."

Signs of stage fright

Where you notice itCommon signs
BodyRacing heart, shaky hands or knees, dry mouth, sweating, shallow breathing
VoiceShaking, higher pitch, running out of breath, speaking too fast
MindGoing blank, imagining the audience judging you, replaying mistakes
BehaviourAvoiding eye contact, rushing to finish, putting off rehearsal

Examples of stage fright

Made-up examples of how it shows up in different speaking situations.

SituationWhat it can feel likeWhat often helps
Class presentationNotes shake in your hands; the first slide comes out too fastPut the notes on the table; slow down on purpose for the first sentence
Job interview presentationYour mind goes blank on the first questionA recovery line: "Let me take a second to think about that."
Wedding toastA dry mouth and a racing heart while the room goes quietWater within reach; a first line you could say half asleep
Video call with senior leadersYour voice sounds higher and quicker than usualA slow breath out before you unmute
Conference talkYou dread it for days, then feel fine by minute threeRehearse the opening most; the rest usually follows

Notice the pattern: worst at the start, easing once you are under way. That is typical.

Stage fright vs. glossophobia vs. nerves

TermUsually means
NervesNormal tension before something that matters, often fading once you start
Stage frightFear in the moment of performing: speaking, music, acting, sport
GlossophobiaFear of public speaking specifically, often used when it is strong or leads to avoidance
Social anxietyA broader term professionals use for fear of everyday social situations, not only performing

Someone can be relaxed playing guitar on stage and still dread giving a toast, or the other way round.

How to handle stage fright before you speak

  1. Rehearse out loud, standing up, several times. Parts you have only read in your head will feel new on the day.
  2. Learn your first two sentences well enough to say them on autopilot.
  3. Arrive early and stand where you will speak.
  4. In the last few minutes, breathe slowly with a longer breath out. Many speakers find it steadies them.
  5. Keep water within reach. A pause to sip looks normal to the audience.
  6. Expect the first minute to feel the worst, and keep going.

If your mind goes blank mid-talk, see what to do when your mind goes blank. For a step-by-step plan from one listener to a full room, see how to overcome fear of public speaking. If fear stops you from working or living the way you want, talk to a doctor or a mental health professional.

Questions people ask

What does stage fright mean?

It means feeling nervous or afraid when you have to perform in front of other people, such as giving a speech or a presentation.

Is stage fright normal?

Yes. Many experienced performers and speakers still feel nervous before they start. The aim is to perform well with it, not to feel nothing.

What are the signs of stage fright?

A racing heart, shaky hands or voice, a dry mouth, sweating, talking too fast and a mind that goes blank.

Does stage fright go away?

It usually eases with repeated experience of speaking or performing, and with rehearsal that feels close to the real thing.

What helps stage fright in the moment?

Slow breathing with a longer breath out, a well-rehearsed first line and a glass of water nearby. See how to stop your voice shaking.

Is stage fright the same as social anxiety?

No. Stage fright is tied to performing. Social anxiety is broader and can affect everyday interactions. A professional can tell the difference for you.
