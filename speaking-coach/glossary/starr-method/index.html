STARR Method: Meaning, Example Answers and STAR vs STARR

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated November 17, 2026

The STARR method is a way to answer behavioral interview questions in five parts: Situation, Task, Action, Result and Reflection. It is the STAR method plus a closing line on what you learned or would do differently. That last line shows the interviewer you learn from experience, which matters most in questions about mistakes, failure and conflict.

What STARR stands for

PartWhat it answersRough length
SituationWhere were you, and what was going on?One or two sentences
TaskWhat did you have to do?One sentence
ActionWhat did you do, step by step?About half the answer
ResultWhat happened, ideally with a real number?One or two sentences
ReflectionWhat did you learn, or what would you change?One or two sentences, about 10 seconds

The first four are the standard STAR method. The fifth goes by other names: Leiden University's career service, for example, labels it "reflection / evaluation". Some people say "Learning". It's the same idea.

STAR vs. STARR vs. CAR

CARSTARSTARR
PartsChallenge, Action, ResultSituation, Task, Action, ResultSTAR + Reflection
Typical length30–60 seconds1–2 minutes1–2 minutes
Ends withThe outcomeThe outcomeWhat you learned
Best forPhone screens, quick follow-upsMost behavioral questionsMistakes, failure, conflict, feedback

The CAR method is the short version. STARR is the one to reach for when the lesson is the point.

Example STARR answers

Both are made up. Swap in your own story.

"Tell me about a time you made a mistake" (nurse)

Situation: "On a busy night shift, I took a rushed handover and skipped reading back the medication list."

Task: "I was responsible for the medication round for six patients."

Action: "Halfway through, I noticed one dose in my notes didn't match the chart. I stopped the round, checked with the doctor on call, and told the nurse in charge straight away."

Result: "We caught it before the patient was affected, and I wrote it up the same day."

Reflection: "What I took from it is that I now read back the medication list at every handover, even when we're rushed. It adds a minute and it's caught two errors since."

"Tell me about a conflict with a coworker" (graduate)

Situation: "In my final-year group project, one teammate kept missing our meetings."

Task: "I was coordinating the report, and we had three weeks left."

Action: "Instead of complaining to the tutor, I asked him for a coffee. It turned out he worked evening shifts, when we met. We moved meetings to lunchtimes and I gave him the data section, which he could do on his own schedule."

Result: "He delivered his part early, and we got the highest mark in the group."

Reflection: "I learned to ask why before assuming someone doesn't care. I've used that question in every team since."

Reflection lines you can adapt

  • "What I'd do differently is…"
  • "Since then, I always…"
  • "The lesson I took was…, and I used it again when…"
  • "If it happened again, I'd start by…"
  • "What I'd keep doing is…"
Weak reflectionStronger reflection
"I learned communication is important.""I now send a two-line summary after every client call."
"I'd do everything the same.""I'd involve finance a week earlier."
"It taught me a lot.""I learned to ask for the deadline in writing."

Good reflections name a changed habit, not a lesson from a poster.

When to use the extra R

Use it for questions about failure, mistakes, conflict and feedback, and in graduate or competency-based interviews that score learning. Skip it when time is short or the story is a plain success: a quick phone screen often wants STAR or CAR.

How to practise

  1. Pick three stories: a mistake, a conflict and a success.
  2. Write one reflection line for each that names something you now do differently.
  3. Say each answer out loud and time it. Keep the reflection under 15 seconds.
  4. Have someone ask "What would you do differently?" If your reflection already answered it, it works.

For building and timing the full answer, see the STAR method guide.

Questions people ask

What does STARR stand for?

Situation, Task, Action, Result, Reflection.

What is the STARR method in an interview?

A way to answer "Tell me about a time…" questions as a short story in five parts, ending with what you learned or would change.

What is the difference between STAR and STARR?

STARR adds a fifth step, Reflection, after the Result. STAR ends with the outcome; STARR ends with the lesson.

Should every answer use STARR?

No. Use the reflection when the question invites it, such as failures, conflicts and mistakes. For straightforward success stories, STAR is often enough.

Can the reflection be positive?

Yes. "What I'd keep doing" is a valid reflection, as long as it's specific.
