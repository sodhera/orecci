Body Language: Meaning, Types and Examples in Communication

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated November 17, 2026

Body language is the information people take from your posture, gestures, facial expressions, eye contact and movement, alongside your words; it is the visible part of nonverbal communication. When you speak, it tells listeners how sure you are, how you feel about them and whether your words match what you mean, and when the two clash, people tend to believe the body.

Meaning

Body language is everything listeners can see you "say" without words. Your voice (tone, pace, pauses) is the other, audible part of nonverbal communication; see vocal variety for that half. Most body language is unplanned, which is why people trust it: it is harder to fake than a sentence.

Six types of body language, with examples

TypePositive exampleNegative exampleWhat listeners often read into it
PostureUpright, weight on both feet, shoulders looseSlouching, rocking, leaning far backSettled and ready, or nervous and bored
GesturesOpen hands at waist height while explainingHands in pockets, gripping the lectern, clicking a penSure of the point, or tense
Facial expressionsA small smile on your opening lineA frown of concentration, or a blank faceWarm, or displeased even when you aren't
Eye contactOne person per sentence, around the roomEyes on slides, notes or the floorTalking to people, or reading at them
Movement and spaceStanding still, then a step forward for the key pointPacing with no purpose, backing awayIn control, or wanting to escape
Head movementsA nod while the other person talksConstant nodding, or none at allListening, or impatient and absent

Same words, different message

Say "I'm confident we'll hit the deadline" twice. First with arms folded, eyes down and a quick shrug. Then standing still, looking at the person, with a short pause before "hit". The words are identical; most listeners will believe only the second.

Body language in an interview

Examples of what interviewers tend to notice:

  • Arriving: a steady greeting, eye contact as you say your name, and a handshake if they offer one.
  • Sitting: back against the chair, leaning slightly forward, hands resting on the table or your lap.
  • Answering: looking at the interviewer as you start and finish each answer. Looking away while you think is normal.
  • Listening: nodding now and then, not checking the time or your phone.
  • On video: camera at eye level, and looking at the lens when you make your key point. See video interview tips.

Body language in a presentation

  • Before you start: plant your feet, look up, pause, then speak.
  • Hands: rest them at your sides or lightly touching at waist height, and gesture only when it matches the words: a size, a number, a contrast. More in what to do with your hands.
  • Eyes: finish each sentence looking at one person, then move to someone else.
  • Slides: glance at them, then turn back to the room before you speak.

Is body language 55% of communication?

Not in general. The often-quoted 7% words, 38% voice, 55% face split comes from Albert Mehrabian's experiments on how people communicate feelings and attitudes, meaning liking or disliking. On his own site he says the figures don't apply unless the speaker is talking about feelings or attitudes. Your words still carry most of the content; body language mainly colours how they are received.

Read signals with care

Body language is not a code with fixed meanings. Eye contact norms differ between cultures. Some people avoid eye contact or fidget because of disability, neurodivergence or habit, not dishonesty. Folded arms may just mean the room is cold. Look for patterns and changes, not one cue.

How to practise

  1. Record yourself giving a two-minute answer or talk on video.
  2. Watch it once with the sound off. What does your body say on its own?
  3. Pick one habit to fix, such as touching your face or swaying. Only one.
  4. Practise your rest position until it feels normal.
  5. Record again and compare the two. Knowing your material well is what lets your body relax.

Questions people ask

What is body language in simple words?

It's what your body says while you talk: how you stand, move your hands, use your face and look at people.

What are examples of positive body language?

An upright but relaxed stance, open hand gestures, eye contact with different people and a natural smile at the start and end.

What are examples of negative body language?

Slouching, crossed arms with a closed face, avoiding eye contact, fidgeting, and checking your phone while someone talks.

What body language should I avoid in an interview?

Looking at the door or your phone, checking the time, and slouching low in the chair are the most commonly noticed.

Does body language matter on a phone call?

Indirectly. Standing up and smiling change how your voice sounds, even though no one can see you.

Can you fake confident body language?

You can practise it, and it may feel unnatural at first. It holds up best when your preparation is solid underneath.
