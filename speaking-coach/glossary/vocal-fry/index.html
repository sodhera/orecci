Vocal Fry (Creaky Voice): Meaning and Examples

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated November 17, 2026

Vocal fry, also called creaky voice or glottal fry, is the low, crackling or popping sound your voice makes at the very bottom of its pitch range, usually on the last word of a sentence. Almost everyone does it sometimes. When you speak on calls or to a room, it matters because the creaky part is quieter, so the ends of your sentences, often where the key word sits, can be harder to hear.

Meaning

Wikipedia describes the vocal fry register as the lowest register of the voice. Other names for it include creaky voice, glottal fry, pulse register and "popcorning". It is a sound quality, not a word choice or an accent: the words stay the same, but the tone turns rough.

What vocal fry sounds like

Take the line "I'll send it over this afternoon." In fry, the pitch sinks on the last word and "afternoon" turns into a slow, rattly creak, like a door hinge or a stick dragged along a fence. The words are still there, but they get quieter and rougher as the sentence ends.

It usually appears:

  • on the last word or two of a sentence,
  • when you're talking quietly or feel relaxed,
  • when you're tired, or first thing in the morning,
  • when you're running out of breath.

Why it happens

Your voice is made by the vocal folds vibrating as air passes through them. When pitch goes very low and little air is pushing through, they vibrate slowly and unevenly, and you hear separate pops instead of a smooth tone. That's why fry often turns up as a sentence trails off: pitch is falling and the breath is nearly used up.

Examples of vocal fry

Made-up lines. The words in bold are where fry most often lands.

SituationExample line
Work update"So that's done, and I'll send it over this afternoon."
Interview answer"We finished the project two weeks early."
Presentation"And that's why we chose option B."
Voicemail"Anyway, call me back when you get a chance."
Casual agreement"Yeah, I know."
Early-morning call"Morning, everyone. Can you hear me?"

In casual chat, as in the last two rows, fry rarely gets in the way. In the first four, the creaky word carries the information.

Vocal fry vs. uptalk vs. monotone

TermWhat you hearWhere
Vocal fryA low, creaky, popping soundUsually the last word of a sentence
UptalkPitch rising, so a statement sounds like a questionThe end of statements
MonotoneLittle change in pitchWhole sentences
HoarsenessA rough or strained voiceAny time, not just at the end

How listeners may hear it

In a quiet room, face to face, many listeners won't notice. On a phone line, a video call or a recording, the creaky part is quieter and less clear, so the end of a sentence can get lost.

Some listeners judge fry. Wikipedia describes a 2014 study in which young women's voices with fry were rated less hirable than voices without it. It also notes that the fry was imitated rather than natural, a limitation linguists have criticised. Such reactions say more about listeners than about your voice; the practical point is simply to make sure your key words are heard.

How to check and reduce it

  1. Record yourself giving a one-minute update.
  2. Listen only to the last word of each sentence. Mark the ones that creak or fade.
  3. Take a breath before longer sentences, and keep a little air for the last word. See how to breathe while speaking.
  4. Split long sentences in two so you don't run out of breath at the end.
  5. Aim the last word at the back of the room, a little louder rather than lower.
  6. Record again and compare. Leave the fry in relaxed chat alone if you like it.

If your voice is often hoarse, or speaking hurts, see a doctor or a speech and language therapist.

Questions people ask

What does vocal fry mean?

It means speaking in the lowest, creakiest part of your voice, so it sounds crackly or popping, usually at the end of a sentence. It is also called creaky voice or glottal fry.

Is vocal fry bad?

It's a normal part of many people's speech. It's worth reducing only when it makes your key words hard to hear, such as on calls or in a large room.

What causes vocal fry?

Low pitch with little air behind it. That's why it shows up at the end of sentences, when you're tired, or when you're speaking quietly.

Do men use vocal fry?

Yes. Anyone can, at any age. Wikipedia notes that in 20th-century English it was most associated with male speakers of Received Pronunciation, a British accent.

Is vocal fry the same as uptalk?

No. Fry is a creaky sound at a low pitch. Uptalk is pitch rising at the end of a statement so it sounds like a question.

How do I know if I use vocal fry?

Record yourself talking for a minute and listen only to the last word of each sentence. If it crackles or rattles, that's fry.
