PREP Method: Meaning and 3 Examples (Speech, Interview)

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated November 16, 2026

The PREP method is a four-part structure for a short spoken answer: Point (your answer), Reason (why), Example (proof) and Point again (restate it). It suits opinion questions in meetings, interviews and impromptu speeches, and a full PREP answer takes about 30 to 45 seconds.

What P-R-E-P stands for

PartWhat it doesTime
PointGives your answer in one sentence, so nobody has to wait for itAbout 5 seconds
ReasonSays why you think so: one reason, your strongest5–10 seconds
ExampleProves the reason with a specific case, number or short story15–20 seconds
PointGives the answer again, often as a next step5–10 seconds

Together that is 30 to 45 seconds, or roughly 60 to 100 words. You may also hear it called the PREP technique, PREP structure or PREP framework.

The repeat at the end isn't wasted. Listeners who drifted during the example get the answer a second time, and it tells everyone you've finished.

Three short example answers

Made-up examples. Swap in your own facts.

A meeting question

Your manager asks, "Should we keep the Friday stand-up?"

Point: "I'd drop the Friday stand-up."

Reason: "By Friday most of the week's work is done, so it repeats what we already said on Thursday."

Example: "I checked the last four Fridays' notes, and three of them had no new blockers at all."

Point: "So let's drop Friday and use that 15 minutes for the weekly demo instead."

An interview question

The interviewer asks, "Do you prefer working alone or in a team?"

Point: "I prefer a team, with some quiet time built in."

Reason: "I get to a better answer faster when someone can challenge my first idea."

Example: "On my last report, a colleague spotted a gap in my data on the first day. Alone, I'd have found it a week later."

Point: "So a team first, with focused hours to do my own part."

An impromptu speech topic

You draw the topic "Early bird or night owl: which is better?"

Point: "I say early bird."

Reason: "The first hour of the day is the only one nobody else has claimed yet."

Example: "When I started getting up at six, I finished my course reading before work, instead of falling asleep over it at eleven at night."

Point: "So if there's something you keep putting off, give it the first hour of your day."

These are compact versions: each is 54 to 60 words, about 25 to 30 seconds aloud. For a longer speech, give two or three reasons, each with its own example, between the two Points.

PREP vs. STAR

PREPSTAR
Stands forPoint, Reason, Example, PointSituation, Task, Action, Result
Use it forOpinions and judgments: "What do you think?"Past experience: "Tell me about a time…"
Starts withYour answerThe background
Usual length30–45 seconds1–2 minutes

If they ask what you think, use PREP. If they ask what you did, tell the story with STAR or the shorter CAR.

When to use it

  • Opinion questions in meetings: "What do you think?"
  • Interview questions that aren't stories: "What makes a good manager?"
  • Impromptu speeches and table topics
  • Any time you're tempted to think out loud

Questions people ask

What does PREP stand for?

Point, Reason, Example, Point.

What is the PREP method in speech?

A way to structure a short speech or spoken answer: state your point, give a reason, back it with an example, then repeat the point. It suits impromptu speaking because you can build it in a few seconds.

How do I use the PREP method in an interview?

Use it for opinion and "how would you" questions. Answer in one sentence, give your reason, add one example from your own work, and restate the answer. For questions about past experience, use a story structure such as STAR.

Is PREP the same as STAR?

No. PREP leads with your opinion and uses an example as proof. STAR tells one past experience in order, from the situation to the result.

Can I give more than one reason?

Yes, but two is usually the maximum for a short answer. Give one example per reason.

What if I don't have an example ready?

Use a quick hypothetical ("Say a customer calls on Friday…") or a general observation, then return to your point.
