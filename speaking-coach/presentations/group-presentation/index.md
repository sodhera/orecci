# How to Do a Group Presentation: Split Parts and Handoffs

Split a group presentation by sections of the argument, not by slides, and give each person a part that plays to their strengths. Write a one-sentence handoff for every change of speaker that names the next person and their topic, rehearse the whole thing together at least twice, and agree before the day who answers which kinds of questions so you don't talk over each other.

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence. Updated 2026-10-22. Source: https://www.orecci.com/speaking-coach/presentations/group-presentation/

## Why group presentations feel disjointed

Most group presentations are four mini-presentations stapled together. Each person built their own slides, uses their own style, repeats the introduction, and the audience can't tell how the parts connect. The fix is to plan it as one presentation first, and only then split it.

## Step 1: agree the one message together

Before anyone opens slides, agree in one sentence what the whole presentation says. For example (made-up, for a business class): "A small café near campus can break even in its first year by targeting students in the morning and remote workers in the afternoon."

Then agree the three or four sections that prove it. Only now do you assign people.

## Step 2: split by sections, not slides

| Way to split | Result |
| --- | --- |
| By slide number ("you do 1–5, I'll do 6–10") | Parts that don't connect; awkward mid-topic handoffs |
| By section of the argument | Each person owns a clear piece; handoffs fall at natural breaks |

Match sections to strengths:

| Role | Who suits it | What they do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Opener | Your most confident speaker | Hook, the one message, introduces everyone and the outline |
| Middle sections | People who know that content best | One section each, with evidence |
| Closer | A clear, calm speaker | Pulls the sections together, repeats the message, leads into questions |
| Q&A coordinator | Someone who knows the whole thing | Directs questions to the right person |

The opener and closer can be the same person; that makes the presentation feel framed. If you have a group of five and only four sections, the fifth person can coordinate Q&A, run the slides or take a short data section.

## Step 3: give everyone the same time budget

Divide the time by section before anyone writes. For a 15-minute presentation with four people:

| Section | Speaker | Time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Opening and outline | A | 1.5 min |
| The market | B | 3.5 min |
| The plan | C | 3.5 min |
| The numbers | D | 3.5 min |
| Close | A | 1.5 min |
| Buffer | | 1.5 min |

Everyone rehearses to their own budget. If one speaker runs over by two minutes, the whole group runs over.

## Step 4: write the handoffs

Handoffs are where group presentations lose the audience. "Um, so, now Jake's going to talk" is the usual version. A good handoff does three things: closes your section, links to the next, and names the next speaker.

Example handoffs (made-up):

> "So that's who our customers are: students before ten, remote workers after two. But knowing who they are doesn't fill the seats. Priya's going to show you how we'll reach them."

> "That's the plan. The question everyone's asking is whether it pays. Daniel has the numbers."

The next speaker can start with a quick bridge back: "Thanks, Priya. So, does it pay?"

Write every handoff down and rehearse them specifically. They're short, so it takes minutes.

## Step 5: make it look like one presentation

- **One slide template**, one font, one colour scheme. One person does a final pass on all slides.
- **Don't repeat the introduction** in each section.
- **Refer to each other's parts:** "As Priya said, mornings are students…" It shows the parts connect.
- **Agree where people stand.** Speakers waiting their turn stand still, look at the speaker, and don't check their phones.
- **One person runs the clicker**, or pass it at each handoff, but decide which.

## Step 6: rehearse together, at least twice

1. **First run: content and timing.** Stop and fix anything that doesn't connect.
2. **Second run: straight through without stopping**, with handoffs and a timer.
3. **If possible, a third run in the room** or on the platform you'll use.

If you can't all meet, each person should rehearse their part and their handoffs alone, out loud and timed. Speaking Coach can listen to your section with your slides as a PDF and ask you questions afterwards, which helps when the group can't meet. For speaking without reading your notes, the tips in [how to present at school without notes](/speaking-coach/presentations/school-presentation/) apply to each section.

## Step 7: agree who answers what

Before the day, list the likely questions and assign each to a person. During Q&A:

- **The coordinator directs:** "That's a numbers question. Daniel?"
- **Only one person answers.** Others add something only if it's important and short: "Just to add one thing…"
- **Don't contradict each other in front of the audience.** If you disagree, say "Another way to look at it is…".
- **If nobody knows**, the coordinator says: "We didn't look at that, but we'd start by…".

More on the questions themselves: [how to handle Q&A](/speaking-coach/guides/how-to-handle-q-and-a/).

## Common problems and fixes

| Problem | Fix |
| --- | --- |
| One member doesn't do their part | Raise it early; agree a draft deadline two days before the final one |
| A speaker freezes | The next speaker or the coordinator steps in with a bridge: "What Sam's showing here is…" |
| Someone runs way over | Agree a signal in advance, and the closer shortens the conclusion |
| The slides look mismatched | One person does a final design pass the day before |

## Questions people ask

### How do you split a group presentation fairly?

Split it into sections of similar length and importance, then match people to sections they know or like. The opener and closer roles count as a section.

### How do you transition between speakers in a group presentation?

Close your section in one sentence, link it to the next topic, and name the next speaker: "Priya's going to show you how we'll reach them."

### Should everyone in the group speak?

Usually yes, especially in class, since many teachers grade each person. Check the brief.

### Who should answer questions in a group presentation?

Decide in advance. One person directs each question to whoever owns that topic, and only that person answers.

### What do the others do while one person presents?

Stand still, watch the speaker, and look interested. The audience notices people who look bored or check their phones.
