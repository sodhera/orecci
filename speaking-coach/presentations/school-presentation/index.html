How to Give a Good Presentation at School Without Notes

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

To present at school without notes, don't memorise a script: learn three or four key points in order, put one keyword or picture per slide as your prompt, and practice saying it out loud in different words each time. Memorise only your first and last sentences, look at your classmates rather than the teacher, and go slower than feels normal.

Why reading your notes goes wrong

When you read from a page or your slides, three things happen: you look down, your voice goes flat, and your classmates read ahead of you and stop listening. Teachers notice it too. Most presentation marking includes eye contact and whether you seem to understand the topic.

But memorising a full script word for word is worse. One forgotten word and the whole thing falls apart. The answer sits in the middle: know your points, not your sentences.

How to learn it without a script

  1. Write your presentation out in full once. This is just to work out what you think.
  2. Turn it into 3–4 key points. For a talk on volcanoes: "plates move", "magma rises", "pressure builds", "eruption types".
  3. Give each point one example or fact. "Iceland sits on two plates pulling apart."
  4. Put one keyword or picture on each slide. That's your prompt. When the slide appears, you remember what to say.
  5. Say it out loud, in your own words, five times. It'll come out slightly different each time. That's good: it means you know it, rather than having memorised it.
  6. Memorise only two sentences: your first and your last. Those are the moments nerves hit hardest.

If your teacher allows a cue card, write only the keywords, in big letters, in order. Not sentences.

A simple structure for a 3–5 minute class presentation

PartTimeWhat to sayExample
Hook15 secondsA question, a surprising fact or a short story"Who here has been on a mountain that could explode?"
Topic15 secondsWhat you're talking about and your main points"I'm going to explain how volcanoes form, in three steps."
Point 11 minuteFirst idea plus examplePlates moving apart and together
Point 21 minuteSecond idea plus exampleMagma rising
Point 31 minuteThird idea plus examplePressure and eruptions
Ending20 secondsSum up in one sentence, then thank them"So a volcano is really a pressure valve for the Earth. Thank you."

At an easy speaking pace, a 5-minute talk is roughly 600–700 words. The speech length calculator turns your word count into minutes.

Use slides as prompts, not a script

Slide that makes you readSlide that helps you talk
Six bullet points of full sentencesOne photo of a volcano and the word "pressure"
A paragraph copied from a websiteA simple diagram you explain
Your whole speech in small fontOne big number you checked, with its source in small print

If you're tempted to put a sentence on a slide, that sentence is the thing you should be saying out loud.

Dealing with nerves

Almost everyone gets nervous presenting to their class. Classmates are often a harder audience than strangers, because you'll see them tomorrow. Things that help:

  • Breathe out slowly before you start. A long, slow breath out helps many people settle before the first line.
  • Find two friendly faces in different parts of the room and talk to them. Switch between them.
  • Pause when you lose your place. Look at your slide, find the keyword, carry on. A three-second pause feels long to you; to the class it looks like you're thinking.
  • Slow down. Nerves speed you up. Aim to feel slightly too slow.
  • Don't apologise. Saying "sorry, I'm so nervous" makes everyone notice. Just keep going.

Answering questions from the class

Before the day, write down three questions someone might ask and say your answers out loud. If you get a question you can't answer, it's fine to say: "I'm not sure. I'll look it up and tell you tomorrow." That's a better answer than guessing.

How to practice at home

  • Stand up, use your slides, and talk to a wall, a pet or a family member.
  • Record one run on your phone and watch it once. Check speed and how often you look down.
  • Try a run with a listener who asks questions afterwards. Speaking Coach can play your audience: add your slides as a PDF, swipe through them as you talk, then answer the questions they ask.
  • Do one full run the night before and one in the morning. Don't cram new content on the last day.

Questions people ask

How do I not read off my slides?

Put only keywords or pictures on them, so there's nothing to read. Practice until each keyword reminds you of what to say.

Is it okay to use notes in a school presentation?

Check with your teacher. If allowed, use a small card with keywords only, and glance at it rather than reading.

What if I forget what to say?

Pause, look at your slide or card, and move to your next point. Nobody knows what you planned to say, so they won't notice a skipped line.

How do I stop my voice shaking?

Breathe out slowly before you start, and speak a little louder than usual. A shaky voice usually settles after the first 30 seconds.

How long should a school presentation be?

As long as your teacher asks, and practice to land about 15–30 seconds under. Talks usually run longer on the day than at home.
