How to Prepare a Presentation for a Job Interview (Examples)

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Start from the brief: work out the one decision or problem the panel wants to see you handle, and make your presentation an answer to it. Build 10–15 minutes around one recommendation and two or three supporting points, finish a minute early, and prepare as hard for the questions as for the talk, because the panel often learns most there.

Pick the angle before you open your slides

Most weak interview presentations fail before the first slide is made: the candidate picks a topic they're comfortable with instead of the one the panel needs. Read the brief and decide which of these four types it is. Each needs a different angle.

Brief typeTypical wordingThe angle that worksThe trap
Plan"How would you approach your first 90 days?"A short, ordered plan with what you'd learn firstPromising big changes before you know the team
Problem"Our churn is rising. What would you do?"One diagnosis, one recommendation, how you'd test itListing ten ideas with no priority
Past work"Present a project you're proud of."The decision you made and what it changedNarrating the whole project in order
Open topic"Teach us something in 10 minutes."Something useful to this team, explained simplyA hobby talk with no link to the job

Write the angle as one sentence before anything else, for example: "I'd fix onboarding before spending more on acquisition." If you can't write that sentence, you don't have a presentation yet, only a topic.

How to choose when the topic is open

"Present on any topic" is still a test of the job. Pick the topic that lets the panel see you doing the work they'd hire you for.

  • For a teaching or training role: teach a short concept, then check the panel understood it, as you would with learners.
  • For a sales or client role: present a recommendation to a customer, and ask the panel a discovery question early on.
  • For an analyst role: walk through one piece of analysis, from question to chart to "so what".
  • For a manager role: show how you'd make a decision with incomplete information.

Avoid topics that need a lot of background, that are controversial, or that you can't take questions on for 15 minutes.

A 12-minute outline you can adapt

Most interview slots are 10, 15 or 20 minutes. Here's an example outline for a 15-minute slot, aiming for about 13 minutes of talk. For a full timing table by slot length, see how to give a presentation in a job interview.

  1. The brief and your answer (1 minute). "You asked me how I'd reduce churn among new customers. My answer: fix the first 30 days of onboarding before anything else."
  2. What you understood (2 minutes). The situation as you see it, and the assumptions you made because you didn't have internal data. Saying assumptions out loud is a strength, not a weakness.
  3. Point one, the evidence (3 minutes). Why you believe the problem sits where you say it does.
  4. Point two, the plan (4 minutes). What you'd do, in what order, and what you'd measure.
  5. Risks (2 minutes). One or two things that could go wrong and how you'd notice early.
  6. Close (1 minute). Repeat the recommendation and name your first step.

Example openings by brief type

These are made-up examples to show the shape. Replace the details with your own.

A 90-day plan for a team lead role:

"You asked how I'd spend my first 90 days. In short: listen for a month, fix one thing that slows the team down in the second, and set goals together in the third. I'll go through each month, then the two risks I'd watch."

A past project for a product designer role:

"I'm going to show you one project, the redesign of a checkout flow, and focus on the one decision that mattered most: removing a step the business wanted to keep. I'll explain how I made the case, and what happened after."

An open topic for a teacher role:

"In the next ten minutes I'm going to teach you how to read a weather map well enough to plan a weekend. At the end I'll ask you two questions, the same way I'd check understanding in class."

Get ready for the panel's questions

Panels use the questions to see how you think once the script runs out. Prepare answers to these, by brief type:

Brief typeQuestions to expect
Plan"What if the team pushes back?" "What would you not do?" "How would you know it's working by day 60?"
Problem"Why that cause and not another?" "What would it cost?" "What data would change your mind?"
Past work"What would you do differently?" "What was your part, exactly?" "Who disagreed with you?"
Open topic"Why did you choose this?" "How would you adapt it for a beginner?"

When a question lands:

  1. Pause for a second, then answer in one sentence.
  2. Add one reason or example.
  3. Stop and let them follow up.
  4. If several panelists are present, answer the person who asked, then glance at the others when you finish.

If you don't know, say what you'd need to find out and how. The guide to handling Q&A covers tough and hostile questions.

A preparation timeline

WhenWhat to do
As soon as you get the briefWrite your one-sentence angle. Ask the recruiter about time, format, audience and equipment
4–5 days beforeOutline on paper, then build slides: one point per slide
3 days beforeFirst full run out loud, timed. Cut whatever ran over
2 days beforeWrite your 10 likeliest questions and say your answers out loud
The day beforeTwo full runs, one with someone asking questions afterwards

Mistakes to avoid

MistakeWhy it hurtsInstead
Choosing the safe topic over the briefThe panel scores against the briefAnswer what they asked, even if it's harder
Hiding your assumptionsIt looks like you missed the gapsName them early: "I assumed..."
Ending on "Any questions?"Weak final lineEnd on your recommendation, then invite questions
Over-designing slidesTime spent on visuals, not thinkingPlain slides with one clear title each

Questions people ask

What is a good topic for a job interview presentation?

If you can choose, pick something that shows you doing the job you applied for: a plan, a recommendation or a short lesson. It should be simple enough to explain in 10 minutes and solid enough to take questions on.

How long should a job interview presentation be?

Exactly as long as they asked, minus a minute. Most slots are 10 to 20 minutes, followed by questions.

Should I talk about myself at the start?

One sentence at most. The panel has your CV; they want to see how you handle the brief.

What if the brief is vague?

Ask the recruiter one or two clarifying questions, then state your interpretation at the start of the talk: "I've taken this to mean..."

Can I bring handouts?

Yes, a printed copy of your slides for each panelist is usually welcome. Hand it out after you finish unless they ask before.
