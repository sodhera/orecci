How to Give a Product Demo That Doesn't Bore Your Audience

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

A product demo stays interesting when it follows one realistic user through one job they care about, showing the outcome first and only the clicks needed to get there. Plan three to five 'wow moments' tied to problems the customer told you about, narrate what matters rather than every click, check in after each one, and have a recorded or screenshot backup ready for when the live demo breaks.

Why demos get boring

Most boring demos are tours: "Here's the dashboard. Up here is settings. Over here you can filter." The person giving it knows every feature and wants to show them all. The audience doesn't care about features. They care whether their problem goes away.

The fix is to stop touring and start telling a story: one person, one problem, one path to the result.

Before the demo: know their problem

A demo for a customer you haven't spoken to is guesswork. If you can, have a short discovery conversation first, or open the demo with two questions:

  • "What made you look at tools like this now?"
  • "What does that job look like for your team today?"

Write down their words. If they say "month-end takes three days", that's the story your demo tells. For more on the conversation around a demo, see how to give a sales pitch.

Structure: outcome first, then the path

StepTime (20-minute demo)What you doExample (made-up invoicing software)
1. Recap their problem1 minSay back what they told you"You said month-end takes your team three days, mostly chasing late invoices."
2. Show the end result1 minJump to the finished outcome"This is what month-end looks like with us: one screen, everything overdue already chased."
3. Walk the story10 minOne user doing the job, start to finish"Let's follow Sam, your bookkeeper, through a Monday."
4. Wow momentsinside step 33–5 moments that solve something they saidAutomatic reminders going out; a client paying from the email
5. Check inafter each momentAsk a question"Is that how Sam would want it to work?"
6. Their questions5 minAnswer, show if needed
7. Next step2 minAgree what happens next"Shall we set up a trial with your real client list?"

Showing the outcome first (step 2) changes how people watch the rest. They know where it's heading, so every click makes sense.

Narrate what matters, not every click

Weak narrationStrong narration
"So I'll click here, and then go to this dropdown, and select 'reminders'…""Sam sets this once. From now on, any invoice 7 days late gets a polite reminder automatically."
"This is our advanced filtering feature with 12 options.""Here's every client who's late more than twice this year. That's who you call first."
"As you can see…" (while the screen loads)Say the point before the click, so loading time isn't dead air

Rule of thumb: say why before you click, then let the screen show what. If you find yourself describing the user interface, you've slipped back into a tour.

Set up a demo that can't embarrass you

  • Use realistic data, in the customer's industry if possible. "Acme Test Client 1" makes people stop imagining it's theirs.
  • Pre-load every screen and log in before the call. Close notifications, chats and email.
  • Make the text bigger. Zoom your browser so people on small screens or the back of a room can read it.
  • Have a reset plan. Know how to get back to the start state in seconds.
  • Keep a backup: a short screen recording or a set of screenshots of the key moments.

When the demo breaks

It will, eventually. How you handle it matters more than the bug.

  1. Name it calmly. "That's not what should happen. Let me show you what it normally looks like."
  2. Don't debug live for more than a few seconds. Nobody wants to watch you search logs.
  3. Switch to your backup (recording or screenshots) and keep telling the story.
  4. Follow up afterwards with a short note on what happened, if it's relevant to them.

Treating a failure with calm humour often builds more trust than a flawless demo, because it shows how you'd behave when something goes wrong for them.

Handling questions mid-demo

Let people interrupt. Questions mean they're imagining using it.

  • Answer briefly and keep the thread: "Yes, it handles multiple currencies. I'll show you that right after this step."
  • If it's a feature you don't have, say so directly, then ask what they'd need it for. Often there's another way to solve the underlying problem.
  • Park deep technical questions: "Good question for our engineer. Can I set up 20 minutes with them this week?"

Rehearsing the talk, not just the clicks

Most people rehearse the clicks and improvise the words, which is why demos ramble. Do at least two runs out loud with someone playing a customer who interrupts. Speaking Coach can play that customer if you describe the situation, and afterwards shows your pace and filler words, which tend to pile up while you wait for screens to load.

Questions people ask

How long should a product demo be?

Often 15–30 minutes inside a longer meeting, with time left for questions. A first demo can be shorter; show the outcome and two or three key moments.

How do I make a software demo engaging?

Follow one realistic user through a job the audience cares about, show the result first, and check in with a question after each key moment.

What should I do if the demo crashes?

Say so calmly, switch to a recording or screenshots, and continue the story. Don't try to fix it live.

Should I let people interrupt my demo?

Yes. Questions show interest. Answer briefly and come back to the story.

How do I demo to a technical audience?

Show more of the real system and fewer slides, but still follow one story. Explain why it works the way it does; see how to explain complex ideas simply.
