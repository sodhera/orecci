How to Present to Executives or a Board: Get to the Point

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Open with the bottom line: the decision you need, or the one thing they must know, in your first sentence. Then give only the two or three facts that support it, a clear recommendation and the risks, and be ready to be interrupted at any point. Plan for half your time to become questions, and answer each one in a sentence before adding detail.

Why executives want it differently

Senior leaders and board members see a lot of material in a short time. They usually read the papers in advance, have limited time on each item, and are judging one thing: what do we need to decide or watch, and can we trust this person's judgement? That leads to three rules:

  1. Start with the conclusion, not the background.
  2. Bring a recommendation, not a menu of options with no view.
  3. Assume they'll interrupt, and make that easy.

This is often called BLUF, bottom line up front, or the pyramid principle: the answer first, then the supporting points, then detail only on request.

The first 30 seconds

Say what you need and why, in plain words. Made-up examples:

"I'm asking for approval to hire three more support staff this quarter. Without them, our response times will miss the contract terms with our two largest customers by January."

"I want to update you on one risk: our main supplier may raise prices 15% next year. I'm not asking for a decision today, but I'd like your view on whether we start looking for a second supplier now."

If they stopped you after that sentence, would they know what you want? If yes, it's a good opening.

A one-page story

Build the whole thing so it fits on one page. If you can't summarise it on one page, it isn't ready.

Notice the questions take half the slot. That's normal. If they don't ask, you finish early, which nobody minds.

Slides and papers

  • Put the full detail in the pre-read or an appendix. The presentation is the summary.
  • Each slide's title is its message. "Response times will breach contract by January" rather than "Support metrics".
  • Few slides. Many board items need three to five.
  • One number per chart that matters, highlighted.
  • Don't re-present the pre-read. Assume they've read it, and say so: "You'll have seen the paper, so I'll focus on the decision."

Handling interruptions

Interruptions are not a sign you're failing. They mean someone is engaged, or that they've already got the point and want to move on.

  • Answer the question right then, even if it's on a later slide. "Good question. The short answer is yes; it's on slide 4 if you want the detail."
  • Don't insist on finishing your deck. If the conversation has moved to the decision, go there.
  • If you lose the thread, return with your headline: "To come back to the ask…"

Short answers to tough questions

Executive questions are often blunt: "Why should we believe this forecast?" "What happens if we say no?" "Whose fault is this?" Use a simple pattern:

  1. Answer in one sentence. "Because it's based on the last six months of actual volume, not projections."
  2. Give one supporting fact.
  3. Stop. Let them ask for more.

How to sound senior in the room

  • Fewer words, more pauses. Short sentences sound more certain than long ones.
  • Drop the hedges: "I just wanted to", "sort of", "maybe we could possibly". Say "I recommend".
  • Own bad news. Say it early and plainly, with what you're doing about it.
  • Don't oversell. Executives trust people who state limits.

Rehearse the questions, not just the talk

Most people rehearse the slides and improvise the Q&A, which is backwards for a board. Write down the ten hardest questions you could get and say your answers out loud, each in under 30 seconds. Questions people ask

How long should a board presentation be?

Often 10–20 minutes per item, including questions. Plan to speak for about half that and leave the rest for discussion.

How many slides for a board presentation?

Usually three to five main slides, with detail in an appendix or the pre-read.

What if a board member challenges me aggressively?

Stay calm, answer the question directly, and don't take it personally. Challenging is part of their job.

Should I present options or a recommendation?

A recommendation, with one or two alternatives you considered. Boards want your judgement, not just choices.

How do I prepare for executive questions?

List the ten hardest questions, write one-sentence answers, and practice them out loud. Ask a senior colleague what this group usually focuses on.
