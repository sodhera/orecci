How to Pitch to Investors: What to Say in Your Startup Pitch

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Open with one sentence that says what your company does and for whom, then cover the problem, your solution, why now, traction, the business model, the team and the ask, in that rough order. Spend the most time on whatever is strongest, usually traction, keep the spoken pitch to the slot you're given, and expect the meeting to turn into questions early: short, direct answers matter more than polished slides.

Start with one sentence anyone can repeat

Investors hear many pitches. The first thing they need is to know what you do, in words they could repeat to a partner later. Before you build a single slide, write this sentence:

"We make [product] for [specific customer] so they can [outcome]."

Example with made-up details: "We make scheduling software for independent physiotherapy clinics, so they stop losing a day a week to phone bookings."

If you say this in the first 15 seconds, everything after it has a hook. If you open with your origin story or the size of the market, the investor spends two minutes trying to work out what you sell.

What to say, in order

Many investor pitches follow this order. Adjust it so your strongest section comes early.

SectionWhat to say out loudRough time in a 10-minute pitch
One-linerWhat you do, for whom15 seconds
ProblemA specific customer, the pain, what it costs them1 minute
SolutionWhat you built and how it removes the pain; a quick demo or screenshot1.5 minutes
Why nowWhat changed that makes this possible or urgent today30 seconds
TractionRevenue, users, growth, pilots, retention: whatever is real and strongest2 minutes
MarketWho buys this and how many of them there are, built from the bottom up1 minute
Business modelHow you make money and what a customer is worth1 minute
CompetitionWho else solves this and why customers pick you1 minute
TeamWhy you are the people to build this1 minute
The askHow much you're raising and what it gets you to30 seconds

If you have strong traction, move it to right after the one-liner: "We launched eight months ago and 140 clinics now pay us monthly." Lead with your best evidence.

A spoken example of the first minute

A made-up example, to show how it sounds when spoken rather than read off slides:

"We make scheduling software for independent physiotherapy clinics. Most of these clinics have one receptionist, and in the clinics we've talked to, she spends most of her morning on the phone moving appointments. Every missed call is a missed booking. Our app lets patients book and reschedule themselves, and it fills cancelled slots from a waitlist automatically. We launched in March. 140 clinics pay us, and none of the first 30 has left."

Notice the details: one customer type, one person with the problem, a concrete outcome, real numbers. Say numbers slowly and once. Investors write them down.

Delivery: how it should sound

  • Talk like you're explaining it across a table, not presenting on stage. Most early pitches happen in small rooms or on video calls.
  • Know your numbers cold. Hesitating over your own revenue or churn is noticed more than any slide design.
  • Stop at the end of each point. A one-second pause lets an investor jump in, and questions are a good sign.
  • Don't read your slides. The slide shows the chart; you say what it means.
  • Plan for being interrupted. Know which three slides you'd keep if you only got five minutes.

You can check your pace with the pace checker. Nervous founders often speed up during traction, which is exactly where you want to slow down.

Investor questions to prepare for

Questions often start before you finish. Write answers to these and say each one out loud in under 30 seconds:

TopicQuestions you'll hear
Market"How big can this get?" "Who's the buyer, exactly?"
Competition"Why won't a big company just build this?" "What do customers use today?"
Traction"What's your retention?" "How did you get your first customers?" "What's growth month on month?"
Economics"What does it cost to acquire a customer?" "What's the gross margin?"
Team"Why you?" "Who's missing from the team?"
The round"What will you achieve with this money?" "Who else is in the round?"

How to answer:

  1. Answer the question asked, first. "Retention is 92% after six months." Then add context if needed.
  2. If the number is weak, say so and explain the plan. Spin is easy to spot.
  3. If you don't know, say you'll follow up, and actually do it within a day.
  4. Treat "Why won't Google do this?" as a normal question, not an attack. Have a calm, specific answer.

Mistakes to avoid

MistakeWhy it hurtsInstead
A huge top-down market numberIt sounds like you haven't thought about real buyersCount real customers times what they'd pay
"We have no competitors"Investors hear "no market" or "hasn't looked"Name the alternatives, including spreadsheets and doing nothing
A vague askThey can't tell what stage you're at"We're raising X to reach Y by Z"
Too many slidesYou rush and run over10–15 slides, one point each
Arguing with feedbackSignals you'll be hard to work withThank them, ask a follow-up, decide later

Questions people ask

How long should an investor pitch be?

Use the time you're given. Many first meetings run 20–30 minutes, with 5–10 minutes of pitch and the rest questions. Have a 1-minute, 3-minute and 10-minute version ready.

How many slides should a pitch deck have?

Around 10 to 15 is common. Each slide should make one point you could say in a sentence; see what a pitch deck is.

Should I memorise my pitch?

Memorise the first minute and your key numbers. Know the rest as a sequence of points so you can jump around when investors interrupt.

What do I do if an investor interrupts me early?

Answer the question, then decide whether to continue in order or follow their interest. An interruption usually means they're engaged.

How do I end an investor pitch?

State the ask clearly, then say what you'd like to happen next, for example a follow-up meeting with a partner or a look at your data room.
