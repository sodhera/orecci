Respiratory therapist interview questions and how to answer them

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Respiratory therapist interviews test what you do when breathing is at stake: how you troubleshoot a ventilator alarm, what you do first at a rapid response, how you teach a patient to use an inhaler or home oxygen, and how you work with nurses and physicians in the ICU. Answer with a real patient, say what you assessed first, and finish with how the patient did.

What the respiratory care manager is listening for

Respiratory therapy interviews often include a panel with the department manager and a lead therapist, and many include a scenario question. They want to hear a safe, systematic approach under pressure, clear communication with the team and patient education that sticks. Say what you'd do first, and always say when you'd call for help.

Your answers should match your training and the hospital's protocols; it's fine to say "per our protocol" and then explain the usual steps.

Ventilator scenarios

The classic question: "The high-pressure alarm is going off. What do you do?" The panel is listening for one thing above all: you look at the patient before the machine.

  1. Look at the patient. Color, chest rise, distress, oxygen saturation.
  2. If the patient is in distress and the cause isn't obvious, take them off the ventilator and support breathing manually with a bag, per protocol, and call for help.
  3. Work through the common causes. Many therapists use the DOPE checklist: displacement of the tube, obstruction (secretions, a kinked tube, biting), pneumothorax, equipment failure.
  4. Fix what you find. Suction, reposition, replace equipment, and alert the physician if you suspect a pneumothorax.
  5. Document and communicate what happened and what you changed.

Example line: "Silencing an alarm without finding the cause isn't fixing it."

Rapid response and codes

Tell one real call in order, focusing on your role.

Example answer (made-up details):

"I was called to a rapid response on a med-surg floor for a post-op patient with oxygen saturation in the low 80s on a nasal cannula. I assessed her breath sounds, which were diminished at the bases, and increased her oxygen per protocol while the nurse got vitals. I gave the physician a quick summary when he arrived and suggested we consider noninvasive ventilation. He agreed, I set it up and stayed at the bedside until her saturation stabilized above 92%. She went to step-down, and I followed up on my next shift."

What works: what you assessed, what you did within your scope, what you recommended and how you followed up.

Patient education

RTs do a lot of teaching: inhalers, spacers, incentive spirometers, home oxygen. Show a method.

StepWhat you could say
Ask first"Can you show me how you use your inhaler at home?"
Demonstrate"I show it once slowly with a placebo device, saying each step."
Teach back"Now you show me, and tell me why you hold your breath."
Keep it short"Two or three key points per session, not ten."
Involve family"For home oxygen, I teach whoever will help at home, including fire safety."

A line that lands: "Watching patients use their inhaler tells me more than asking if they use it."

Teamwork in the ICU

Expect a question about working with nurses and physicians, often on weaning or a disagreement. Show that you bring data and speak up respectfully.

Example answer:

"During rounds I suggested a spontaneous breathing trial for a patient whose numbers met our weaning criteria. The resident wanted to wait another day. I shared the overnight values and offered to run the trial with close monitoring and stop at the first sign of trouble. The attending agreed. The patient passed the trial and was extubated that afternoon. When a physician says no, I ask what they're worried about, because sometimes they know something I don't."

Questions people ask

How do I answer scenario questions as a new graduate?

Talk through your reasoning step by step, mention protocols and say when you'd escalate. Use clinical rotation patients for the story questions.

How long should my answer to a ventilator scenario be?

About one to two minutes, in clear steps. Stop and let the panel add a complication; they often will.

Should I mention end-of-life care?

If asked, yes. Talk about supporting the family, working with the team during withdrawal of support, and how you look after yourself afterwards.

What should I ask at the end?

Ask about the patient mix, therapist-to-patient ratios, whether therapists follow protocols independently, and how orientation works in the ICU.
