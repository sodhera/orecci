# Airline pilot interview questions and how to answer them

Airline pilot interviews ask why this airline, how you handle crew resource management problems, what you would do if you disagreed with the captain, how you make decisions under pressure, and technical questions you must answer out loud. Show that you speak up clearly and early, use the whole crew, follow procedures, and can explain technical points simply without rushing.

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence. Updated 2026-10-22. Source: https://www.orecci.com/speaking-coach/interview-questions-by-role/pilot/

## What the panel is listening for

Airline interviews often include a technical portion, a simulator check and a behavioural interview with a captain and someone from HR or recruitment. The exact format depends on the airline and whether you're applying as a cadet, a direct-entry first officer or a captain.

In the behavioural part, nobody is testing whether you can fly. They're testing whether they'd want you in the other seat at 3 a.m. with a problem developing. Every answer should show: you speak up, you listen, you follow procedures, and you put the safety of the flight above your ego or anyone else's.

## The disagreement with the captain

This is the question pilots prepare most, and for good reason. A typical version: *you're the first officer, the approach is becoming unstable, and the captain wants to continue.*

The panel wants to hear an escalating, respectful, clear sequence. Many pilots describe it as moving from a question, to a statement of concern, to a direct call, using the procedure their operator trains.

Example answer for a first officer:

> "I'd say what I see first, with numbers: 'We're 15 knots fast and still high at 800 feet.' If the captain carried on, I'd state my concern clearly: 'I'm not comfortable, this doesn't meet our stable approach criteria.' If we reached the company's gate and it still wasn't stable, I'd call 'Go around', as our procedures require. Afterwards, on the ground, I'd want to talk it through properly, because the relationship matters for the rest of the trip. But in the moment, the procedure decides, not seniority."

Avoid two extremes: staying quiet to keep the peace, and making it a personal battle. If you have a real story, from training or a previous operator, use it, with a calm debrief at the end.

## CRM scenarios

Crew resource management questions test how you use everyone available: the other pilot, cabin crew, dispatch, ATC and maintenance. Typical prompts:

- A cabin crew member reports a passenger feeling unwell mid-flight.
- You have a minor technical fault and weather deteriorating at your destination.
- A new captain is quiet and you sense the briefing missed something.

A strong answer names who you'd involve and what you'd ask them, then how you'd decide. Many pilots use a decision-making model taught by their operator or training school; if you use one, name it and walk through it briefly. Panels like a structure, but they want to hear the thinking, not a recited acronym.

Example answer for the unwell passenger:

> "I'd get the purser's full picture first: symptoms, whether there's a medical professional on board. The captain and I would share the workload: one flies and handles ATC, the other works the problem with the cabin and any medical advice service the company uses. Then we'd weigh diversion options against fuel, weather and the medical urgency, and decide together, with the captain making the final call."

## Technical knowledge spoken aloud

Some panels ask you to explain something technical out loud: an aircraft system, a weather phenomenon, performance limits. Knowing it on paper isn't the same as saying it clearly under pressure.

| Common problem | Instead |
| --- | --- |
| Starting with a long definition | Give the one-sentence answer, then the detail |
| Rushing to show you know it | Slow down; a measured pace sounds confident |
| Freezing when you don't know | "I'm not certain. I'd check the manual, but my understanding is…" |
| Bluffing | Panels probe until bluffs break, and it damages trust |

Practise five technical explanations out loud and time them at about a minute each. A [pace check](/speaking-coach/tools/pace-checker/) can show if nerves speed you up.

## Decision-making and mistakes

"Tell me about a mistake" is a test of honesty. Pick a real one from training or flying that was caught, explain how, and what you changed. A pilot who claims never to have made a mistake is a worry, not a strength.

## Why this airline?

Talk about the operation: route structure, fleet, base, training culture, and what you know from people who fly there. Avoid making it only about pay or the aircraft type.

## How to prepare

1. Write five stories: disagreement, mistake, decision under pressure, teamwork, a difficult person.
2. Rehearse the captain disagreement sequence until it sounds natural, not recited.
3. Practise explaining five technical topics out loud.
4. Prepare questions about the airline's training and line operations.
5. Run the scenarios with someone who pushes back. Speaking Coach can play the panel and follow up on exactly what you said.

## Questions people ask

### How technical are airline pilot interviews?

It depends on the airline and the role. Cadet interviews focus more on aptitude and motivation; experienced-pilot interviews often include aircraft systems, performance and procedures. Check what your airline's recruitment pages say.

### What if I have no story about disagreeing with a captain?

Use training: an instructor, a sim partner, or a group decision. Then add how you'd handle it on the line. Be honest that it's from training.

### Should I use a decision-making acronym?

If your training used one, yes, briefly. Then show your real reasoning with the facts of the scenario.

### How do I answer questions about fatigue?

Be honest that it's real, describe what you actually do to manage it, and say you'd use your operator's fatigue reporting if you weren't fit to fly.

### How long should behavioural answers be?

Aim for about 90 seconds to two minutes. Leave room for the panel's follow-up questions.
