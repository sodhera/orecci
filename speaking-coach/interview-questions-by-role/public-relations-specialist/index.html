Public relations specialist interview questions and answers

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

PR interviews test how you handle a crisis in the first hours, whether you can pitch a story to a journalist in 30 seconds, how you recover when coverage goes wrong and how you show that coverage mattered. Expect to pitch live. Answer with a real campaign or crisis, the steps you took in order and a measure of the result beyond clip counts.

Why PR interviews feel like a performance

In most jobs, the interview is about the work. In PR, the interview is the work: talking clearly under pressure, getting to the point and making someone care. Interviewers notice how you pitch yourself as much as what you say about pitching.

Read the company's recent coverage and know who writes about their industry. "Which journalists cover us?" is a common question, and blanking on it is hard to recover from.

Pitching journalists

Many interviews include a live pitch. Keep it to about 30 seconds: the news, why it matters to that outlet's readers and what you can offer.

Example pitch (made-up details):

"Hi Sam, I know you cover small business finance. A survey our client ran of 500 local café owners found most of them are now paying more in card fees than in rent. I've got the full data, a café owner in your city who'll talk on the record and the CEO available Thursday. Would that work for a piece on rising costs?"

If they push back ("Why would my readers care?"), don't repeat the pitch louder. Give them a new angle or a local hook. The elevator pitch guide helps with keeping it short.

Crisis communication

Interviewers want an order of operations, not panic and not spin.

  1. Get the facts. What happened, who's affected, what's confirmed and what isn't.
  2. Assemble the team. Leadership, legal, operations and whoever owns the issue.
  3. Issue a holding statement quickly. Acknowledge it, say what you're doing and when you'll update.
  4. Prepare the spokesperson with key messages and the hardest questions.
  5. Update on time, even when there's little new to say.
  6. Review afterward what you'd do differently.

"When a client's app leaked some users' email addresses, the first hour was about facts: how many people, what data, was it fixed. We put out a short statement within two hours acknowledging it, explaining that passwords weren't affected and saying when affected users would be emailed. I prepped the CTO with the five hardest questions. Coverage focused on the fast response rather than the leak."

A story that went wrong

Show calm, facts and relationships. Don't attack the journalist.

"A trade outlet reported our client's funding as twice the real amount. It looked good, but it was wrong and would be quoted elsewhere. I emailed the reporter within the hour, thanked her for the piece and sent the press release with the correct figure. She updated it the same day. Because we kept it friendly, she covered the client again later."

If the story was negative but accurate, say how you helped the client respond and what you changed.

Measuring coverage

Show you go beyond counting clips.

  • Quality: did the coverage reach the outlets your audience reads?
  • Messages: did the key messages appear?
  • Share of voice: compared with competitors on the same topic.
  • Action: website visits, sign-ups or enquiries during the coverage period.
  • Relationships: new journalists who now come to you.

Mistakes to avoid

  • A rambling live pitch. Lead with the news, finish within 30 seconds.
  • Spin in the crisis answer. Interviewers want honesty and speed.
  • Blaming journalists. You'll work with them again.
  • Reporting only volume. Ten irrelevant clips aren't a result.

Questions people ask

How do I prepare for a live pitch in the interview?

Pick a real story from the company or a client, write the news in one sentence and say it out loud until it takes under 30 seconds.

What if I don't have crisis experience?

Use a smaller version: a complaint that went public, a negative review, an error you corrected. Walk through the same steps.

Is agency PR interviewing different from in-house?

Agencies focus more on pitching, juggling clients and new business. In-house roles focus more on knowing the company, working with leadership and long-term reputation.

What should I ask at the end?

Ask which outlets matter most to them, how PR success is measured and how a recent crisis or negative story was handled. Interviews can feel deliberately tough; see stress interviews.
