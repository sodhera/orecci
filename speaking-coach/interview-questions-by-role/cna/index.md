# CNA interview questions: what they ask and how to answer

CNA interviews in nursing homes and long-term care ask how you run a shift, what changes in a resident you would report to the nurse, how you handle a combative resident, and how you cope when you have more residents than time. Answer with the steps you actually take, put safety first, and show you tell the nurse quickly and specifically.

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence. Updated 2026-10-22. Source: https://www.orecci.com/speaking-coach/interview-questions-by-role/cna/

## What the director of nursing is checking

A CNA interview in long-term care is usually with the director or assistant director of nursing, sometimes with a unit nurse. They are hiring someone who will be alone with residents for most of the shift. Their questions boil down to: do you know the routine, will you notice when something is wrong, and will you stay safe and kind on a hard day?

If you're newly certified, use your clinical hours. A real moment from clinicals beats a textbook answer.

## Walk me through your shift

This is often the first real question. Give a short, ordered routine, not every task.

Example answer:

> "I start by getting report from the off-going aide and checking my assignment sheet, so I know who's a fall risk, who's on a special diet and who needs two-person transfers. Then I do quick rounds to lay eyes on everyone. Morning care comes next, starting with residents who have early appointments or need help first. I take vitals when they're due, help with breakfast, and chart as I go rather than saving it all for the end. Before I leave, I give a clear report to the next shift."

## Reporting changes to the nurse

Interviewers want to hear that you know what's worth reporting and that you report it right away, in plain facts. Name examples.

| What you notice | What you'd say to the nurse |
| --- | --- |
| New confusion | "Mrs. Lee didn't know where she was this morning. Yesterday she was chatting normally." |
| Skin change | "There's a red area on Mr. Park's left heel that doesn't fade when I press it." |
| Not eating or drinking | "She's eaten about a quarter of her meals today and hardly drank." |
| Pain | "He grimaces when I turn him and said his hip hurts, which is new." |
| Vitals out of range | "His blood pressure is much higher than his usual readings. I rechecked it." |

Example answer:

> "Anything that's different from that resident's normal, I report straight away, even if I'm not sure it matters. I tell the nurse what I saw, when, and how it's different from usual, then I write it down. It's the nurse's job to decide what it means, but it's my job to make sure they know."

## A combative resident

*"A resident with dementia hits out at you during morning care."* They want safety for both of you and no force.

Example answer:

> "I'd step back out of reach first and make sure the resident is safe, for example not about to fall. I wouldn't raise my voice or keep trying to finish the task. Often the care itself is what frightened them. I'd try again in a few minutes, explaining each step, or ask a coworker the resident responds better to. I'd tell the nurse, because it could be pain or an infection, and I'd document what happened and what helped."

## When the assignment is too heavy

Short staffing is real, and directors know it. Saying "I always get everything done" is less believable than a plan.

Example answer:

> "I'd prioritize safety: residents who are fall risks, who need toileting, or who are waiting on a transfer come first. Things like changing bed linens can move later. I'd tell the charge nurse early, not at the end of the shift, so they can reassign or help. And I'd ask the aide next to me if we can do two-person transfers together to save time."

The general version is on [how do you prioritize your work](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/how-do-you-prioritize-your-work/).

## Scope and abuse questions

Two questions catch people out.

- **"A resident asks you to give them their pills."** Say it's outside a CNA's role and you'd tell the nurse right away, then let the resident know the nurse is coming.
- **"You see a coworker being rough with a resident."** Say you would step in to keep the resident safe, then report it to the nurse or supervisor immediately, following the facility's reporting policy. Don't say you'd "have a word" with the coworker first and leave it there.

## How to prepare

1. Write your shift routine in six steps and practice saying it in under a minute.
2. Pick three real moments from work or clinicals: a change you reported, a resident who resisted care, a heavy day.
3. Practice the scope and abuse questions until your answer is instant. Hesitation on those sounds like doubt.
4. Say everything out loud. Speaking Coach's interviewer follows up on what you said, such as "What did the nurse do next?", which is how real interviews go.

## Questions people ask

### I'm a new CNA. What experience do I talk about?

Your clinical rotation, any family caregiving and any job with people, such as retail or food service. Be specific about what you did, not what you learned.

### How do I answer "Why did you become a CNA?"

Give one real reason, a person or a moment, and say what you like about the daily work. The [why did you choose this career](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/why-did-you-choose-this-career/) guide has a simple structure. If you plan to become a nurse, say so; many facilities like that.

### Should I mention I want to go to nursing school?

Yes, briefly. Then make clear you're committed to doing this job well now, and ask whether they support schedules for students.

### What if I've never had a combative resident?

Say what you would do, step by step, and mention what your training taught you. Then use a related story, such as calming an upset person in another job.

### Do they ask about night shift?

Often. Be honest about your availability and, if you'd work nights, say what you know about night duties such as rounds and repositioning.
