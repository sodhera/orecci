HVAC technician interview questions and how to answer them

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

HVAC technician interviews ask how you'd diagnose a no-heat call, how you handle refrigerant, how you recommend extra work without pressuring customers, and how you feel about on-call shifts. Walk through diagnostics in order, be clear about which EPA 608 certification you hold, and show you recommend repairs a customer actually needs.

What the service manager is listening for

HVAC companies need techs who can find the fault on the first visit, handle refrigerant legally, and leave the customer feeling looked after, not sold to. Your answers should show a clear diagnostic process, the right certification, and honesty with customers.

Diagnosing a no-heat call

This is the classic interview question. Show you start simple and work through the system in order, instead of jumping to the most expensive part.

Example answer for a residential tech, gas furnace:

"First I'd ask the customer what happened: did it stop suddenly, any noises, any error lights. Then I'd start simple: is the thermostat calling for heat, are the batteries good, is the breaker on, is the furnace switch on, is the filter blocked. If all that's fine, I'd read the control board's fault code and watch the sequence: inducer, pressure switch, ignition, flame sensing. If it lights then drops out after a few seconds, I'd suspect the flame sensor, so I'd test it and clean or replace it. Then I'd run a full cycle to confirm it heats and shuts off properly."

Notice the order: talk to the customer, check the simple things, read the codes, follow the sequence, test before replacing, confirm the fix. If you're asked about heat pumps or boilers, use the same pattern.

Refrigerant handling

The EPA requires technicians who work on equipment that could release ozone-depleting refrigerants to be certified under Section 608. The EPA lists four types: Type I for small appliances, Type II for high- and very high-pressure appliances, Type III for low-pressure appliances, and Universal for all of them. Say exactly which one you hold.

"I hold Universal 608. On every job I recover refrigerant into a proper recovery cylinder, never vent it, and I keep the records my company requires. I check for leaks before topping up a system. If a system keeps needing refrigerant, the honest answer is to find and fix the leak, not keep charging it."

Interviewers react badly to anything that sounds like venting refrigerant or topping up a leaking system again and again without telling the customer.

Upselling honestly

Most service companies want techs who can recommend extra work or replacements. The question is whether you can do it without pressure. Show you base recommendations on what you found, and give options.

Pushy versionHonest version
"You really need a new system today.""Your heat exchanger has a crack, which is a safety issue. I can't leave it running. Here are your options and prices."
"Everyone's getting this upgrade.""Your system works, but it's 18 years old. When you're ready to plan a replacement, here's what I'd consider."
"I'll throw in a discount if you sign now.""Here's the quote. Take your time, and call me if you have questions."

"I separate what's a safety issue, what needs fixing now, and what's worth planning for. Customers buy more from techs they trust, and they call them back."

On-call work

Most service roles include on-call rotation. Be clear about what you can do.

"I'm fine with on-call. I've done one week in four at my current job, nights and weekends. I keep my van stocked, so I can usually fix it on the first visit, and I'm honest on the phone about when I can arrive. In a heatwave or cold snap, I know people are stressed, so I keep them updated if I'm running late."

Mistakes to avoid

  • Jumping straight to "replace the board" without describing your tests.
  • Being vague about your 608 certification.
  • Talking about sales targets more than customer needs.
  • Saying you'd rather not do on-call when the job ad mentions it.

Talk through a no-heat and a no-cool call out loud before the interview, and keep each under two minutes. The interview answer review shows your pace and pauses. Speaking Coach can play the service manager and ask "what if the flame sensor's fine?" so you practise the next step.

Questions people ask

What do entry-level HVAC interviews ask?

Why HVAC, your training, whether you hold or are studying for your 608, your mechanical and electrical basics, and whether you're happy in attics, crawl spaces and bad weather.

Should I mention commercial or residential experience?

Yes. Say which systems you've worked on, residential or commercial, and which brands. Be clear about what you haven't done yet.

How do I answer questions about sales or commission?

Say you're comfortable recommending work based on what you find, and that you give customers options with clear prices. Avoid sounding like you'd sell things people don't need.

What should I ask the employer?

Ask about the on-call rotation, van stock, training on new equipment, and how they handle callbacks.
