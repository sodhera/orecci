# Manager interview questions and how to answer them

Manager interviews ask how you lead people, not how good you are at the work: your management style, how you handled someone underperforming, a decision your team didn't like and what you delegate. Answer with real people and real outcomes, say what you did when it got uncomfortable, and show that you can be both kind and clear.

## The shift interviewers listen for

For a people-leader role, the panel is listening for one shift: from "I did the work" to "I got the work done through others." Candidates who talk mostly about their own output, however impressive, sound like they're applying for their old job.

Every answer below should have a person in it (anonymized), a moment where it got uncomfortable and what changed for the team.

## "What is your management style?"

Don't answer with a label like "servant leader" and stop. Give two or three behaviors, then a quick example.

> "I set very clear outcomes and then stay out of the way on how people get there. I meet each person one-to-one every week for 30 minutes, and they set the agenda. And I say hard things early: if something's off, people hear it from me that week, not at review time. When I took over a team of eight at a logistics company, two of them told me later the weekly one-to-ones were the first time a manager had asked what they wanted to work on."

Then be ready for "What's the downside of your style?" A fair answer: "I can give people too much room. I now agree check-in points on bigger pieces of work." For more, see [how to describe your leadership style](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/what-is-your-leadership-style/).

## An underperformer

This is the most telling manager question. Interviewers want fairness and follow-through: you found the cause, set clear expectations, supported, and acted if it didn't improve.

| Step | What a strong answer includes |
| --- | --- |
| Notice | What you saw, with facts, and how long it had been going on |
| Ask | A private conversation to find the cause before judging |
| Agree | Specific expectations and a timeline, in writing |
| Support | What you changed: training, workload, more check-ins |
| Decide | What happened, including if it ended in a move or exit |

Example answer (made-up details):

> "One of my account coordinators started missing client deadlines, three in a month after two good years. I asked her privately what had changed. She'd taken on a big new client without dropping anything else and didn't want to say she was struggling. We moved two smaller accounts to a colleague and agreed she'd flag any deadline at risk 48 hours ahead. She hit every deadline over the next quarter. My lesson was to check workload before assuming it's a performance problem."

Have a second example ready where it *didn't* work and you had to move someone on, handled respectfully and with HR. Interviewers sometimes ask for it.

## A decision your team disliked

Interviewers are checking whether you can hold an unpopular decision without hiding behind your boss, and whether you listen.

> "Our company moved my team from fully remote to three office days a week. I didn't make that call, but I owned it with my team rather than blaming leadership. I explained the reasons I'd been given, then asked what would make it workable. We agreed the same three days for everyone so office time was spent together, and I kept Friday meeting-free. Two people were still unhappy, and I told them honestly it wasn't going to change. One stayed and one left within six months, and I gave her a strong reference."

Being honest that not everyone came round makes the answer more credible.

## Delegation

Say what you delegate, how you choose who, and how you check in without micromanaging.

- **Delegate outcomes, not tasks:** "Get the new supplier onboarded by the 15th" rather than "fill in this form."
- **Match to growth:** give stretch work to the person who wants to grow in that area.
- **Agree the check-ins up front** so you aren't hovering.
- **Keep what only you can do:** hiring decisions, difficult conversations, decisions above the team's authority.

> "I delegated our quarterly budget pack to a senior analyst who wanted to move toward management. We agreed two check-ins and I reviewed the final draft. She presented it to the director herself, and did it alone the next quarter."

## Mistakes to avoid

- **All "I," no team.** Talk about what the team achieved and your role in it.
- **Only success stories.** Leaders who never misjudged anything don't sound real.
- **Blaming senior leadership** for unpopular decisions.
- **Labels without behavior.** "Collaborative" means nothing without an example.

The underperformer conversation is hard to describe well if you've never practiced saying it. Speaking Coach has a "Give tough feedback" scene where an AI partner plays your team member and reacts to what you actually say.

## Questions people ask

### What if this is my first management role?

Use moments where you led without the title: training new starters, leading a project, covering for your manager. Be honest that it's your first formal role and say how you'd learn.

### How do I answer "Tell me about a time you fired someone"?

Focus on the process: clear expectations, support, documentation and treating the person with respect. Never criticize the person you let go.

### What's the difference between manager and supervisor interviews?

Supervisor interviews focus on frontline issues: shifts, rules and daily coaching. Manager interviews focus more on goals, budgets and developing people. See [supervisor interview questions](/speaking-coach/interview-questions-by-role/supervisor/).

### How long should my answers be?

Around 90 seconds to two minutes for stories. Manager interviews often have follow-ups, so stop at the result and let them dig.
