Police officer interview questions and how to answer them

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

A police oral board asks why you want the job, what you would do about a colleague's misconduct, how you calm people down, and how you handle stress. Give a real example wherever you have one, say exactly what you would do and who you would tell, and never bend on honesty: the ethics scenarios are built to see whether you will.

How the oral board works

The oral board is the spoken part of police hiring, separate from the written and physical tests. A small panel, often officers of different ranks and sometimes a civilian, asks every candidate the same questions and scores answers against set criteria.

The panel can only score what you say, not what you meant, so say your actions out loud and in order. Answer the whole panel, not just the person who asked; the panel interview guide covers how.

Why do you want to be a police officer?

The panel is checking your motive. "Excitement" and "I've always loved cop shows" score badly. So does a purely family reason with nothing of your own in it. Tie the job to something you have actually done.

Example answer for a candidate from a retail management job:

"For four years I ran the night shift at a supermarket. The part of the job I was best at, and liked most, was the part nobody wanted: dealing with shoplifters, calming arguments at the tills, and walking a scared employee to her car. I want to do that work properly, with training and a team behind me, in the city where I grew up. I've done two ride-alongs with your department, and what stood out was how much of the shift was talking, not chasing."

The misconduct scenario

Almost every board asks some version of this: you see a senior officer, maybe your training officer, take money or property during a search. What do you do? Then they push: "He's your friend. He says it's nothing. Everyone does it."

They are scoring two things: do you act, and do you report? Do both, calmly, without a speech about loyalty.

Example answer:

"If it's happening in front of me, I'd say something right then: 'That needs to go in the property bag.' Once we're clear of the scene, I'd tell my sergeant what I saw and write it up the way policy requires. I'd tell my training officer I was doing it, not go behind his back. If he says it's nothing, it still gets reported. It's not my call to decide it's small, and if I let it go once, I'm part of it."

Keep the same answer under pressure. If you change it on the second push, that is the moment the panel writes down.

De-escalation

Expect "Tell me about a time you calmed down an angry person." Use a real story from any job, and name what you did, because "I stayed calm" is not an action.

Example answer:

"A customer was shouting at a cashier about a refund and had stepped behind the counter. I walked over slowly, stood to his side rather than in his face, and used his name from the receipt. I lowered my voice so he had to quiet down to hear me. I told him what I could do, which was call the manager now, and what I couldn't, which was refund cash without a receipt. Once he had a choice, he stepped back. I wrote down what happened before I went home."

Stress and what you carry home

The panel wants real habits and a willingness to ask for help. Name two concrete things, such as training for a race or talking to a specific person, and say you would use peer support if a call stayed with you. Claiming nothing bothers you is weaker than admitting what does.

How to prepare

  1. Write six short stories: a conflict you calmed, a mistake you owned, a rule you followed that cost you, a quick decision, a stranger you helped, a team effort.
  2. Read the department's website and community programs, and have one specific reason for choosing them.
  3. Say each answer out loud and time it. Aim for 60 to 90 seconds.
  4. Get someone to argue with your ethics answers. In Speaking Coach you can describe the situation, set the pressure to Tough, and let the partner push back on what you actually said.
  5. Practice the general questions too: tell me about a time you faced an ethical dilemma and how do you handle stress.

Questions people ask

Is the oral board the same as the background interview?

Usually not. Many departments run a separate background investigation that checks your history, where full honesty about your past matters even more.

What if I don't know the department's policy for a scenario?

Say what you would do and that you would follow department policy and your supervisor's direction. Panels don't expect recruits to know the manual, only to act honestly and report.

Can I say I would let a small thing slide?

On integrity questions, no. Even a free coffee scenario is testing whether you draw a line. You can say you would handle it proportionately, but it still gets raised.

What if I have no law enforcement experience?

Most candidates don't. Use examples from work, sport or volunteering where you dealt with conflict, rules or pressure. The board scores judgment, not police knowledge.

How long should each answer be?

About one to two minutes. Long enough to state the situation and your actions in order, short enough for the panel to follow up.
