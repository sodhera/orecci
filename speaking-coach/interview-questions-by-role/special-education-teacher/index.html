Special education teacher interview questions and example answers

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Special education interviews focus on four things: how you write and track IEP goals, how you support students in general education classrooms, what you do when a student is in crisis, and how you lead paraprofessionals and partner with families. Answer with one real student, described without identifying details, and show the data, the plan and what changed for that student.

Why specifics matter more here than anywhere

A special education panel usually includes a principal and a special education coordinator, and sometimes a general education teacher. The coordinator will know immediately whether you've actually written goals and sat through hard IEP meetings. Broad statements like "every child can learn" get a polite nod. A goal you can recite with a baseline and a measure gets attention.

Always describe students without names, schools or details that could identify them.

IEPs: goals, progress and meetings

Writing a measurable goal

Interviewers often ask you to talk through one. Use a clear shape: the condition, the skill, the measure and the timeline, built from a baseline.

"For a fourth grader reading 45 words per minute on second-grade passages, with a lot of guessing at multi-syllable words, I wrote: 'Given a second-grade passage, the student will read 70 words correct per minute with 95% accuracy in four of five weekly probes by the annual review.' It came straight from the baseline data, and anyone on the team could measure it the same way."

Tracking progress

Say how often you collect data, what tool you use and what you do when the line is flat. "I graph it every two weeks, and if three points in a row are below the aim line, I change the intervention and tell the family" is the kind of sentence coordinators want. The same habit answers the data decision question.

Meetings with upset families

Show that you prepare, listen first and keep the student at the center.

"Before a meeting where I know a parent is frustrated, I call them to ask what they most want to talk about, so nothing is a surprise. In the meeting I start with something their child does well, share the data in plain words and ask what they see at home. When one father said the school had 'given up' on his son, I didn't argue. I showed him the progress graph and asked what he wanted the next goal to be. We wrote it together."

Inclusion and co-teaching

Panels want to know you can work in other teachers' rooms, not just your own. Name the support you'd give and how you'd win over a teacher who is unsure.

"With a science teacher who saw accommodations as extra work, I offered to make the first modified quiz myself. The student passed it, and she asked me to help plan the next unit together."

A student in crisis

This question tests safety, calm and process. Say what you do before, during and after.

  1. Before: know each student's behavior plan and early signs, and keep the room set up to reduce triggers.
  2. During: keep everyone safe, lower your voice, give space, use the de-escalation steps in the plan and call for support using the school's process.
  3. After: debrief with the student when calm, document what happened, inform the family and review the plan with the team.

"A student with a behavior plan began throwing chairs during a fire drill change. I had the para lead the other students to the library, stayed at a safe distance, spoke quietly and offered his calm-down space. Once he was calm, we talked about what happened. I documented it, called his mother that afternoon and asked the team to add drill warnings to his plan."

Mention any de-escalation training you have, and say you follow your district's rules on restraint and reporting.

Paraprofessionals and families

Show you lead paras with clear roles and respect. Give each para a written schedule, explain the goal behind each task, give feedback privately and include them in planning. For families, mention regular contact beyond IEP meetings, such as a weekly note or a quick call with good news.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Talking about a student so they could be identified. Confidentiality counts in the interview too.
  • Treating paras as helpers rather than team members. Panels hear it.
  • Promising services you can't guarantee. In meetings, say what the team will consider, not what you'll give.

Questions people ask

How do I answer if I'm new and haven't run an IEP meeting?

Use your student teaching or para experience, and be clear about your part. Describe a meeting you observed and what you would do.

Should I bring sample goals or data?

A short portfolio with anonymized goals and a progress graph can help. Offer it when it supports an answer.

What do I say about paperwork and deadlines?

Show a system: a calendar of review dates, time blocked each week for writing and checking compliance with your coordinator.

What should I ask at the end?

Ask about caseload size, how co-teaching is scheduled, how paras are assigned and what support exists for students in crisis.
