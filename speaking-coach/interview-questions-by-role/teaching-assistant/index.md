# Teaching assistant interview questions and how to answer them

Teaching assistant and paraprofessional interviews ask how you would support one pupil with additional needs, how you would work under the class teacher's direction, how you would handle a behavior moment, and what you would do if a child told you something worrying. Show patience, show you build independence rather than doing the work for the child, and show you follow the school's safeguarding policy.

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence. Updated 2026-10-22. Source: https://www.orecci.com/speaking-coach/interview-questions-by-role/teaching-assistant/

## What schools look for in a TA or paraprofessional

The panel is usually the head teacher or principal, the special educational needs coordinator, and sometimes the class teacher. Some schools add a short task, such as reading with a child while they watch. They want someone patient and steady who follows the teacher's plan, notices what a child needs, and keeps children safe.

If your experience is from parenting, coaching, Sunday school or babysitting, use it. Be specific about what you did with children.

## Supporting one pupil with additional needs

*"How would you support a pupil with additional needs in a mainstream class?"*

Example answer:

> "First I'd learn everything I can about him: his plan, what the teacher and SENCO say works, and from his parents. Then I'd get to know him, what he likes and what he finds hard. In lessons I'd break tasks into small steps, use visuals if they help him, and give him time to answer before stepping in. I'd sit near, not on top of him, so he's part of the class. And I'd keep short notes on what worked each day to share with the teacher."

If you know the specific need from the job post, such as autism, dyslexia or a physical disability, mention one strategy you have used or learned. Don't claim expertise you don't have.

## When the pupil refuses to work

Example answer:

> "I'd try to find out why first. Is the task too hard, is he tired, did something happen at break? I'd offer a smaller first step: 'Let's just write the date together.' Or a choice: 'Do you want to start with the picture or the sentence?' If he's overwhelmed, I'd use whatever calming strategy is in his plan, like a short movement break. I wouldn't argue or make it a battle in front of the class. Afterwards I'd tell the teacher what happened and what helped."

## Following the teacher's lead

*"What if you disagreed with how the teacher handles a child?"* They're checking you won't undermine the teacher.

Example answer:

> "In the lesson, I'd follow the teacher's approach, because the child needs us to be consistent. Afterwards, at a quiet moment, I'd share what I've noticed: 'When he's given a timer, he seems to settle faster. Would it be worth trying?' It's the teacher's call. I'd see my job as giving them good information about the child."

## Behavior moments

| Situation | What to do | Example words |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Low-level chatter in a group | Quiet reminder, praise who's on task | "I like how Amira's already started." |
| A child throws something | Stay calm, keep others safe, follow school policy | "Let's put that down. We'll talk in a minute." |
| A meltdown | Reduce demands, follow the child's plan, get support | "You're safe. Let's go to the quiet corner." |
| Repeated refusal | Choice, small step, tell the teacher | "First the first line, then your break." |

Always say you'd follow the school's behavior policy and the child's plan, and keep the teacher informed.

## Safeguarding: a child tells you something

This question comes up in almost every school interview.

Example answer:

> "I'd stay calm and listen, without asking leading questions. I wouldn't promise to keep it secret. I'd say I need to tell someone whose job is to help keep them safe. As soon as possible, I'd report it to the designated safeguarding lead, following the school's policy, and write down exactly what the child said in their words, with the time and date. I wouldn't investigate myself or talk to the parents."

## How to prepare

1. Read the job post for the specific need or year group, and prepare one strategy for it.
2. Prepare two stories with real children: one where you helped a child learn, one where you handled a difficult moment.
3. Practice the safeguarding answer until you can say it calmly in order. Hesitation on it worries panels.
4. Say your answers out loud and keep each to about a minute. Speaking Coach's interviewer follows up on your actual words, such as "What would you do if that didn't work?"

For team questions, see [tell me about a time you worked well in a team](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/tell-me-about-a-time-you-worked-well-in-a-team/).

## Questions people ask

### Do I need qualifications to be a teaching assistant?

It depends on the school, district and country. Some roles ask for specific certificates or tests; others train on the job. Check the job post and mention any relevant training.

### What if I have no classroom experience?

Use any experience with children, such as parenting, coaching or youth groups, and describe exactly what you did. Show you'll learn the school's ways quickly.

### How do I answer "Why do you want to be a TA?"

Give a real reason, ideally a child or moment, and mention what you like about the job itself: helping one child make progress, day by day. More on [why do you want this job](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/why-do-you-want-this-job/).

### What should I say if a parent asks about their child?

Be friendly and positive, share small things you're allowed to, and refer bigger questions to the class teacher. Don't discuss other children.

### What will the classroom task involve?

Often reading with a child or supporting a small group. Be warm, let children do the thinking, and praise effort.
