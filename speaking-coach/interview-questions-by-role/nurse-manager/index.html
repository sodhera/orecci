Nurse manager interview questions and how to answer them

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Nurse manager and charge nurse interviews test whether you can run a unit, not just care for patients: how you cover a short shift safely, how you handle a nurse who is struggling, how you resolve a patient complaint and which unit goals you would chase. Answer with one real shift or situation, say what you decided and why, and end with what changed on the unit.

What changes when you interview for leadership

A staff nurse interview asks what you did for a patient. A nurse manager or charge nurse interview asks what you did for the unit. The panel is often a director of nursing, someone from HR and one or two staff nurses who might report to you. Each is listening for something different:

Panel memberWhat they want to hear
Director of nursingYou make safe decisions alone, escalate early and understand the numbers
HRYou document, follow policy and treat staff fairly
Staff nursesYou will back them up, be on the floor and not play favorites

So every answer needs a decision, a reason and a result for the team. Clinical skill is assumed.

Staffing shortages

This is the question most panels ask first, because it happens every week. Show a calm order of moves, then show you also think about why it keeps happening.

  1. Check the real gap. Census, acuity and expected admissions and discharges, not just headcount.
  2. Use the approved options. Float pool, per diem staff, voluntary extra shifts, the house supervisor.
  3. Rebalance the assignments. Spread the heaviest patients, pair a newer nurse with an experienced one, take a lighter assignment yourself if you are charge.
  4. Tell the team the plan.
  5. Escalate in writing if the unit is still unsafe, following your hospital's process.

Example answer (made-up details):

"As charge on nights, I had two sick calls at 6 p.m. with 28 patients and three expected admissions. I got one float nurse and one per diem for half the shift, then rebuilt the assignments so no one had more than one patient on a drip. I took two patients myself and kept the free nurse for admissions. I told the house supervisor we would be unsafe after 3 a.m. without more help, and documented it. We got a nurse from the next unit at 2 a.m. Afterward I showed my manager that weekend nights had the most sick calls, and we added a per diem slot on Saturdays."

A nurse with performance problems

Panels want fairness and follow-through.

  1. Gather facts: specific shifts, charts, incidents, not hallway talk.
  2. Meet privately and early. Describe what you saw, then ask what is going on.
  3. Agree a plan with clear expectations, support (an educator, a preceptor) and a review date.
  4. Document each meeting and follow up on the date you set.
  5. If nothing changes, move to your formal process with HR.

Example answer:

"One of my experienced nurses started missing reassessments after pain medication, three times in two weeks. In a private meeting I showed her the charts and asked what was happening. She was working a second job and exhausted. We agreed she would drop to three shifts a week for a month, and I checked her charts every shift for two weeks. The gaps stopped. If they hadn't, I had already told her the next step would be a formal plan with HR."

Patient and family complaints

Show that you listen without defending staff on the spot, investigate before you judge, and close the loop with the family and the nurse.

Example answer:

"A son complained that his mother's call light went unanswered for 40 minutes. I met him the same day, listened and apologized for her experience without blaming anyone. I pulled the call light log and talked to the nurse: she had been with a patient in rapid response and the aide was on break. The problem was coverage, not care. I called him back with what I'd found, and we changed breaks so an aide is always on the floor. I told the nurse what the family had said and what we changed, so she didn't hear it secondhand."

Unit goals

When they ask what you would focus on, pick two or three goals, tie each to a measure and say how you would involve staff. Good choices are falls, pressure injuries, infection rates, patient experience scores, turnover and overtime. Don't promise a number you haven't seen; say you would start from the unit's current data.

"In my first three months I'd look at the unit's falls, overtime and turnover data and ask staff what slows them down. If falls are the biggest gap, I'd start a short safety huddle at each shift change and have two staff nurses lead the project, so the change belongs to the unit, not just to me."

Mistakes to avoid

MistakeWhy it hurtsTry instead
Talking only about clinical skillThey need a leader, not the best bedside nurseShow a decision you made for the whole unit
"I'd just work the extra shift myself"Shows you won't solve the real problemCover the shift, then fix the pattern
Criticizing your current managerThe panel imagines you talking about themDescribe what you would do differently, without names

Questions people ask

What is the difference between a charge nurse and a nurse manager interview?

Charge nurse interviews focus on running a single shift: assignments, flow, escalation. Nurse manager interviews add hiring, budgets, performance management and longer-term goals.

Can I get a nurse manager job without formal management experience?

Many people move up from charge or preceptor roles. Use those examples and be clear about what you decided yourself.

How do I answer "What is your leadership style?"

Name it in one sentence, then prove it with a shift or decision. See how to answer the leadership style question.

What should I ask the panel?

Ask about current turnover, how leadership supports managers when staffing is short, and what the last manager's biggest challenge was.
