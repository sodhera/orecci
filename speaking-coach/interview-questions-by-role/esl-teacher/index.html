ESL teacher interview questions and how to answer them

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

ESL and English-teaching-abroad interviews test whether you can teach students who understand very little English, run a short demo lesson where students talk more than you, keep a mixed-level class working and adapt to life and classrooms in another country. Answer with a real lesson or class, show what students did, and keep your own English simple and clear while you speak.

How ESL interviews are different

Many English-teaching interviews, especially for jobs abroad, happen by video, often across time zones. The interviewer is judging two things at once: what you say about teaching, and how you speak. Clear, unhurried English with simple words is itself evidence you can teach beginners. Rushing, idioms and long sentences are evidence you can't.

Most include a demo lesson, either live with the interviewer playing a student or recorded in advance. If yours is on video, read the video interview tips first.

Teaching complete beginners

Interviewers want to hear that you rely on showing, not explaining.

  • Gesture, pictures and objects before words.
  • Model, then practice: you say it, the class says it, pairs say it.
  • Grade your language: short sentences, the same instruction words every time.
  • Check understanding with simple questions, never "Do you understand?"

Example answer (made-up details):

"On the first day with adult beginners, I'd teach greetings and names. I'd point to myself: 'I'm Sam.' Then point to a student with an open hand and wait. When they say their name, I'd say 'Hello, Ana!' and get the class to repeat. Then I'd get them to stand and say it to three people. In 20 minutes everyone can greet and introduce themselves, and I've barely explained anything."

Checking understanding

This small topic comes up often, because "Do you understand?" always gets "yes." Show you use concept-check questions instead.

Instead ofAsk
"Do you understand 'used to'?""I used to smoke. Do I smoke now?"
"Is the task clear?""Are you working alone or with a partner? How many minutes?"
"Got it?""Show me: what's the first thing you'll do?"

The demo lesson

Keep it simple. One small target, real student talk and a clear check at the end. A common shape for a short demo:

  1. Warm-up (1 minute): a picture or question to get them talking.
  2. Present (1–2 minutes): the target language in context, with a model.
  3. Practice (2 minutes): controlled, like repeating or matching.
  4. Produce (1–2 minutes): students use it to say something real about themselves.
  5. Check (30 seconds): one question that proves they've got it.

Aim for students talking more than you. Interviewers often notice teacher talk time more than the activity itself. Rehearse with a timer: if you run over, cut the presentation, not the student practice. There's more on timing in presenting in an interview.

Mixed-level classes

Show practical tools rather than "I differentiate."

"In a class where some students could hold a conversation and others were still on basic sentences, I used the same topic with different tasks. For a lesson on weekends, lower-level students completed sentence frames like 'On Saturday I ___,' while stronger students interviewed each other with follow-up questions. Then I mixed pairs so stronger students had to adjust their English. Everyone worked on the same topic, at their own level."

Other tools to mention: open tasks with several right answers, extension cards for fast finishers and seating that changes depending on the activity.

Culture and adapting abroad

Schools hiring from abroad worry about teachers who leave in the first months. Show you've researched the country and thought about classroom norms.

"I've read about how classes here are often quieter and more teacher-led than I'm used to. I wouldn't expect students to jump into debates on day one. I'd start with pair work, which feels safer, and build up. Outside school, I plan to take language lessons and say yes to invitations from colleagues."

Mistakes to avoid

  • Over-explaining grammar in the demo. Show it in use instead.
  • Speaking fast or using idioms. Your interview English is part of the test.
  • Correcting every error. Correct the target language; let fluency flow elsewhere.
  • Talking about travel instead of teaching. It's fine to want adventure, but lead with students.

Questions people ask

Do I need a TEFL or similar certificate?

Many schools and countries ask for one, but requirements vary. Check the job post and the country's official rules for work visas.

What if I've never taught?

Use any experience explaining things to people: tutoring, training at work, volunteering. In the demo, show that you know the basics: model, practice, check.

How do I answer "Why this country?"

Be specific about the country and the job, not just travel. Mention something you've learned about its schools or culture and why it suits you.

Should I speak the students' language in class?

It depends on the school's policy. Say you'd use English as much as possible, with gestures and pictures, and ask how the school handles it.

What should I ask at the end?

Ask about class sizes, students' typical levels, how much planning time you have and what support new teachers get with housing and settling in.
