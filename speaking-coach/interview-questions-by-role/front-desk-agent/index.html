Hotel front desk agent interview questions and how to answer them

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Hotel front desk interviews test how you sound when a tired guest hears bad news. Expect questions on telling a guest the hotel is overbooked, handling a complaint at check-in, offering a room upgrade without pushing, and working nights alone. Answer by saying the actual words you would use at the desk.

What the interviewer is listening for

A front office manager is not mainly testing your knowledge of booking software. Most systems can be learned in a week. They are listening to your voice: whether you stay warm when a guest is rude, whether you can say "no" without sounding cold, and whether you would know what to do at 3 a.m. with no manager on site.

The best way to show this is to answer the scenario questions with the words you would actually say to the guest, not a description of them. "I'd apologize and find a solution" is a summary. "I'm really sorry, Ms. Patel, I know it's late and this is the last thing you want to hear" is an answer.

Overbooking: the question that separates candidates

"We're overbooked and a guest with a confirmed reservation arrives. What do you do?" This is the hardest conversation at a hotel desk, so it comes up often. A strong answer has four parts:

  1. Tell them straight away and own it. No blaming the system or another shift.
  2. Have the fix ready before you speak. A room at a nearby hotel, transport there, and whatever your hotel's policy covers.
  3. Make it personal. Their name, a direct number to call, a follow-up the next day.
  4. Tell the manager, and note it on the guest's profile so the next shift knows.

Example answer: "I'd make sure the alternative was arranged before I spoke to them. Then: 'Mr. Okafor, I'm sorry. We've had more arrivals than rooms tonight, and that's our mistake, not yours. I've booked you a room at the hotel across the road, the taxi's on us, and I'll call you myself tomorrow to bring you back here.' Then I'd log it and tell my manager."

A complaint at check-in

"The guest says the room isn't what they booked." Listen fully first, check the reservation while they talk, and only then offer what you can do. Avoid arguing about what the booking said, even if you are right.

Example answer: "First I'd let them finish and repeat the problem back, so they know I heard it. Then I'd check the booking. If we got it wrong, I'd fix it now: move them or offer the right room type. If the booking really was for a standard room, I'd still look for a way to help, maybe a better view or a later checkout, rather than pointing at the screen."

Upselling without pushing

Many hotels judge front desk agents partly on upgrades sold. Interviewers want to hear that you match the offer to the guest, not that you pitch everyone.

Couple on an anniversary"Do you want to upgrade?""For your anniversary, there's a corner room with the river view tonight. It's an extra amount per night if you'd like it."
Business traveler, late arrivalListing every room type"There's a quiet room on the top floor with a desk, away from the lift, if you'd like it."

Say the offer once, then accept the answer. Repeating it after a "no" is what makes guests feel sold to.

Night shifts

"Are you comfortable working nights alone?" Say yes only if it is true, then show you understand the job: night audit, security rounds, a guest who is drunk or locked out, a fire alarm. Give one example of handling something alone.

Example answer: "Yes. I worked nights at a 90-room hotel for a year. On my own, I ran the night audit, handled late arrivals, and once dealt with a guest who'd lost their key and ID. I didn't let them into the room until I'd matched them to the booking photo and called the number on file. I'm calm on my own, and I know when to call the duty manager."

Questions you can't answer by being friendly

Some questions check judgment on security and privacy. If a caller or visitor asks for a guest's room number, the answer is no, every time. You offer to call the room or take a message instead. If a card is declined, speak quietly, never across a busy lobby, and offer to try another card.

How to prepare

  1. Say your overbooking answer out loud, including the guest's name and the actual offer.
  2. Prepare one real story about an angry customer and one about handling something alone.
  3. Practice one upsell line for three different guest types.
  4. Have someone play the guest and refuse your first offer. Speaking Coach's built-in "Handle a rude customer" scene does this, with a partner who reacts to what you say.

Questions people ask

What do front desk interviews usually start with?

Often "Tell me about yourself" and "Why hotels?" Keep both under a minute and connect them to guests: a past service job, languages you speak, or why you like the pace of a lobby.

Do I need hotel experience?

Not always. Retail, restaurant, call center and receptionist work all transfer. Name the overlap: handling queues, money, complaints and a phone at the same time.

Should I mention languages I speak?

Yes, and be honest about your level. "Fluent Spanish, conversational French" is useful. Expect them to switch language for a minute to check.

What should I wear to a hotel interview?

Dress as you would for the desk itself: neat, simple and professional. The interviewer is picturing you greeting guests.

How do I answer questions about availability?

Be specific about which shifts you can work, including weekends, holidays and nights. Vague answers here cost offers, because hotels schedule around them.
