Program manager interview questions and how to answer them

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Program manager interviews test whether you can see across several projects at once: how you map dependencies, how you tell executives the truth in two minutes, and what you do when the whole program is slipping. Answer with one real program each time, name the dependency or decision that mattered, and end with what changed because of you.

What the panel is listening for

A program manager interview is not a project manager interview with bigger numbers. The interviewers want to hear that you think in connections: which project blocks which, which team's slip lands on whose launch, and which single decision would unblock three others. They also want proof you can say hard things to senior people without drama.

Expect the hiring manager, leads whose teams you would coordinate, and often a senior leader playing your sponsor.

Running several connected projects

Open with the shape of the program, not the history. "Six teams, one launch date, three external partners" tells the panel more than two minutes of background.

Example answer (made-up details):

"I ran the move of our billing system to a new payments provider. It was five projects: the API integration, data migration, finance reporting, customer emails and support training. Each had its own lead. My job was the seams between them. The data migration couldn't start until finance signed off the new report formats, so I put that sign-off on the critical path and got it scheduled in week two instead of week six. We cut over on the planned weekend with two known issues, both in the support runbook."

Dependencies

Say how you find them, how you track them and what you do when one breaks. A good method sounds plain: a dependency map built with each lead in the first two weeks, a named owner and date on every handoff, and a weekly review of the ones due in the next 30 days.

Then give one dependency you caught early. The catch is the proof; the spreadsheet is not.

Executive updates

This is often tested live: "Give me the update as if I were the sponsor." Lead with the status and the ask, then stop. A useful shape:

  1. Status in one line. "We're amber: launch is at risk by two weeks."
  2. Why, in one sentence. "The vendor's security review is late, and three teams wait on it."
  3. Options. "We hold the date and launch without the partner feature, or we move to the 18th."
  4. Your recommendation and the decision you need. "I recommend the 18th. I need your yes by Friday so marketing can move the announcement."

Aim for 60–90 seconds. For more, see presenting to executives.

A program in trouble

The panel wants three things: you saw it early, you didn't hide it and you offered choices instead of panic.

Example answer:

"Three months into a data platform program, two of four teams were missing sprint goals and nobody had said 'red' yet. I sat with each lead for an hour and found the same cause: a shared test environment that was down two days a week. I took it to the sponsor that week as red, with three options: buy a second environment, cut one workstream, or move the date six weeks. We funded the environment, re-planned with the leads, and shipped four weeks late instead of the ten we were heading for. I now ask every lead for a red-amber-green call in their own words each week, not just a percent complete."

Questions people ask

What is the difference between a project manager and a program manager in an interview answer?

Say that a project manager delivers one defined outcome, while a program manager coordinates several related projects toward a bigger goal and owns the dependencies between them. Then give one example of a dependency decision you made.

How technical do I need to be for a technical program manager interview?

Enough to explain the system your program touched and why a dependency mattered. Expect to describe an architecture at a whiteboard level, not to write code.

How long should my program story be?

About 90 seconds to two minutes, with the first 15 seconds on the shape of the program. Let the panel ask for detail.

What should I ask at the end?

Ask how programs are staffed here, who the sponsor would be and what the last program that slipped taught them. More ideas are in questions to ask at the end.
