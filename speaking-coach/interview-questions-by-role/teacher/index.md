# Teacher interview questions and how to answer them

K-12 teacher interviews keep coming back to four things: how you run a classroom, how you teach students at different levels in the same room, a lesson you actually taught and how you work with parents. Answer each with a real class, a real routine and what students did as a result, not with a teaching philosophy.

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence. Updated 2026-10-22. Source: https://www.orecci.com/speaking-coach/interview-questions-by-role/teacher/

## Why vague answers sink teacher interviews

Teacher interviews are often panels: a principal, a department or grade-level lead, sometimes a parent. Every one of them has heard "I build relationships and set high expectations." What they haven't heard is your version: the routine on the first day, the student who tested it, what you did at minute three of the disruption.

The fix is the same for every question below. Replace the belief with the practice.

| Vague | Specific |
| --- | --- |
| "I have high expectations." | "On day one we practice how to enter the room, and we practice it again on day two." |
| "I differentiate my instruction." | "For fractions I ran three groups off the same exit ticket from the day before." |
| "I keep parents in the loop." | "I send a two-line positive note home for every student in the first three weeks." |

## Classroom management

Panels want to know two things: what you set up before problems happen, and what you do when they happen anyway. Answer in that order.

Example answer (made-up details):

> "I spend the first two weeks teaching routines like content: how to enter, how to get materials, what to do when you finish early. Most behavior problems are a routine nobody taught. When a student still disrupts, I go quiet and close first: I move next to their desk and keep teaching. If it continues, I give a private, calm choice. Last year one boy kept calling out during read-aloud. A quiet word didn't work, so I gave him a job holding the class question cards. The calling out became his turn to read the question."

Mention consequences, but put the weight on prevention and private correction.

## Differentiating for mixed abilities

"Differentiation" can sound like a buzzword. Make it concrete by describing one lesson and what each group did.

1. **How you found out the levels.** A pre-assessment, last week's exit ticket, reading levels.
2. **What changed for each group.** The task, the support or the text, not three separate lessons.
3. **How you moved between them.** Where you sat, who you pulled for a small group.
4. **What the evidence showed.** The next exit ticket, a quiz, a student who moved up.

Example answer:

> "Before our fractions unit, a five-question check showed about a third of my class couldn't place a half on a number line. The next day everyone worked on the same comparison problems, but that group started with fraction strips at my table while the rest worked in pairs, and early finishers got a 'prove it two ways' card. Two days later the same check showed most of my table group placing fractions correctly."

## A lesson you taught

When they say "walk us through a lesson," they are checking whether your lesson has a clear objective, a moment where students do the thinking, and a way to check understanding. Use that structure:

- **The objective,** in student words: "I can explain why the seasons change."
- **The hook,** in one sentence.
- **What students did,** with you talking less than half the time.
- **How you checked,** such as an exit ticket or a quick show of whiteboards.
- **What you changed next time.** This is the part most candidates skip, and it's what shows you reflect.

If you're asked for a lesson that went badly, use the same structure and spend the time on what you changed. If your interview includes a demo lesson, the same habits apply. Our guide to [giving a presentation in an interview](/speaking-coach/interviews/interview-presentation/) covers timing.

## Working with parents

Show that you contact parents before there is a problem, and that you stay calm when there is one.

Example answer for an upset parent:

> "A mother emailed me angry that her son got a C on a science project. I called her rather than replying by email, and asked what she'd seen at home. He had worked hard on the model, but the rubric weighted the written explanation, which he'd rushed. I walked her through the rubric, showed her two strong explanations from the class with names removed, and offered him a chance to revise that section. She thanked me, and he revised it."

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Reciting your philosophy instead of answering.** One sentence of belief, then the practice.
- **Criticizing a past school or parents.** Panels hear it as a warning sign.
- **Forgetting the evidence.** Every example ends with what students did or learned.

Say your classroom management and parent answers out loud a few times before the day. With Speaking Coach you can paste in the job post and practice with an AI interviewer who asks the follow-up a principal would.

## Questions people ask

### How do I answer "What is your teaching philosophy?"

Give one sentence of belief and immediately show it in a routine or a lesson. "I think students learn most when they talk, so every lesson has a pair discussion before anyone writes" is stronger than a paragraph of theory.

### What if I've only taught as a student teacher?

Use your placement examples, and be clear about what you planned and decided yourself. Panels hiring new teachers expect student-teaching stories.

### Should I bring a portfolio?

If you have one, bring a slim version: two or three lesson plans, student work samples with names removed and any data you used. Offer it when it supports an answer rather than handing over the whole folder.

### What should I ask at the end?

Ask about the school, not the salary: how grade-level teams plan together, how new teachers are mentored and what behavior support looks like. See [questions to ask at the end of an interview](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/do-you-have-any-questions-for-us/).
