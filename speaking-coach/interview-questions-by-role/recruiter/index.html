Recruiter interview questions and how to answer them

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Recruiter interviews test whether you can fill hard roles, manage hiring managers and read your own funnel. Expect a sourcing walk-through, a story about a demanding hiring manager, a candidate who backed out, and questions on your metrics. Answer with one real search each time: the role, what you did, and the numbers that moved.

What the interviewer is listening for

Recruiting interviews are run by people who do the job, so vague answers stand out fast. They are checking three things: can you find people nobody else found, can you manage the hiring manager instead of taking orders, and do you know your numbers without looking them up. Every answer should sound like a search you actually ran.

"Walk me through how you sourced a hard role"

Pick one search and tell it in order: why it was hard, where you looked, what you changed when the first approach failed, and how it ended. Interviewers want the change in approach most, because that is where judgment shows.

Example answer for an agency or in-house tech recruiter:

"We needed a payments engineer with card-network experience, and the job board brought in 40 applicants with none. I mapped the five companies near us that run their own payment processing, found 60 people on LinkedIn and GitHub, and wrote messages that named the actual problem the team was solving instead of the job title. 14 replied, six went to screen, and we hired in seven weeks. The bit I'd repeat is asking the hiring manager which competitors they'd poach from on day one."

"Tell me about a demanding hiring manager"

This is a question about influence. Don't blame the manager. Show how you used evidence to reset the brief.

  1. Say what kept going wrong: "She rejected eight candidates in a row after the first interview."
  2. Say what you found: the feedback pointed to a requirement that wasn't in the job post.
  3. Say what you did: a 20-minute recalibration with three anonymised profiles to rank.
  4. Say the result: a sharper brief, and the next shortlist moved forward.

"After the eighth rejection I stopped sending profiles and asked for a recalibration. I brought three CVs with names removed and asked her to rank them out loud. It turned out 'senior' meant 'has managed a budget', which was nowhere in the post. We rewrote it together and the next two candidates both reached the final round."

"Tell me about a candidate who reneged"

Everyone in recruiting has one. The interviewer wants to know whether you saw the signs, how you told the hiring manager, and what you changed afterwards. Own the part that was yours.

"A sales lead accepted, then took a counteroffer two days before starting. Looking back, he'd gone quiet after the offer and I hadn't asked about counteroffers directly. I told the manager the same day, went back to our silver-medal candidate, and she started three weeks later. Now I ask every finalist, 'If your company offers you more to stay, what will you do?' before the offer goes out."

Pipeline metrics: know yours cold

You will be asked for numbers. Have three or four ready, with the period and what you did to move them. Use your own figures; if you don't have exact ones, give a range and say so.

Questions people ask

Do agency and in-house recruiter interviews ask different questions?

Agency interviews lean on business development, billing and how many placements you made. In-house interviews lean on stakeholder management, employer brand and candidate experience. The sourcing and metrics questions come up in both.

What if I'm moving into recruiting from another job?

Use stories from roles where you persuaded people, worked a pipeline or matched people to needs, such as sales, customer success or teaching. Say plainly why you want to recruit and what you've done to learn the tools.

How do I answer if my offer acceptance rate was low?

Give the real number, explain the cause you found, and say what you changed. A low number with a clear fix is more convincing than a perfect number nobody believes.

Will they ask me to do a mock intake or screen?

Often, yes. Treat it like a real call: ask what the role must achieve in its first six months, what the deal-breakers are, and who the team is hiring against.

How long should my answers be?

Aim for 60 to 90 seconds per story. Recruiters know when a candidate is padding, and they will ask follow-up questions anyway.
