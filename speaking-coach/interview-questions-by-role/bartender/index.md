# Bartender interview questions and how to answer them

Bartender interviews ask how you would cut off a guest who has had too much, how you run a bar three deep, what you do with an ID you doubt, and how you make a classic drink. Show you protect the license and your guests first, stay fast and friendly under pressure, and can talk through a recipe calmly.

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence. Updated 2026-10-22. Source: https://www.orecci.com/speaking-coach/interview-questions-by-role/bartender/

## What the bar manager is listening for

Bar interviews are often quick and informal, sometimes followed by a stage or trial shift behind the bar. Under the friendliness, the manager has two worries: will you protect the bar's license, and will you keep up on a Saturday? Every scenario question is one of those two in disguise.

## Cutting someone off

This is the question that matters most. The manager wants firm, private and calm.

Example answer:

> "I'd go to them directly, not shout across the bar, and keep it low and simple: 'I can't serve you another drink tonight, but I'll get you some water and a menu.' I wouldn't argue about how much they've had or say they're drunk. If they're with friends, I'd ask a sober friend to help them get home safely and offer to call a cab. I'd tell the other bartenders and the manager or door staff, so nobody else serves them. If it gets aggressive, security handles it, not me."

Key lines to keep: **no debate, offer water or food, tell the team, help them get home.**

## Running a busy bar

*"The bar is three deep. How do you handle it?"*

Example answer:

> "First, acknowledge everyone. A nod or 'I'll be right with you' keeps people patient. I serve in order as best I can, and I take two or three orders at once when they're simple. I build drinks in the most efficient order: anything shaken first, beer and wine while it chills. I keep my station stocked during any gap so I'm not running for ice mid-rush. And I keep tabs tidy as I go, because closing out a wrong tab at 1am costs more time than anything."

## Checking ID

Say you would follow the law and the house policy every time, regardless of how old someone looks or whose friend they are.

Example answer:

> "I check ID for anyone who looks under the age in our house policy, and I take it out of the wallet. I look at the photo against the face, the date of birth, the expiry date, and how the card feels. If I'm not sure, I ask a quick question, like their zip code or star sign, and watch how fast they answer. If I'm still unsure, I don't serve, and I politely say so. Losing one sale is better than risking the license."

If you're asked about what to do with a suspected fake, say you'd follow house policy and involve the manager. Policies differ on whether to keep the card.

## The drink question

Many managers ask you to talk through a classic, such as an Old Fashioned, Margarita or Negroni. They're checking you know the build and can explain it the way you would to a guest. Recipes vary by house, so say that.

Example answer:

> "For an Old Fashioned I'd use bourbon or rye, a little sugar or syrup and a few dashes of aromatic bitters, stirred over ice until it's cold and slightly diluted, then strained over a large cube with an orange peel expressed over the top. If you have a house spec, I'd learn that. If a guest isn't sure what to order, I'd ask what spirit they like and whether they want something sweet, sour or bitter."

## The trickier questions

| Question | What they're checking | Good answer includes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Drink's too weak" | Calm with complaints, standard pours | Apologize, explain the pour, offer to remake or swap |
| Coworker giving free drinks | Honesty | Raise it with them, then the manager if it continues |
| Upselling | Sales without pushing | Ask about taste, suggest one specific drink |
| Difficult guest | De-escalation | Short words, low voice, involve door staff early |

## How to prepare

1. Know three classics and how you'd describe each to a guest in one sentence.
2. Practice the cut-off line until it sounds natural, not rehearsed. Tone matters more than words here; Speaking Coach can play the guest and push back on what you say.
3. Prepare one story about a busy night and one about a difficult guest.
4. Visit the bar once if you can, and notice the menu and the crowd.

The general version of the complaint question is on [how to handle an angry customer](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/how-would-you-handle-an-angry-customer/).

## Questions people ask

### Do I need bartending experience?

For many bars, yes, or at least barback or serving experience. Some places hire barbacks and train them up. Say honestly what you've done and that you'd start wherever they need.

### Do I need an alcohol-serving certificate?

It depends on where you work. Check local requirements, and mention any responsible-serving training you've done.

### What happens at a bar trial shift?

You'll usually work alongside staff, make some drinks and serve guests. Keep your station clean, ask where things go, and stay friendly.

### How do I answer "Why do you want to bartend here?"

Mention something specific about the bar, such as its cocktail list, crowd or pace, and connect it to what you're good at. The [why do you want to work here](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/why-do-you-want-to-work-here/) guide shows how.

### What should I ask the manager?

Ask about busiest nights, how tips work, and what they expect from new bartenders in the first month.
