Graphic designer interview questions and how to answer them

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Graphic designer interviews are mostly you talking through your portfolio, so the real test is whether you can explain your thinking, not just show finished work. Expect to walk through two or three projects, describe feedback you disagreed with, explain how you read a brief, and talk about juggling deadlines. Lead each project with the problem, then your choices.

The portfolio talk is the interview

Most design interviewers have already seen your portfolio before you arrive. What they can't see is your thinking. Designers who describe what's on screen ("so here's the logo, and here's the business card") give the interviewer nothing new. Designers who explain the problem and the decisions make the work look better than it does on its own.

Expect to show two or three projects, about three to five minutes each, with questions in the middle.

How to walk through a project

Use the same short order for every project so it sounds organized:

PartWhat to sayTime
The problemWho the client was, what wasn't working, what success meant30 seconds
The brief and constraintsBudget, deadline, brand rules, where it would be used20 seconds
Your optionsTwo or three directions you explored, and why you dropped some60 seconds
The choiceThe final design and the reasons for type, color and layout60 seconds
The resultWhat happened after it launched, or what the client said20 seconds

Example opening for a branding project: "A family bakery was opening a second shop and their old logo didn't work small, on stickers and the app. The brief was 'keep it warm, but make it read at 2 centimeters.' I explored three directions: a hand-drawn mark, a wordmark, and a simplified version of their old wheat icon. I'll show you why we went with the wordmark."

Always say "the problem" before you show the solution. If they see the final logo first, they judge it on taste alone.

Explaining your choices

"Why this typeface?" is the most common follow-up. "I liked it" is a weak answer. Link every choice to the brief, the audience or the medium.

Example answer: "The bakery's customers are mostly older neighbors and families, so I wanted something friendly but easy to read. I chose a rounded sans serif because it stays legible at small sizes on packaging, and I kept the brown from their original sign so regulars would still recognize them."

Feedback you disagreed with

This checks whether you can defend your work without becoming defensive. A good story shows you listened, made your case once with reasons, and accepted the outcome.

Example answer: "A client wanted their logo much bigger on a poster, which crowded the event details. I mocked up both versions side by side and showed them from across the room, which is how people would see it. They saw the dates disappear in the big version and agreed to something in between. When I've lost those arguments, I've still delivered the client's version well. It's their brand."

Showing two versions is better than arguing in words. The general version of this question is how do you handle criticism.

Briefs and changing clients

"How do you handle a vague brief?" Describe the questions you ask: who the audience is, where it will appear, what they liked or disliked before, the real deadline, and who signs off. Then say how you confirm it: a short written summary the client agrees to before you design anything.

For a client who keeps changing their mind, mention limits on revision rounds, putting changes in writing, and explaining the time cost calmly: "We can do that. It'll move the delivery to Thursday. Is that okay?"

Deadlines

"How do you manage several projects?" Give your system and one real busy week.

Example answer: "At the agency I often had four or five jobs open. I list each one with its deadline and next step every morning, and I do the work that blocks someone else first, like getting artwork to the printer. When two deadlines clashed last spring, I asked my manager which mattered more instead of guessing, and we moved the internal one by two days."

Mistakes to avoid

  • Showing ten projects. Three strong ones you can talk about beat ten you can't.
  • Apologizing for your work before you show it.
  • Taking credit for a team project. Say clearly which parts were yours.
  • Using design jargon with a non-designer, like a recruiter or a marketing manager.

How to practice

Say your portfolio walkthrough out loud with a timer, one project at a time. Then do it again with someone interrupting with "Why?" Questions people ask

How many projects should I present?

Usually two or three, each three to five minutes. Ask beforehand how long you have, and pick the projects closest to the job's work.

Should I bring printed work?

If the interview is in person, a few printed pieces help for print-focused roles. Always have a digital version ready too.

What if I don't have client work?

Use personal or student projects, and treat them like real briefs: say who it was for, the problem and your constraints.

What if they ask about a design test?

Ask how long it should take and what they'll judge. Explain your thinking when you present it, just like portfolio work.
