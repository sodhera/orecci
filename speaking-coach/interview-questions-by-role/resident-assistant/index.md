# Resident assistant interview questions and how to answer them

Resident assistant interviews ask why you want to be an RA, how you would mediate a roommate conflict, what you would do for a student in distress, how you would enforce a rule on a friend and how you would build community on your floor. Show that you listen first, stay fair, follow policy, and know when to bring in professional staff instead of handling it alone.

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence. Updated 2026-10-22. Source: https://www.orecci.com/speaking-coach/interview-questions-by-role/resident-assistant/

## How RA selection usually works

Many colleges hire RAs in stages: an application, a group process where staff watch candidates work together, then an individual interview. Expect scenario questions and follow-ups that change the facts ("And if she says no?").

The panel is not looking for someone who can fix everything. They want someone who listens, stays fair, knows the limits of the role and calls professional staff at the right moment. If your process has a group stage, read the [group interview tips](/speaking-coach/interviews/group-interview/) first.

## Roommate conflict

This is almost guaranteed. A typical version: *two roommates come to you separately, each upset about the other's guest staying over every night.*

A strong answer follows a clear order: **listen to each separately, meet together on neutral ground, help them agree specific terms, write it down, and check back.**

Example answer:

> "I'd talk to each of them on their own first, mostly listening, so each feels heard before they're in a room together. Then I'd sit down with both, ideally in my room or a lounge rather than theirs, and ask them to agree on specifics: how many nights a week, quiet hours, whether guests use the shower. I'd suggest they put it in their roommate agreement. Then I'd check in with both a week later. If it kept escalating or someone felt unsafe, I'd bring in my hall director."

Don't pick a side or promise a room change you can't grant.

## A student in distress

Interviewers ask this to check you know your limits. *A resident tells you late at night that they don't see the point in anything and have been thinking about hurting themselves.*

The answer they want: stay with them, take it seriously, and follow your school's emergency procedure straight away. You are not their counsellor.

Example answer:

> "I'd stay calm, stay with them, and thank them for telling me. I'd take what they said seriously and not try to talk them out of how they feel. I'd tell them honestly that I can't keep this between us, because their safety matters more, and I'd contact the on-call professional staff right then, following our protocol. If they were in immediate danger I'd call emergency services. Afterwards I'd document it the way I'd been trained and check in on them."

Say "follow the protocol I'm trained on" rather than inventing steps; panels know you haven't had training yet.

## Enforcing rules on your friends

*Your close friend lives on your floor and you walk into a room with alcohol where it isn't allowed.* The panel wants to see that the rules apply equally, and that you can be firm and kind at once.

Example answer:

> "It would be awkward, and I'd say so to them later. But in the moment I'd treat it exactly as I would with anyone else: knock, identify myself as the RA, and follow the documentation process. If I let it go for a friend, every resident who saw it would know the rules depend on who you know. The next day I'd talk to my friend privately and explain why. A friend who'd stop being my friend over that probably wasn't much of one."

Prepare a real time you held a line with someone you liked, such as a team captain enforcing practice rules or a shift lead at work.

## Building community

"How would you build community?" is the question where weak answers list events: pizza night, movie night, game night. Strong answers start with the residents.

| Weak | Stronger |
| --- | --- |
| "I'd hold lots of events." | "In week one I'd knock on every door and learn one thing about each person, then plan around what they told me." |
| "A floor group chat." | "A group chat, plus a door board where people post what they're studying, so neighbours find study partners." |
| "Big parties to bring people out." | "Small, low-effort things: tea in the lounge during finals week, a walk to the dining hall for first-years in week one." |

Mention the quiet residents who never come to events.

## Why do you want to be an RA?

"Free housing" is honest but not enough. Give a real reason from your own experience, such as an RA who helped you, then say what you'd bring, in under a minute.

## How to prepare

1. Write out your answers to the four scenarios above in a few lines each.
2. Find out your school's resources by name: counselling centre, on-call staff, conduct process.
3. Prepare two real stories: one where you helped someone, one where you held a rule.
4. Practice out loud with someone who changes the scenario mid-answer. Speaking Coach can play the interviewer or an upset roommate and follow up on what you said.

## Questions people ask

### Do I need leadership experience to become an RA?

No title is needed. Tutoring, team sports, part-time jobs, clubs or helping run a family household all show the skills panels look for.

### What if I don't know the right policy answer?

Say what you would do in principle, then that you would follow the school's protocol and check with your supervisor. Panels don't expect you to know policies before training.

### How do I answer how I'd balance RA work with classes?

Give a concrete system you already use, such as a weekly planner or fixed study hours, and a time you managed a busy term. Don't claim it will be easy.

### What should I avoid saying?

Avoid promising confidentiality you can't keep, saying you'd handle a crisis alone, or describing yourself as the "cool RA" who looks the other way.

### Should I talk to current RAs before the interview?

Yes, if you can. Ask what surprised them about the role and what their hall director values. It gives you real detail for "Why do you want to be an RA?"
