Babysitter and nanny interview questions and how to answer them

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Parents interviewing a babysitter or nanny ask what you'd do in an emergency, how you handle discipline, what you think about screen time and who can vouch for you. Answer emergency questions step by step, describe discipline you'd use that matches their rules, ask about their screen time limits instead of guessing, and have two or three references ready with phone numbers.

What parents are listening for

A babysitting or nanny interview usually happens at the family's kitchen table or on a video call, and the parents are asking one question underneath everything: can I trust this person alone with my children? They want to hear that you'll stay calm in an emergency, follow their rules rather than your own, and actually enjoy being with kids. They also watch how you talk to the children if they're around. Get down to their level and say hello.

Emergencies

Parents ask this first because it's their biggest fear. Answer in clear steps, and ask about their specifics.

Example answer (made-up details):

"If one of the kids got hurt, I'd first make sure the other child was safe and stay with the one who's hurt. For anything serious, like trouble breathing, heavy bleeding or not responding, I'd call emergency services right away, then call you. For small things like a scraped knee, I'd clean it up, comfort them and text you. Can I ask: does either child have allergies or medical conditions, and where do you keep the first aid kit and any medicine?"

Asking about allergies, medicines and the house layout shows you're thinking ahead. If you hold first aid or CPR training, say so and when it expires. Don't claim medical skills beyond your training.

Discipline

Parents want to know you'll follow their approach. Describe what you usually do, then ask how they handle it.

"With younger kids, I start by getting down to their level and saying what I need, simply: 'Blocks stay on the floor.' If it keeps happening, I give a choice: 'You can build with the blocks, or we put them away.' I follow through calmly. I never shout, and I'd never use physical punishment. How do you handle it when they don't listen? I'd like to keep it consistent with what you do."

For a tantrum in public:

"I'd stay calm, keep them safe, and not give in to what caused the tantrum. Once they've calmed down, I'd talk about it briefly. I'd tell you about it afterwards so you know."

Screen time

Don't share a strong personal opinion first. Ask about their rules, then show you'll stick to them and have other ideas ready.

"I'll follow whatever limits you set. What are they for each child? I usually plan the day so screens aren't needed, with things like a walk to the park, drawing, a simple baking project or building a fort. If you're fine with a show at a set time, I'd use it then and turn it off when it's done."

Have three or four activities ready for the ages of their children. Parents notice when you've thought about their kids specifically.

References and trust

Bring a written list of two or three references with names, phone numbers and how they know you: families you've babysat for, a teacher, a coach or an employer. Tell your references to expect a call.

Parents may also ask about background checks, driving, and social media. Answer directly: yes, you're happy to have a background check if they request one; you won't post photos of their children anywhere.

Mistakes to avoid

  • "I love kids" with no examples. Tell them about a family or age group you've cared for.
  • Strong opinions before you know their rules. Ask first.
  • Being on your phone during the interview. They'll picture you doing it on the job.
  • No references ready. It slows down or ends many decisions.

Questions people ask

What should I bring to a babysitting interview?

Your references list, any first aid or CPR certificate, your availability, and a few activity ideas for the children's ages.

What if I don't have paid babysitting experience?

Talk about younger siblings, cousins, neighbours, volunteering with kids or helping at a school or club. Describe specific things you did with them.

How should I talk about pay?

When they ask, give a clear hourly rate or range, and ask whether it changes for extra children, late nights or driving. It's normal to discuss this at the first meeting.

How do I answer "What would you do if you disagreed with us?"

Say you'd follow their rule while you're working and bring it up privately with them later, calmly, if it still worried you. It's their family and their decision.
