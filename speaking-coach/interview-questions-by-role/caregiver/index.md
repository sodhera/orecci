# Caregiver interview questions and how to answer them

Caregiver interviews, whether with an agency or a family, ask how you would handle a client who refuses care, how you protect dignity during bathing and toileting, how you respond to confusion from dementia, and whether you will show up. Answer with calm, specific steps, respect the client's choices, and say who you would tell when something changes.

## Who is interviewing you, and what they worry about

Private caregiving interviews happen in two places: at a home care agency, or at a family's kitchen table. Either way, the person across from you is trying to picture you alone in a house with someone vulnerable. They worry about three things: will you be kind when no one is watching, will you know when to call someone, and will you turn up.

If you've cared for a parent, grandparent or neighbor, that is real experience. Say so, and use those stories.

## When a client refuses care

*"Your client refuses to shower today."* This question tests whether you respect choice or force the task. Never say you would insist, and never say you would just skip it without telling anyone.

Example answer:

> "First I'd try to find out why. Often it's cold, or they're tired, or they feel embarrassed. I'd offer choices instead of a yes or no: 'Would you rather wash now or after breakfast?' or a strip wash instead of a shower. If they still say no, I'd respect it, try again later, and write it in the notes. If it keeps happening, I'd tell the agency or the family, because it might mean pain, fear of falling or something else changing."

The same pattern works for medication: follow the care plan and what your role allows, remind and encourage, never hide pills in food unless that is written in the plan, and report refusals.

## Dignity during personal care

Interviewers want to hear the small, concrete things you do, because that is what dignity actually looks like.

Example answer:

> "I knock and say who I am, even if the door's open. I explain each step before I do it. I keep them covered with a towel except for the part I'm washing, and I close curtains and doors. I let them do as much as they can themselves, even if it's slower, like washing their own face. And I talk to them like an adult, about normal things, not over their head."

## Dementia moments

A typical question: *"Your client keeps asking for her husband, who died years ago. What do you say?"* The interviewer is checking that you won't argue or correct her harshly.

Example answer:

> "I wouldn't tell her he's dead again, because she'd feel that grief like the first time. I'd go with the feeling instead: 'You're thinking about Frank. Tell me about him.' Then I'd gently move to something else, like making tea together or looking at photos. If she got very distressed or this was new behavior, I'd note it and tell the family or the agency."

## Reliability

Families often lose sleep over whether the caregiver will come. Answer this one with a plan, not a promise.

Example answer:

> "If I'm running late, I call before my start time, not after, and say exactly when I'll arrive. If I'm sick, I tell the agency as early as I can so they can find cover, because my client can't just wait. I keep my phone charged and have a backup way to get there if my car won't start."

## What they are really asking

| Question | What they worry about | What to include |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Client refuses care | You'll force it or ignore it | Find out why, offer choices, document, report patterns |
| Personal care | Embarrassment, rough handling | Knock, explain, cover, let them do what they can |
| Dementia confusion | Arguing, distress | Go with the feeling, redirect, report changes |
| A fall | Panic or moving them wrongly | Check, don't lift if hurt, call for help, tell family or agency |
| Lateness or sickness | Being left alone | Call early, give a time, have a backup |
| Family criticism | Defensiveness | Listen, ask what they'd like, agree a change |

## How to prepare

1. Write down three real moments from caring for anyone: a refusal, a moment of confusion, a hard day.
2. Make a short honest list of tasks you're comfortable with and any you're not. Families appreciate clarity.
3. Say your answers out loud. Calm, unhurried speech matters in this job. The [free speech review](/speaking-coach/tools/free-speech-review/) shows your pace when you practice.
4. Prepare two questions for the family, such as what a good day looks like for their relative.

Speaking Coach lets you describe the situation, such as "a daughter interviewing me to care for her father", and the partner plays her and follows up on what you say.

## Questions people ask

### Does caring for a family member count as experience?

Yes. Describe what you actually did: meals, bathing, medication reminders, appointments, night waking. Specific tasks tell an interviewer more than a job title.

### Do I need a certificate to be a private caregiver?

It depends on where you live and on the agency. Ask what training they require or provide, and mention any first aid or care training you have.

### How do I answer "Why do you want to be a caregiver?"

Give one real reason, ideally a person or moment, and connect it to the work itself, as in [why did you choose this career](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/why-did-you-choose-this-career/), such as helping someone stay in their own home. Avoid answers that are only about flexible hours.

### What if the family asks about something I'm not allowed to do?

Say clearly that it's outside your role or the care plan, and offer to check with the agency or help them find who can do it.

### How do I talk about burnout without sounding fragile?

Name what you do to recharge and say you'd speak up early if you were struggling. Interviewers trust caregivers who know their limits.
