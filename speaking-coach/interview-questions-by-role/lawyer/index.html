Lawyer interview questions: what they ask and how to answer

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Lawyer and associate interviews ask why this firm, about a matter you worked on, how you explain things to clients and how you perform under deadline pressure. Answer with specific, anonymized work: what the issue was, what you did yourself, the judgment call you made and the outcome, in about 90 seconds. Show you understand the firm's practice, not just its rankings.

This page is about interviews for lawyer and associate jobs: at law firms, in-house and in government. It doesn't cover law school admissions.

What interviewers are testing

Partners and hiring committees have read your CV. What they want to learn in the room is whether they'd trust you with a client and a deadline at 9 p.m. That comes down to four things:

TopicWhat they're really asking
Why this firmHave you done your homework, or is this one of 40 applications?
A matter you handledWhat did you do, and how good is your judgment?
Client communicationCan you explain law in plain words and give bad news well?
PressureWill you stay accurate and honest when the hours are long?

Junior associates get more questions about research, writing and learning. Lateral and senior associates get more about matters they led, client relationships and business they've brought in.

Why this firm

Rankings and "prestigious practice" are what everyone says. Name the work.

Example answer (made-up details):

"I want to do more cross-border disputes, and your arbitration group works on exactly the energy and construction cases I've been doing. I spoke to one of your senior associates last month, and she described how juniors here draft submissions rather than just doing document review. That's the training I'm looking for. And I'd bring three years of construction disputes from day one."

The why do you want to work here guide has a general structure that adapts well to firms.

Walking through a matter

This is the core of most associate interviews. Keep it confidential and keep it about your role.

  1. The matter, anonymized: "A commercial lease dispute for a regional retailer." No client names, no confidential facts.
  2. The issue: the legal question or problem in one sentence.
  3. Your role: what you personally drafted, researched, argued or negotiated.
  4. A judgment call: a decision you made or recommended, and why.
  5. The result, and what you'd do differently.

"I was the second associate on a contract dispute for a manufacturing client. The question was whether a limitation clause covered late delivery. I did the research and drafted the summary judgment brief. My judgment call was to drop our weakest argument, which the partner had wanted to keep, because it undercut our stronger reading of the clause. I explained why in a two-paragraph memo, and she agreed. The court granted partial summary judgment on our main argument."

Expect follow-ups that test depth: "What was the other side's best argument?" "What would you have done if you'd lost?" Know your matters well enough to answer.

Client communication

Show you lead with the answer, avoid jargon and handle bad news directly.

"When I had to tell a client their claim was weaker than they hoped, I called rather than emailed. I gave the bottom line in the first minute: we had a reasonable chance on one claim and a poor chance on the other. Then I explained why in plain terms, gave them options with rough costs and timelines and followed up in writing. They chose to settle the weaker claim and pursue the other."

The guide on explaining things simply helps with this skill in general.

Pressure and deadlines

Interviewers want to hear how you work when it's hard, not that you love long hours.

"Before a filing deadline last year, I had a brief due for one partner and a document production for another in the same week. I told both partners on the Monday, agreed which came first and asked a junior to take the first review pass on the production. I worked late two nights and filed on time. Afterwards I suggested we start production reviews a week earlier on that matter."

If you're asked about a mistake, choose a real one that was caught, say how you told the supervising lawyer and what changed.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Breaching confidentiality in your stories. Interviewers notice instantly, and it's disqualifying.
  • Claiming the whole team's work. Say "I drafted," "I argued," "the team decided."
  • Talking only about rankings and prestige. Name the practice and the work.
  • Criticizing your current firm. Explain what you want more of instead. See why you're leaving your current job.

Questions people ask

How much detail can I give about a matter?

Only what's public or fully anonymized. Describe the type of client, the issue and your role, never names or confidential facts.

How do I answer "Why law?" as a lateral or senior hire?

Focus less on why you went to law school and more on why this practice area and this stage of your career.

What if I'm asked a technical legal question?

Think out loud, state the general rule you know and say what you'd check. Admitting uncertainty with a clear approach is better than bluffing.

What should I ask at the end?

Ask how work is allocated, how associates get client contact, how feedback works and what a typical path to senior roles looks like.
