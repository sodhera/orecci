# Professor interview questions and how to answer them

Faculty and lecturer interviews test whether you can explain your research to people outside your subfield, show how you actually teach, run a teaching demo that engages students and contribute to the department beyond your own work. Prepare a 30-second and a two-minute version of your research, tie your teaching philosophy to one real course, and be specific about the service you'd take on.

## The parts of a faculty interview

Academic hiring usually has several stages: a first-round interview (often by video), then a campus visit with a job talk or research presentation, a teaching demo at many institutions, and meetings with faculty, students and the dean or chair. Teaching-focused colleges weigh the teaching demo heavily; research universities weigh the job talk.

| Stage | Who's listening | What they want |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First round | Search committee | A clear research story and a fit with their courses |
| Job talk | Whole department | Depth for specialists, clarity for everyone else |
| Teaching demo | Faculty, sometimes students | Students thinking, not just listening |
| One-to-ones | Faculty, chair, dean | Collegiality, plans, service |

## Your research in plain words

Most of the committee is outside your subfield. If they can't repeat your research to the dean, you're in trouble. Prepare two versions.

**The 30-second version:** the problem, why it matters, what you found.

Example (made-up details):

> "I study why some rivers flood worse after wildfires than others. Burned soil stops absorbing water, but the effect varies a lot. My work showed that soil type explains most of that difference, which helps towns decide where to put flood defenses first."

**The two-minute version** adds your method, your next project and how it could be funded. Name one or two specific grant programs or funders you'd apply to, and how your work would involve students.

Avoid jargon unless you define it. The [guide to explaining things simply](/speaking-coach/guides/how-to-explain-things-simply/) helps here.

## Teaching philosophy

Don't recite your teaching statement. Give one belief and immediately show it in a course.

> "I think students learn by doing the work of the field, not watching it. In my intro statistics course, every week starts with a real data set from the news. Students make one claim and defend it to a partner before I teach the method. Mid-semester feedback said that was the part they remembered most."

Then be ready for follow-ups: how you'd teach a large lecture, how you support students who struggle and how you'd adapt for non-majors.

## The teaching demo

You may teach a real class or faculty pretending to be students. Treat it as a class, not a talk.

1. **Pick a small topic** that fits the time, usually 15–30 minutes.
2. **Start with a question or problem** that students try before you explain.
3. **Get them doing something** every few minutes: a pair discussion, a quick poll, a sketch.
4. **Check understanding** with one question at the end.
5. **Finish on time.** Running over is noticed.

## The job talk and its Q&A

The job talk is closer to a [conference talk](/speaking-coach/presentations/conference-talk/), but the audience is broader. Spend the first few minutes on the big question in plain words before the detail. In Q&A, repeat or rephrase the question, answer briefly and admit limits honestly. "That's a good point. I haven't tested that yet, and here's how I would" is a strong answer.

## Service

Committees want colleagues who share the work. Be concrete: advising students, a committee you'd join, outreach, mentoring, a seminar series you'd help run. Link it to something you've already done.

## Mistakes to avoid

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Try instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A research pitch only specialists follow | Most of the committee is outside your subfield | Lead with the problem and why it matters |
| Reading your teaching statement aloud | Sounds rehearsed and abstract | One belief, one course, one result |
| Lecturing for the whole demo | Shows how you'll teach | Get students working early |
| Not knowing their courses | Looks like a generic application | Name courses you could teach from their catalog |

Say your 30-second research pitch out loud until it sounds like you talking, not your abstract. With Speaking Coach you can practice it with an AI partner who asks the follow-up a skeptical committee member would.

## Questions people ask

### How long should my answer about my research be?

Have a 30-second and a two-minute version. Start short and let them ask for more.

### What if I'm asked about a course outside my specialty?

Say what you could teach well, what you'd need to prepare and how you'd do it. Honest enthusiasm beats overclaiming.

### How do I handle a hostile question in my job talk?

Stay calm, thank them, answer the part you can and acknowledge the limit. See [how to handle Q&A](/speaking-coach/guides/how-to-handle-q-and-a/).

### What should I ask the committee?

Ask about teaching load, startup support, how tenure or promotion works in the department and what students in the program are like.
