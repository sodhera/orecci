# Event planner interview questions and how to answer them

Event planner interviews ask about an event that went wrong on the day, how you negotiate with vendors, how you keep to a budget and how you handle a difficult client. Answer with the event's size and budget, what broke, the fix you made in the moment, and what you put in place so it couldn't happen again.

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence. Updated 2026-10-22. Source: https://www.orecci.com/speaking-coach/interview-questions-by-role/event-planner/

## What the hiring manager is listening for

Whether it's an agency, a hotel, a venue or a corporate events team, the person hiring you wants to know that you stay calm when things break, that you protect the client's money, and that guests never see the panic. Every event planner has a disaster story. They're listening for how you tell it.

Give scale in every answer: number of guests, budget, how many vendors. "A 220-guest wedding with a $60,000 budget and nine vendors" sets the scene in one line.

## The event that went wrong on the day

This is the question that decides most event interviews. Use four parts: what broke, what you did in the first ten minutes, what the guests or client noticed, and what you changed afterwards.

Example answer (made-up details):

> "At a 300-person product launch, the AV company's projector failed 40 minutes before the doors opened. I had the venue's in-house tech on my contact sheet, so I called him first, and he had a backup projector up in 25 minutes. Meanwhile I asked the caterer to open the bar 15 minutes early so the delay felt like part of the evening. The client only found out at the debrief. Since then, every AV contract I write includes an on-site backup unit."

Notice what makes it work: a time, a contact you'd prepared in advance, a guest-facing decision, and a lasting change. Avoid stories where you fixed it by running around for six hours. That sounds like a lack of planning.

## Vendor negotiation

Interviewers want to hear that you negotiate for value, not just a lower price, and that vendors still want to work with you afterwards.

| Lever | What you can say |
| --- | --- |
| Off-peak dates | "We moved the gala from Saturday to Thursday and the venue dropped its minimum spend." |
| Package and volume | "I booked the same florist for three client events that season in exchange for a lower rate." |
| Payment terms | "I agreed to a larger deposit for a fixed price, which protected us from menu price rises." |
| Scope | "We swapped a plated dinner for food stations, which needed fewer staff." |

Example answer (made-up details):

> "The caterer's quote came in $8,000 over our line. Instead of asking for a discount, I asked what drove the cost. It was staffing for a plated service. We switched to stations with the same menu, saved $6,500, and the client said the room felt livelier."

## Budgets

Describe the tool and the habit. A typical answer: a spreadsheet or planning platform with every line item, a contingency of a set percentage, and a weekly check of committed against actual spend. Say what you do when a line goes over.

> "When the client added a photo booth late, I showed them it would come out of contingency and what that left us for on-the-day surprises. They chose a smaller package. I never move money between lines without the client seeing it."

## Difficult clients

They don't want a story about a terrible client. They want a story about how you kept the relationship.

1. **Listen fully first.** Let them finish before you explain.
2. **Name what they're worried about.** Often it's how the event will look to their guests or their boss.
3. **Offer two options with the trade-offs.** Don't argue for one.
4. **Put the decision in writing** the same day.

Practice saying these answers out loud, because tone carries most of the meaning with an upset client. Speaking Coach can play the client and push back so you can hear how calm you sound.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **"Nothing has ever gone wrong."** No one believes it, and it suggests you haven't run real events.
- **Blaming vendors.** Say what you did, not who failed.
- **No numbers.** Guest counts, budgets and timings make your stories believable.
- **Talking only about the creative side.** Logistics, contracts and money get people hired.

## Questions people ask

### How do I answer if I've only planned events as a volunteer or student?

Use them. A charity dinner or a university festival still has a budget, vendors and things that go wrong. Give the same scale numbers you would for a paid event.

### What should I bring to an event planner interview?

A short portfolio with two or three events: the brief, the budget, a run sheet or timeline, and photos. Be ready to talk through one run sheet line by line.

### How do I talk about an event that went badly overall?

Be honest about what didn't work, own your part, and spend most of the answer on what you'd do differently and what you changed in your process.

### What do corporate event interviews ask that wedding interviews don't?

Expect more on stakeholders, internal budgets and measuring results, such as attendance, leads or survey scores. Wedding roles focus more on families and emotional clients.

### How long should my "went wrong" story be?

About 60 to 90 seconds. Enough for the problem, your fix and what changed, and then let them ask follow-ups.
