# Substitute teacher interview questions and how to answer them

Substitute teacher interviews check whether you can walk into a room you've never seen, take control in the first five minutes, keep teaching when the lesson plan is missing and handle behavior without the regular teacher's relationships. Answer with a clear first-minutes routine, a backup plan and one real example, and show that you leave the teacher a useful note.

## What districts and agencies want

A substitute interview is usually short, often with a district HR person or an agency coordinator. They are not judging your lesson design. They want three things: you'll show up, you'll keep children safe, and you won't call the office every ten minutes. Every answer should show you are calm, prepared and self-sufficient.

## Your first five minutes

Most interviews ask some version of "You walk into a class you've never met. What do you do?" Have a routine and say it in order.

1. **Arrive early** and find the plans, seating chart, emergency procedures and any notes about students with medical needs.
2. **Stand at the door** and greet students as they come in.
3. **Introduce yourself** in one line and write your name on the board.
4. **Take attendance** using the seating chart, so you learn names fast.
5. **State two or three expectations** and the first task, then get them working within five minutes.

Example answer (made-up details):

> "I get there 20 minutes early to read the plans and find the fire exit and seating chart. I stand at the door and say hello to each student. Once they're seated I say: 'I'm Ms. Rivera, I'm here for Mr. Chen today. He left you a reading task and I'll be checking it at the end.' Then I take attendance by the chart, so I can use names straight away. A class that's working in the first five minutes is much easier to keep."

## When there's no lesson plan

This is the question that shows whether you're prepared. Don't say you'd wait for the office. Show you'd ask once, then teach.

> "I'd tell the office right away in case a plan exists somewhere. While I wait I start one of my backup activities, matched to the grade. I carry a folder of them. If no plan comes, I check the class's recent work in notebooks or on the board and continue from there, so the day still connects to what they're learning."

A good substitute kit might include:

| Item | Why it helps |
| --- | --- |
| Three short activities per grade band | Fills a period without a plan |
| A short read-aloud and discussion questions | Works for almost any age |
| Name tags or sticky notes | Learn names quickly |
| A blank note template for the teacher | Makes your end-of-day report fast |
| Pens, a whistle or timer | Small things you can't count on finding |

## Behavior without the relationship

Students test substitutes. Show that you stay calm, private and consistent, and that you follow the teacher's systems where you can. If they ask how you cope with a hard day, see [how to answer the stress question](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/how-do-you-handle-stress-and-pressure/).

> "In a seventh-grade class, two students kept talking over the reading. I walked over and stood near them while still reading aloud. When it continued, I quietly said, 'I need you to work, or I'll move you to the front.' One stopped; the other kept going, so I moved him and kept the lesson going. I wrote his name and what happened in my note for the teacher, along with the students who worked well."

For "Our teacher always lets us," a simple answer works: "I'll follow the plan your teacher left. If they normally allow it, I'll mention it in my note and they can decide next time."

## Short notice

Be honest about how fast you can get ready and how far you'll travel. "If you call by 6:30, I can be in the building by 7:45 anywhere in the district" is a stronger answer than "I'm always available."

## The note you leave

Mention it even if they don't ask. Teachers ask for subs who leave a good note. Include what was covered, what wasn't, who was absent, any behavior issues with names, and students who were helpful.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Saying you'd just "keep them busy."** Show you'll keep them learning.
- **Overpromising availability.** Say what you can actually do.
- **Planning to argue with students.** Calm, private and brief works better.
- **Forgetting safety.** Mention medical notes and emergency procedures.

## Questions people ask

### Do I need teaching experience to be a substitute?

Requirements vary by district and country. Check the job post. If you don't have classroom experience, use examples of leading groups, such as coaching, tutoring or camp work.

### How do I answer "Why substitute teaching?"

Be honest: flexibility, a path into teaching or a love of variety. Then add what you bring to a school on a hard day. The [why do you want this job guide](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/why-do-you-want-this-job/) has more examples.

### What should I wear to a substitute interview?

Dress as you would to teach: neat, comfortable and professional.

### What should I ask at the end?

Ask how assignments are offered, how much notice is typical, who to contact on site and whether there's orientation for new substitutes.
