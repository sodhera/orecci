Line cook interview questions and how to answer them

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Line cook interviews are short and practical: the chef wants to know if you can keep up on a full rush, hit ticket times without sending bad plates, follow food safety without reminders, and take a correction mid-service without sulking. Answer in kitchen language, with your station, your covers and a real night.

What a chef is actually checking

A line cook interview is often ten minutes at the pass, followed by a stage (a working trial shift). The talking part is a filter: can you describe kitchen work like someone who has done it? Chefs listen for specifics. "I worked sauté and fry at a 120-seat brasserie doing about 250 covers on Saturdays" tells them more than five minutes of "I'm passionate about food".

Keep answers short. Kitchens run on short sentences, and a cook who rambles in an interview is a cook who rambles on the line.

Working a busy pass

"Tell me about the worst rush you've worked" is the most common real question. Pick a real night and show how you stayed organized.

Example answer: "Mother's Day brunch, about 300 covers, and our egg cook went home sick at 10. I took eggs on top of the flat-top. I called back every ticket out loud so the chef knew I had it, grouped the same orders together, and asked the dish porter to keep my plates coming. We ran about five minutes behind for half an hour, then caught up. Afterwards I made a prep list for that station so the next cover would start ahead."

What makes it work: the number, what you did with your hands and your voice, and what you changed after. Calling back tickets ("Heard, two burgers mid-rare") is a habit chefs love to hear about, because a silent station is a scary one.

Ticket times and being in the weeds

QuestionWhat they want to hear
"How do you keep ticket times down?"Mise en place full before service, firing in the right order, reading the whole board, not just the next ticket
"What do you do in the weeds?"Say so early, out loud, so someone can help; never send a rushed plate
"What if a ticket's been waiting too long?"Tell the expo or chef, refire if needed, don't hide it
"How do you set up?"Your actual setup list, in order, with quantities for a busy night

Example answer for "in the weeds": "I say it early: 'Chef, I'm getting buried on grill.' Then I stop and reorganize for ten seconds, read the whole board, and fire the longest cooks first. I'd rather ask for help at minute two than send out a dry steak at minute ten."

Food safety

Expect at least one direct question. Answer with the habit, not just the rule.

  • Temperatures: the US CDC lists 165°F for poultry and 160°F for ground meats like beef and pork, and treats 40°F to 140°F as the danger zone where bacteria grow fast. Say how you check: "I probe every chicken breast in the thickest part."
  • Cross-contamination: separate boards and knives for raw proteins, raw meat stored below ready-to-eat food, gloves changed between tasks.
  • Labeling: date and label everything you prep, first in, first out.
  • Allergies: when a ticket says allergy, you clean down, use fresh tools, and tell the chef. Never guess.

If you hold a food handler card or similar certificate, mention it with the year you got it. Rules vary by city and country, so use the standards your last kitchen followed.

Taking criticism from a chef

"The chef tells you a plate is wrong in front of everyone. How do you react?" This checks whether you can be corrected at speed. The right answer is short: "Yes, Chef," fix it, and talk later if you need to.

Example answer: "In service I say 'Heard, Chef,' and I redo the plate right away. If I don't understand what was wrong, I ask quickly: 'Too much sauce, or the wrong sauce?' After service, if I think I was set up badly, say my sauce was made wrong by the prep shift, I'd mention it calmly. But never during the rush."

Mistakes to avoid

  • Saying you can work "any station" when you've only done fry. The stage will show it.
  • Badmouthing your last chef. Kitchens are small worlds; they may know each other.
  • Vague availability. Say exactly which nights you can work.
  • Turning up to a stage without your own knives, if the chef asked you to bring them.

How to practice

Say your "worst rush" story out loud and time it. It should be under a minute. Then have someone interrupt you with "So what did you fire first?" Interviews with chefs are blunt and full of interruptions. Speaking Coach's Tough pressure level gives you a partner who pushes back the same way.

Questions people ask

What is a stage or trial shift?

It's a working interview: you cook a few hours of prep or service on the line while the chef watches. Arrive early, listen, call back every instruction, and clean your station as you go.

What should I bring to a line cook interview?

Your knives if asked, non-slip shoes, and a clean apron or chef jacket if you have one. Bring a short list of your stations and kitchens.

I have no restaurant experience. Can I still get hired?

Yes, often as a prep cook first. Talk about speed, reliability and any food work you've done, and say you want to learn stations.

How do I answer "Where do you see yourself in five years?"

Be honest and kitchen-specific: running a station, then sous chef. The chef interview questions page shows what that next interview looks like.
