# Nursing interview questions and how to answer them

Registered nurse interviews test clinical judgment more than knowledge: how you keep patients safe, which patient you see first, how you calm a frightened family and how you escalate. Answer with one real shift each time, say exactly what you assessed, who you called and what happened, and keep it to 60–90 seconds.

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence. Updated 2026-10-22. Source: https://www.orecci.com/speaking-coach/interview-questions-by-role/nurse/

## What an RN interview is really checking

An experienced nurse interview is usually run by the unit manager, often with a charge nurse or staff nurses on the panel. They already know you can start an IV. What they are listening for is judgment: what you notice, what you do first, who you tell and how you talk to people when it is tense.

Generic answers ("I always put the patient first") tell them nothing. Clinical detail does. "Her respirations went from 16 to 28 and she was newly confused" is the kind of sentence that makes a panel lean in.

## The four question types, and what a strong answer shows

| Question type | Typical wording | A strong answer shows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Patient safety | "Tell me about a time you prevented harm." | A specific check you did, the escalation, and the follow-through |
| Prioritization | "Four call lights at once. Who first?" | A clear rule (airway, breathing, circulation; unstable before stable) and what you delegated |
| Difficult family | "How do you handle an angry family member?" | Listening for the fear underneath, a concrete next update, knowing when to bring in others |
| Clinical story | "Tell me about a time a patient deteriorated." | Assessment, SBAR call, intervention, outcome, in that order |

## Patient safety

Pick a moment where your check stopped something reaching the patient. Say what you checked, what you found, how you escalated and what changed afterwards. Mention the incident report or debrief: it shows you treat near misses as system problems, not secrets.

Example answer (made-up details):

> "On nights on a med-surg floor I took report on a patient on a heparin drip. At the bedside check the pump rate didn't match the latest order, which had changed after a lab result. I paused handoff, the off-going nurse and I checked the order together, and the charge nurse verified the corrected rate. I filed the report that shift, and our unit added a pump-to-order check to bedside handoff. Nothing reached the patient because we checked at the bedside, not at the station."

## Prioritizing several patients

Interviewers often give you a scenario: a patient with new chest pain, one asking for pain medication, a discharge waiting and a family wanting an update. Say your rule out loud, then apply it.

1. **Unstable first.** New chest pain gets assessed now: vitals, ECG per protocol, call the provider.
2. **Delegate what you safely can.** The CNA gets the discharge patient dressed and their belongings packed.
3. **Close the loop with everyone waiting.** "Tell Mr. Lee I'll be there with his medication in ten minutes."
4. **Come back in order.** Pain medication, then the family update, then the discharge teaching.

The panel wants a reason behind your order, not one "right" answer.

## A difficult family

Most angry families are frightened families who don't know what is happening. Show that you listen, name the concern, give a concrete next step and know when to bring in the provider, charge nurse or social work.

Example answer:

> "A patient's daughter met me at the door saying nobody had controlled her father's pain or told her anything all day. I stepped into the room with her rather than talking in the hallway, asked her what she'd seen, and she was right: his last dose had worn off. I reassessed him, gave his PRN, and paged the provider about a longer-acting plan. Then I told her I'd be back at 4 p.m. with the provider's answer, and I was. By evening she was asking me questions instead of shouting them."

## Building a clinical STAR answer

The [STAR method](/speaking-coach/guides/star-method/) works for nursing if you put most of your time into the action, where your judgment lives.

- **Situation (10 seconds):** unit, patient type, time of shift. No names or identifying details.
- **Task (5 seconds):** your role. Primary nurse, charge nurse, rapid response.
- **Action (40–60 seconds):** what you assessed, what you did, how you escalated. Say the SBAR call in your own words.
- **Result (15 seconds):** what happened to the patient, and anything that changed on the unit.

Have five stories ready: a deterioration you caught, a near miss, a family conflict, a disagreement with a physician and a heavy short-staffed shift. Most questions can be answered with one of those five.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Saying you have never made a mistake.** Every panel knows that's not true. Talk about a near miss and what you changed.
- **Telling the story without the escalation.** Who you called and what you said is often the whole point.
- **Blaming colleagues.** Describe a short-staffed shift as a problem you managed, not a complaint about management.

Say your five stories out loud before the interview, ideally to someone who interrupts. In Speaking Coach you can add the job post and the AI interviewer follows up on what you actually said, the way a charge nurse on the panel would.

## Questions people ask

### Do nursing interviews include clinical scenario questions?

Often, yes. Expect at least one "what would you do if" scenario, usually about prioritization or a deteriorating patient. Talk through your reasoning out loud rather than jumping to the answer.

### Should I talk about a mistake I made as a nurse?

Yes, if you pick one that was caught or corrected, and spend most of the answer on how you reported it and what changed. See [how to talk about a mistake](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/tell-me-about-a-time-you-made-a-mistake/).

### How long should a clinical example be?

About 60–90 seconds. Long enough to show your assessment and escalation, short enough that the panel can follow up.

### What should I ask at the end of a nursing interview?

Ask about the unit itself: typical patient ratios, how long orientation lasts, how floating works and how charge nurses support new staff. More ideas are in [questions to ask at the end](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/do-you-have-any-questions-for-us/).
