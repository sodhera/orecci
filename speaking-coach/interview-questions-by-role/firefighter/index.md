# Firefighter interview questions and how to answer them

Firefighter oral boards ask why you want to join the fire service, how you'll fit into station life, what you'd do in an ethics scenario involving the chain of command, and how you serve your community. Boards score how honest, humble and specific you are. Use real stories, keep each answer to about two minutes, and in ethics scenarios, start at the lowest level of the chain of command.

## How the oral board works

Most departments use a panel of two to five people: officers, firefighters and sometimes someone from HR or the city. Each person scores your answers on a sheet. They often can't help you or react much, so a quiet board doesn't mean you're doing badly. Speak to the whole panel, not just the person who asked.

Answers that score well are **specific** (real events, not adjectives), **humble** (you know you're starting at the bottom) and **honest** (you don't claim experience you don't have).

## "Why do you want to be a firefighter?"

This is often the first question, and the one candidates over-rehearse. Avoid "I've wanted to since I was a kid" on its own, and avoid sounding like you want excitement. Connect a real experience to the work the department actually does, which includes a lot of medical calls and public service.

Example answer for a candidate working as an EMT:

> "I've worked as an EMT for two years, and on most of my calls the engine company arrives first. I've watched how they take care of people who are scared and alone, and then go back to the station and train for the next one. I want that mix of service and teamwork, and I've spent the last year getting ready: my EMT card, the fire academy prerequisites, and volunteering at the station's community events."

## Station life

Boards want to know you'll fit in a house where people live, cook and train together. Show you understand that the newest firefighter does the chores, learns quietly, and earns trust.

> "As the newest firefighter I'd be first up and last to sit down. I'd do the chores without being asked, learn every piece of equipment on the rig, and ask the senior firefighters to teach me. I grew up in a big family and I've shared a house with four roommates, so I know living with people means pulling your weight and not letting small things fester."

## Chain-of-command ethics scenarios

These scenarios, like seeing a senior firefighter take cash from a victim's home or smelling alcohol on a crew member, test integrity and respect for the chain of command at the same time. A common, safe structure:

1. **Make sure the public and the crew are safe first.** If someone is impaired on a call, safety comes before everything.
2. **Go to the person directly,** privately, and give them the chance to put it right.
3. **If they don't, go to your immediate officer,** usually the captain.
4. **Keep going up the chain** if it still isn't dealt with.
5. **Never ignore it,** and never skip straight to the top unless the situation demands it.

Example answer to "You see a senior firefighter pocket cash at a fire scene":

> "Stealing from a victim is a crime and a betrayal of the public's trust, so I can't ignore it. I'd speak to him privately straight away and tell him what I saw, and that it needs to go back. Whatever he says, I'd report it to my captain, because it's not mine to decide whether it's handled. If the captain didn't act, I'd follow the department's policy for taking it higher. I'd rather be the rookie who spoke up than the one who looked away."

Note that this answer doesn't stop at a private word. For theft or anything illegal, boards expect you to report it. For smaller issues, like a sloppy chore, a private word is often enough.

## Community service

Departments do school visits, smoke alarm installs and public events. Show you already serve your community, in any form.

> "I coach a youth basketball team on Saturdays and I've helped at our neighbourhood's food drive for three years. Being a firefighter would let me do more of that with the department's school visits and safety events."

## Mistakes to avoid

| Mistake | Why it costs points | Try instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "I'd just tell the chief" | Skips the chain of command | Start low and go up |
| "I'd ignore it, he's senior" | Fails integrity | Act, respectfully and through the right steps |
| Adrenaline-focused reasons | Sounds immature | Talk about service and teamwork |
| Answers over three minutes | Panels lose the thread | About two minutes, then stop |
| Not knowing the department | Shows little effort | Know its stations, calls and community programs |

Practice your answers out loud with a timer; two minutes goes faster than you think. ## Questions people ask

### How long should oral board answers be?

Aim for 90 seconds to two minutes for most questions. Long enough for a real example, short enough that the panel can score it clearly.

### Should I mention my family members in the fire service?

You can, briefly, but don't rely on it. The board wants to know why *you* want the job and what *you* have done to prepare.

### What should I wear to a firefighter oral board?

A suit or smart business clothes, clean and pressed. Boards notice attention to detail.

### What if I don't know the answer to a scenario?

Take a breath, say how you'd keep people safe, and walk through the chain of command. A calm, reasoned answer scores better than a fast one.
