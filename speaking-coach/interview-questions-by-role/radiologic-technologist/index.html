Radiologic technologist interview questions and example answers

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Radiologic technologist interviews test safety and judgment more than physics: how you position a patient who can't cooperate, how you keep radiation dose as low as reasonably achievable, how you calm a frightened patient, and how you work fast in a trauma bay without moving an injured patient unsafely. Answer with a real exam, the steps in order, and how you checked your work.

What the imaging manager is listening for

Radiology interviews are usually with the department manager or lead technologist, sometimes with a short tour of the department. They know you passed your boards or are about to. They want to hear how you work when the patient, the order or the situation isn't textbook. Short, step-by-step answers that end with how you checked the image or the patient work best.

Positioning a patient who can't cooperate

Positioning questions are rarely about the standard projection. They're about the patient who can't hold it.

Example answer (made-up details):

"On a clinical rotation I had an elderly patient with a suspected hip fracture who couldn't roll or bend. Instead of moving her into a standard lateral, I explained what I'd do, kept her on the stretcher, and used a cross-table approach with the image receptor positioned beside her. I asked a nurse to help support her leg rather than doing it alone. Before she left I checked that the images showed what the order needed, and the radiologist didn't ask for repeats."

The pattern to show: adapt the technique to the patient, not the patient to the technique, get help, and check the result.

Radiation safety

Expect "How do you practice ALARA?" ALARA means keeping exposure as low as reasonably achievable, built on time, distance and shielding. Turn those principles into habits you actually do:

PrincipleWhat you could say you do
Time"I get the positioning right before exposing so I don't need repeats."
Distance"I step behind the barrier for every exposure and ask family to leave or wear protection if they must stay."
Shielding"I follow our department's shielding policy, which I know has changed in some places."
Collimation"I collimate to the area of interest, which reduces dose and improves the image."
Right exam, right patient"Two identifiers and a check of the order before every exam."

Also mention pregnancy screening: asking every patient of childbearing age as your department's policy requires, and escalating to the radiologist when the answer is yes or unsure.

A frightened patient

Many patients are anxious about scans, pain or results. Show what you say.

Example answer:

"A man in his thirties came in for a CT with contrast and told me he'd had a panic attack in a scanner before. I showed him the scanner first and explained that the CT ring is short and open, and that he'd hear my voice through the speaker. I told him the warm feeling from the contrast was normal before he felt it. We agreed he'd raise his hand if he needed me. He got through it, and he thanked me for telling him what was coming."

Lines that help:

"You'll hear a whirring sound. That's normal."

"I'll be right behind that window and I can hear you the whole time."

A trauma case

Trauma questions test speed, teamwork and spinal safety. Say the order of your actions.

  1. Arrive ready. Equipment in place before the patient arrives, if there's notice.
  2. Identify the patient using the trauma naming system your hospital uses.
  3. Follow the trauma team's priorities and the protocol for which images come first.
  4. Don't move the patient beyond what the team allows. Adapt your technique: cross-table views, angling the tube instead of turning the patient.
  5. Communicate. "Exposing in three, everyone clear or shielded."
  6. Check images immediately and flag anything urgent the way your department requires.

Example closing line: "In trauma I talk more, not less, because the room is loud and everyone needs to know when I'm exposing."

Mistakes to avoid

MistakeWhy it hurtsInstead
Reciting physics definitionsSounds like a textbook, not practiceTurn principles into what you do at the table
Moving a trauma patient for a better imageA safety riskAdapt the technique, follow the team
"I never need repeats"Not believableSay how you reduce them and learn from them
Ignoring the patient's fearLeads to motion and repeatsExplain, reassure, agree a signal

The trauma story needs to be clear when told out loud, in order, without jargon pile-ups. In Speaking Coach the AI interviewer can ask "Why that view?" after your answer, which is exactly what a lead tech will do.

Questions people ask

Should a new graduate use clinical rotation examples?

Yes. Rotation patients are real patients. Use them for positioning, frightened patient and trauma questions, and say clearly they were during clinicals.

Will they ask me technical questions?

Some managers ask about exposure factors, contrast reactions or specific projections. Answer briefly and tie it to safety.

How do I answer "Why radiology?"

Name a moment that made you choose it, such as seeing an image change a patient's care, and connect it to the kind of department you're applying to. See why did you choose this career.

What should I ask at the end?

Ask about the mix of modalities, how often techs rotate to the emergency department or portables, cross-training opportunities and how new techs are oriented.
