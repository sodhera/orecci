Childcare worker interview questions and how to answer them

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Daycare and childcare worker interviews are mostly about safety and routine: how you run the day, what you do when a child bites, how you handle and report an injury, and how you manage pickup when something isn't right. Answer with the steps you'd actually take, in order, and show that you follow the center's policies and tell the right people.

What a center director is checking

In a childcare interview, the director is picturing you alone in the room for ten minutes with eight toddlers. Every question comes back to: will these children be safe, cared for and kept to their routine? Directors also listen for whether you'd follow their policies or do your own thing.

If the job is more about teaching and planning activities, see the preschool teacher questions. For care roles, the strongest answers sound like steps. "First I'd… then I'd… then I'd tell…" Specific, calm, in order.

Routines

You'll often be asked to walk through a day. You don't need exact times, but show you know why routines matter for small children: they feel safe when they know what's next.

Part of the dayWhat you'd mention
ArrivalGreeting each child and parent, a quick check on sleep and health, a calm handover
Meals and snacksHandwashing, allergy lists, sitting with children, encouraging self-feeding
Play and activitiesIndoor and outdoor, a mix of free play and short group activities
Rest timeA quiet routine, checking sleeping children as required, letting non-sleepers rest quietly
TransitionsWarnings before changes, songs, headcounts every time you move
PickupChecking authorization, sharing one thing about the child's day

Example answer (made-up details):

"In a toddler room, I give a two-minute warning before every change, like 'Two more minutes, then we wash hands for lunch,' and use the same tidy-up song each time. Before we go outside and when we come back in, I count every child and say the number to my colleague. At pickup I tell each parent one specific thing, like 'Maya built a tower and counted the blocks.'"

A child who bites

Biting is common in toddler rooms, and directors want to know you won't panic or shame anyone.

  1. Care for the bitten child first. Comfort them, wash the area and check the skin.
  2. Speak to the child who bit, calmly and briefly: "No biting. Biting hurts." Then redirect.
  3. Write the incident up for both children, following the center's form.
  4. Tell both families the same day, without naming the other child.
  5. Look for a pattern: time of day, teething, crowding, a trigger. Plan with the lead teacher.

"One boy in my room bit three times in a week, always just before lunch. We realized he was hungry and tired, and the play area got crowded then. We gave him a small snack earlier and set up a quiet corner before lunch, and the biting stopped. I told his parents what we'd noticed and what we changed, and kept the other families updated without naming him."

Injuries and reports

Show first aid, calm communication and accurate records.

"If a child fell and bumped their head, I'd stay with them, comfort them and check them according to our first aid training, calling for the lead or first aider. I'd follow the center's head injury policy, which usually means calling the parent and watching the child closely. Then I'd fill in the injury report: what happened, the time, who saw it, what first aid was given. I'd write only what I saw, and get the parent to sign it at pickup."

Mention your first aid or CPR training if you have it.

Parent pickup

Directors test this with tricky scenarios. Show you'd follow the policy even when it feels awkward.

  • Someone not on the list: Don't release the child. Politely explain, call the parent and involve the director.
  • An authorized adult you don't recognize: Check photo ID against the file.
  • A parent who seems unfit to drive: Don't argue alone. Follow the center's policy and involve the director straight away.
  • A parent who is very late: Stay calm with the child, call the contact numbers and follow the late pickup policy.

"I'd say, 'I'm sorry, I can't let Leo go with you, as you're not on his pickup list. Let me call his mum.' Then I'd keep Leo with me and get the director."

Questions people ask

Do I need qualifications to work in childcare?

Requirements vary by state and country. Many centers require background checks and first aid training. Check the job post.

What if I don't have childcare work experience?

Use babysitting, younger siblings, camp or volunteering. The babysitter and nanny questions show how to turn that into examples. Show you understand routines and safety, and that you'll follow policies.

How do I answer "How would you handle a crying child at drop-off?"

Describe a calm, quick goodbye, a familiar comfort or activity and a short update to the parent later. Most children settle soon after the parent leaves.

What should I ask at the end?

Ask about ratios, the age group you'd work with, how staff are trained and how the center communicates with families. More ideas in questions to ask at the end.
