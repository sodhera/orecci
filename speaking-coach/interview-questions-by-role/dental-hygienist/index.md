# Dental hygienist interview questions and how to answer them

Dental hygienist interviews focus less on technique and more on how you talk to patients: how you explain gum disease without scaring or lecturing, how you get a patient to accept treatment, how you stay on time when the schedule slips, and how you calm someone who dreads the chair. Answer with a real patient situation, the words you used and what the patient did next.

## What the dentist or office manager is listening for

Most hygiene interviews are with the dentist who owns the practice, sometimes with the office manager, and many include a working interview: a paid day treating patients. In the sit-down part, they're picturing you with their patients. Can you keep a full schedule, explain treatment so patients say yes, and make nervous people want to come back?

Your clinical skill matters, but they'll see it on the working day. In the interview, show how you talk.

## Patient education that doesn't sound like a lecture

Interviewers often ask how you'd get a patient to floss or brush better. The weak answer is "I explain why it's important." The strong answer shows a method:

1. **Ask first.** "What does your routine look like at home?"
2. **Show, don't tell.** Hand them a mirror and point to the bleeding area.
3. **Pick one change.** "If you only add one thing, try floss picks at night in front of the TV."
4. **Follow up next visit.** "Last time we talked about the back molars. Let's see how they look."

Example line you could use in the interview:

> "I try to leave every patient with one thing to change, not five. Most people won't do five."

## The gum disease conversation

This is the question most hygiene interviews include in some form, because it affects whether patients accept periodontal treatment. Show that you explain clearly, avoid blame and give them a choice.

Example answer (made-up details):

> "I had a patient in his fifties who hadn't been in for four years. His probing depths were five and six millimeters in several areas and his gums bled a lot. He said nothing hurt, so he didn't see the problem. I showed him his chart on the screen and compared it to a healthy one, and explained that gum disease usually doesn't hurt until it's advanced. I avoided 'you should have come sooner.' I said, 'This is very treatable now, and the deep cleaning stops it getting worse.' The dentist confirmed the diagnosis at the exam. He booked the first quadrant before he left."

What makes it work: plain words, a visual, no blame and a clear next step.

## Schedule pressure

Running behind is constant in hygiene. Interviewers want to see that you protect the patient's care and the rest of the day.

| Situation | What you could say you do |
| --- | --- |
| Running 15–20 minutes behind | Tell the front desk right away so they can warn the next patients |
| A patient arrives late | Do what's clinically needed in the time left and rebook the rest, following office policy |
| The dentist is slow to come for the exam | Use the time for education or charting, and give the dentist a one-line summary when they arrive |
| A new patient needs a full chart | Ask for extra time to be booked for new patients if it keeps happening |

A line that lands: "I'd rather tell a patient I'm running ten minutes late than rush their cleaning."

## Anxious patients

Many adults dread the dental chair. Show a few concrete habits.

Example answer:

> "I had a patient who gripped the armrests from the moment she sat down. I asked what had happened before, and she said a past cleaning had really hurt. I agreed a stop signal with her, raising her left hand, and told her what I was about to do before each step. I checked in every few minutes. Halfway through she let go of the armrests. She asked for me by name at her next visit."

Other things to mention: explaining sensations before they happen, offering breaks, and talking to the dentist about options for comfort when needed.

## Mistakes to avoid

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Using clinical jargon in your example lines | Patients don't understand "pocket depths" | Say what you'd actually tell the patient |
| Scaring patients into treatment | Patients avoid coming back | Calm facts, a visual and a choice |
| "I never run late" | Not believable | Say how you handle it when you do |
| Criticizing your current dentist | The new dentist imagines being next | Say what you're looking for instead |

## Questions people ask

### What is a working interview for a hygienist?

It's a trial day or half day where you treat the practice's patients so the dentist can see your clinical work and chairside manner. Ask beforehand how it's paid and what equipment you'll use.

### How do I answer "Why do you want to leave your current practice?"

Keep it about what you want next, such as more time per patient, a periodontal focus or a shorter commute, without criticizing your current office. See [why are you leaving your current job](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/why-are-you-leaving-your-current-job/).

### Should a new graduate hygienist mention clinic patients from school?

Yes. School clinic patients are real patients. Use them for education, anxious patient and treatment acceptance stories.

### What should I ask the dentist at the end?

Ask how long hygiene appointments are, whether you'll have an assistant, how the dentist handles exams during your appointments and what the periodontal program looks like.
