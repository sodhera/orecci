Server and waitress interview questions and how to answer them

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Server and waitress interviews test how you handle the four moments of a shift that go wrong or get busy: a wrong order, a packed section, selling the specials and keeping things smooth with the kitchen. Answer with what you'd actually say at the table and to the cooks, and show you stay calm, own mistakes and keep every table looked after.

What a restaurant manager is hiring for

A restaurant manager interviewing servers is imagining a Friday night at 8 p.m., with your section full, a ticket wrong and a two-top waving for the check. They want someone who stays calm, owns mistakes without drama, sells naturally and doesn't start a war with the kitchen. Some places will ask you to describe a dish or do a short role-play, so answer as if you're already at the table.

A wrong order

Interviewers want ownership and speed, not blame. Four steps:

  1. Apologize without excuses. "I'm so sorry, that's not what you ordered."
  2. Fix it fast. Take the plate and tell the kitchen right away, clearly and calmly.
  3. Look after them while they wait. Offer something small if the restaurant allows it, or check back so they're not forgotten.
  4. Tell your manager, especially if the guest is upset or a comp might be needed.

Example answer (made-up details):

"A guest ordered her burger with no onions, and it came out with onions. I said, 'I'm sorry, that's my mistake,' because I'd forgotten to key the modification. I took it straight back, told the grill cook exactly what was wrong, and asked for it on the fly. I let my manager know, and he sent over fries for the table while they waited. I checked back two minutes later so they knew it was coming. Since then I read every modification back to the guest before I leave the table."

Saying "my mistake" plainly, even when the kitchen might share the blame, is what managers listen for. For really upset guests, see how to handle an angry customer.

A busy section

Show a system for deciding what comes first and doing several things per trip.

"My first priority is greeting new tables within a minute, even just 'Hi, I'll be right with you,' so nobody feels ignored. Then I go in order of what's time-sensitive: food that's up in the window, checks people are waiting on, then drink refills. I never walk in or out of the kitchen empty-handed: I'll take a dirty plate back and bring water out. If I'm truly underwater, I tell my manager or ask a teammate to run my food, before tables start complaining."

Upselling specials

Managers want to hear that you sell by describing, not by asking yes-or-no questions.

Try the dish before you describe it, if you can, and use two or three words about how it tastes. Honest enthusiasm sells more than a script.

Teamwork with the kitchen

This question is checking whether you'll be calm and respectful under pressure, because tension between front and back of house ruins shifts.

"When a plate goes back, I tell the cook what's wrong, not that the guest is 'complaining.' I make sure my tickets are clear and any modifications are entered correctly, so I'm not the problem. If food is sitting in the window for another server's table, I'll run it. Once, a cook snapped at me during a rush because I'd rung in a table late. I said 'You're right, my fault,' and after the rush I asked how he'd like me to handle late tables. We were fine after that."

Mistakes to avoid

  • Blaming the kitchen for a wrong order in your story.
  • Saying you can "handle anything." Show how you handle a full section instead.
  • Guessing about allergens. Say you'd check with the kitchen or manager every time.
  • Unclear availability. Restaurants are busiest when most people aren't working.

Say the specials and the wrong-order answer out loud before your interview, because it's what you'd do every shift. Speaking Coach's AI partner can play an annoyed guest and react to what you actually say.

Questions people ask

What if I've never been a server?

Use any experience where you served people, worked fast or worked in a team: fast food, retail, catering at events, a family business. Say you're keen to learn the menu and the systems.

Should I mention tips?

No. Focus on giving guests a good experience; good service and good tips go together, and managers know it.

What should I say about allergens?

Say you'd never guess. You'd check with the kitchen or manager, tell the guest what you found out and make sure the ticket is clearly marked.

What should I wear to a server interview?

Neat, clean clothes close to the restaurant's style, such as black trousers and a plain shirt for most places. Tie long hair back.

How do I answer "Why this restaurant?"

Eat there first if you can, and mention a dish or something about the service you liked. It shows you'd describe their menu with real enthusiasm. More ideas are in why do you want to work here.
