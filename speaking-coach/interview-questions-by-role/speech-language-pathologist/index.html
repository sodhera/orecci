Speech-language pathologist interview questions and answers

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Speech-language pathologist interviews test how you plan and communicate: how you manage a large caseload, how you write measurable goals, how you bring families into therapy, and why you fit this setting, whether it's a school, a clinic or a hospital. Answer with one real client or student (never identifiable), say what you assessed, what you did and how you measured progress.

What the hiring panel is listening for

SLP interviews look different by setting. A school panel might be a principal, a special education director and another SLP. A hospital or rehab interview might be a rehab manager and a lead therapist. Both want the same core things: organized caseload management, measurable goals, clear communication with families and colleagues, and a real reason for choosing their setting.

Keep every example free of identifying details: age range, setting and diagnosis are enough.

A caseload you managed

Give numbers and a system. "I'm very organized" isn't an answer.

Example answer for a school setting (made-up details):

"In my last district I had 58 students across two elementary schools, from articulation to students using communication devices. I grouped students by goal type, not just grade, so most sessions were groups of two or three with similar targets. I kept a single calendar with every evaluation and plan deadline, color-coded by month, and blocked Friday afternoons for paperwork. I didn't miss a deadline that year, and I still saw my students with the most complex needs individually."

For a medical setting, swap in daily patient numbers, productivity targets and how you prioritize new referrals such as swallowing evaluations.

Goal writing

Expect "Write a goal out loud for this student" or "How do you make goals measurable?" Show the parts of a measurable goal.

PartExample
Who"The student will"
Does what"produce /s/ in the initial position of words"
Under what conditions"in structured conversation with one visual cue"
How well"with 80% accuracy"
How measured, by when"across three consecutive sessions, by the end of the plan year"

Add how you choose goals: from assessment results, what matters in the classroom or daily life, and what the family cares about most. A line that lands: "I write goals a teacher or parent could check without me."

Working with families

Families keep progress going between sessions. Show how you explain, involve and listen.

Example answer:

"A father brought his three-year-old for an evaluation because daycare had raised concerns, but he felt his son was just a late talker. I started by asking what he noticed at home, and he told me his son pointed and pulled him toward things instead of using words. I connected my findings to exactly that. Instead of a long report, I gave him two strategies to try at bath time and meals, and showed him once. At the next session he told me his son had started saying 'more' at dinner. After that he asked for new strategies every week."

Lines you might use with families:

"You know your child best. Tell me what you see at home."

"Pick one routine each day to practice. That's enough."

School versus medical settings

Panels often ask why you want their setting, especially if you're switching. Be honest about what draws you.

  • School: language and literacy in the classroom, working with teachers, a long relationship with each student, the school calendar.
  • Medical: swallowing and adult neurogenic disorders, fast-paced teamwork, seeing recovery over weeks.

If you're moving from one to the other, name one transferable skill: schools teach caseload management and family communication; medical settings teach rapid assessment and interdisciplinary work.

Questions people ask

What should a clinical fellow emphasize?

Your graduate placements, the populations you've worked with and your supervision plan. Show that you seek feedback and know when to ask your supervisor.

How do I answer questions about a client who didn't progress?

Explain what you saw, what you changed (approach, frequency, home program) and whether you referred or consulted. Show that you used data to decide.

Do school SLP interviews ask about IEP meetings?

Often. Be ready to explain how you prepare, present results in plain language and handle disagreement from parents or staff.

What should I ask at the end?

Ask about caseload size, how many schools or units you'd cover, materials and assessment budgets, and how SLPs are supported with paperwork time.
