Home health aide interview questions and how to answer them

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Home health aide interviews check whether an agency can trust you alone in someone's home: how you handle a safety concern with no one else there, how you work with family members who have strong opinions, how you report changes to your supervisor, and how you write down the care you gave. Answer with a real visit, say what you noticed, who you called and what you wrote.

What the agency is really asking

Home health interviews are usually short, often a phone screen and then a meeting with a nurse supervisor or coordinator. They have one big worry: you'll be alone with a vulnerable person and nobody will be watching. So nearly every question is about trust. Will you follow the care plan, call when something changes, keep the client safe and write down what you did?

Simple, honest answers with a real example are best. If you've cared for a parent or grandparent, that counts. Say so.

Working alone in a client's home

Show that you like independence and that you know when to call.

Example answer (made-up details):

"I cared for my grandmother for two years before she moved into a care home, so I'm used to being the only one there. I like working on my own and keeping a routine. But I also know I'm not the nurse. If something changes, like she's more confused than usual or won't eat, I call the office first and write it down, even if it seems small."

Safety concerns

You may be asked two kinds of safety question: the client's safety and your own.

SituationWhat you could say you'd do
Client is on the floor when you arrive"I wouldn't lift them alone. I'd check if they're hurt and talk to them, call emergency services if they're hurt or I'm unsure, then call my supervisor and follow the agency's fall procedure."
Client seems suddenly confused or unwell"Call my supervisor right away, or emergency services if it looks serious, and stay with them."
A hazard in the home, like loose rugs or no working smoke alarm"Tell my supervisor so the nurse or family can deal with it, and write it down."
You feel unsafe (an aggressive visitor, weapons, threats)"Leave politely, get somewhere safe, and call my agency. My safety comes first so I can keep caring for clients."

A line that lands: "I follow the care plan, and when something isn't in it, I call before I act."

Family members

Families love their relatives and sometimes tell you to do things differently. Show you stay kind and stick to the plan.

Example answer:

"A client's daughter told me to stop helping her mother walk to the kitchen because she was scared of a fall. Walking was in the care plan from the physical therapist. I told her I understood her worry, explained that the walk was part of her mother's plan, and showed her how I used the gait belt. I still called my supervisor that afternoon so the nurse could talk to her. She came to trust me after that and started asking me how her mother was doing each visit."

Things to say you'd never do: give medication you're not allowed to give, change the care plan because a family member asked, or take sides in family arguments.

Documenting care

Agencies need accurate visit notes for safety and billing. Show your habits.

  1. Write it during or right after the visit, not at the end of the day.
  2. Write what you did and what you saw, not opinions. "Ate half of lunch, said she wasn't hungry," not "had a bad day."
  3. Note changes in eating, skin, mood, mobility or toileting, and call the office about them.
  4. Use the agency's system, whether it's an app or paper, and clock in and out correctly.
  5. Never sign for care you didn't give.

Dignity and refusals

Expect a question about bathing, toileting or a client who says no. Show respect.

"I explain what I'm about to do, keep them covered, and let them do whatever they can for themselves. If they refuse a shower, I don't force it. I ask why, offer a sponge bath or a different time, and if they still refuse, I write it down and tell my supervisor."

Mistakes to avoid

MistakeWhy it hurtsInstead
"I'd handle it myself" for an emergencySounds unsafeSay who you'd call and in what order

Questions people ask

Do I need experience to become a home health aide?

Requirements depend on your state and the agency, and some require training or certification. If you've cared for a family member, use that experience in your answers.

What is the difference between a home health aide and a caregiver?

Home health aides usually work under a nurse's care plan and may help with some health-related tasks; caregivers often focus on companionship and daily living. Ask the agency what this role includes.

How do I answer "Why do you want this job?"

Give a real reason, such as caring for a relative, and say what you enjoy about helping people stay in their own homes.

What should I ask at the end?

Ask about training, how visits are scheduled, travel time and mileage, who you call after hours, and how many clients you'd see in a day.
