Operations manager interview questions and how to answer them

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Operations manager interviews ask you to explain a process you improved, the KPIs you owned and moved, how you handled a supplier or staffing failure, and how you cut costs without breaking service. Answer each with a baseline, what you changed, the result in numbers, and the trade-off you managed, because interviewers will ask about the trade-off anyway.

What operations interviews test

Operations roles differ a lot: a warehouse, a clinic network, a software company's support team. The questions are similar everywhere. The hiring manager wants evidence that you find the real bottleneck, fix it with the people who do the work, and can show the result in numbers. Every answer should have a before, a change, and an after.

Improving a process

"Tell me about a process you improved."

Example answer (figures are illustrative):

"Order-to-dispatch at our warehouse averaged 26 hours, and customers were complaining about late deliveries. I walked the process with the pick team for two days and found orders sat in a queue for hours because picking was batched by shift, not released continuously. We changed to releasing orders every 30 minutes and moved packing stations closer to the fastest-moving stock. In eight weeks, dispatch time dropped to about 14 hours, with no extra staff. The trade-off was more frequent pick runs, so we tracked picker walking time to make sure we hadn't just moved the problem."

Note the trade-off at the end. Interviewers trust answers that name one. The general version is tell me about a time you solved a problem.

KPIs you owned

Don't list ten metrics. Pick two or three you were accountable for and say how you moved one.

KPIGood way to talk about it
On-time deliveryBaseline, target, what you changed, result, how you measured it
Cost per unit or per orderWhat drove cost, what you cut, what you protected
Labor productivityUnits per hour, and how you kept safety and quality steady
Error or defect rateRoot cause found, fix, whether it stayed fixed
Staff turnoverWhat you changed in scheduling or training, and the result

Example line: "I owned on-time delivery, cost per order and safety incidents. On-time went from 91 to 97 percent over two quarters, mainly by fixing carrier cut-off times."

A supply or staffing failure

"Tell me about a time a supplier let you down" or "How did you handle a sudden staffing shortage?" They want calm triage, then a fix so it doesn't happen again.

Example answer:

"Our main packaging supplier missed two deliveries during peak, and we had about three days of boxes left. First, triage: I prioritized our biggest customers' orders and switched slower lines to an alternative box size we had in stock. I called two backup suppliers the same day and got an emergency order at a higher price, which I flagged to finance immediately. Then I told sales which customers might see delays so they heard it from us first. Afterwards we qualified a second supplier and set a minimum stock level that triggers a reorder earlier."

For staffing, the same shape works: protect what matters most, borrow or reassign people, tell customers early, then fix the root cause, such as cross-training or a standby pool.

Cutting costs

Interviewers fear the manager who cuts until service breaks. Show what you protected.

Example answer:

"Leadership asked for a 10 percent cut in operating costs. I looked at the biggest lines first, not the easiest. Overtime was the largest controllable cost, and most of it came from poor scheduling on Mondays. We moved two people's shifts and used a short-term temp only for peak weeks, which cut overtime by about half. We renegotiated our waste contract too. I refused to cut the safety training budget, and I said so in the plan, so the trade-off was visible."

Getting people to adopt change

Many ops answers skip the people. Mention them: you involved the team in finding the problem, ran a small pilot, listened to their objections, and let a respected team member explain the change. "We piloted it on one shift first" is a strong sentence in any ops interview.

How to prepare

  1. Pick three stories with numbers: a process improvement, a failure you handled, a cost cut.
  2. For each, know the baseline, the change, the result and the trade-off.
  3. Practice telling each in under two minutes. Results go in the last 20 seconds, not lost in the middle.
  4. Rehearse with someone who asks "How did you measure that?" Speaking Coach's interviewer follows up on what you actually say, and you can add the job post so questions match the role.
  5. Prepare a first-30-days answer: what would you do in your first 90 days has a structure.

Questions people ask

What if my numbers are confidential?

Use percentages or rounded figures and say so. "Cut overtime by about half" tells the story without exposing exact costs.

How do I talk about a target I missed?

Give the target, what happened, what you learned about the cause, and what you changed. Owning it shows judgment.

Do I need to know their industry?

It helps, but operations skills transfer. Show how you find bottlenecks and lead change, and ask good questions about their process.

How technical should I get?

Match the interviewer. With a senior leader, lead with results. With an ops peer, go deeper into the method when they ask.

What should I ask at the end?

Ask which KPI they most want moved in the first year and what has stopped it moving so far.
