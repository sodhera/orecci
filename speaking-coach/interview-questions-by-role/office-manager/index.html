Office manager interview questions and how to answer them

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Office manager interviews ask how you run facilities and vendors, how you manage and save on an office budget, how you onboard new people, and how you kept the office calm when something went wrong. Answer with the systems you set up and the results, like a cost you cut or a first week you redesigned, and show you can handle many requests without dropping any.

What the hiring manager wants

An office manager is the person everyone goes to when something isn't working. The interviewer, often a COO, finance lead or founder, wants someone who runs the place quietly: things get fixed before people notice, money isn't wasted, and new starters feel looked after. They'll test whether you think in systems or just react.

Good answers include a number: a budget size, a saving, how many people you onboarded, how fast something was fixed.

Facilities and vendors

"How do you choose and manage vendors?"

Example answer (figures are illustrative):

"For anything recurring, like cleaning, catering or IT support, I get at least three quotes and check references from similar-sized offices. I write down what good looks like in the contract: response times, what's included, how we'll review. Then I keep a simple vendor sheet with renewal dates so nothing auto-renews without a decision. At my last company, reviewing contracts that way saved about 15 percent on cleaning and catering in the first year, mostly by cutting services nobody was using."

Budget

Example answer:

"I managed an office budget of around $250,000 a year covering rent extras, supplies, food, equipment and events. I tracked it monthly against plan in a spreadsheet shared with finance, so there were no surprises at year end. When we had to cut 10 percent, I asked the team which perks they actually valued. They wanted good coffee and lunch on Fridays, not the expensive snack delivery. We cut the snacks, kept the coffee, and nobody complained."

The last part answers the follow-up "without upsetting people" before they ask it.

Onboarding new staff

"Walk me through a new starter's first week." Show the steps in order.

  1. Before day one. Laptop ordered, accounts set up, desk and access card ready, welcome email with time, place and who to ask for.
  2. Day one. Greeted at the door, tour, lunch with their team or a buddy, a simple first-week schedule.
  3. First week. Meetings with key people booked in advance, office how-tos (printing, booking rooms, expenses) in one short document.
  4. End of week one. A five-minute check-in: what's missing, what confused them?
  5. After a month. Ask what you'd change for the next person.

Example line: "My goal is that nobody spends their first morning waiting for a laptop."

Keeping calm in a crisis

Pick a real event: a flood, a power cut, a building alarm, a failed move. Tell it in order and end with what you changed.

Example answer:

"On the morning of a big client visit, a pipe burst above our main meeting room. I moved the meeting to the second-largest room and had facilities set up the screen there, and I told the host what had changed before the client arrived so they weren't surprised. I sent one clear message to the whole office: which rooms were closed, where to go, and when I'd update next. I called the landlord and our insurer, and took photos for the claim. The client never knew. Afterwards I made a one-page plan for losing a room or the internet, with backup locations and contacts."

One clear update to everyone beats lots of individual replies.

Competing requests

Two managers want the same room or your time at once. Say how you decide: urgency, business impact, and who's affected, then tell both people what you've done. The conflicting tasks question covers this in more detail.

How to prepare

  1. Pick three stories with numbers: a saving, an onboarding improvement, and a crisis.
  2. Know the size of the offices and budgets you've run.
  3. Practice the crisis story out loud in under two minutes. Speaking Coach's interviewer follows up on what you said, such as "What would you do if that room was also booked?"
  4. Look at the company's office and team size, and prepare one question about what isn't working today.

Questions people ask

What's the difference between an office manager and an executive assistant interview?

Office manager interviews focus on the whole office: facilities, vendors, budget and people. Executive assistant interviews focus on supporting one or two executives.

How do I show I can handle many requests at once?

Describe the system you use, such as a shared request form or ticket list, and a day when it kept things from slipping.

What if I've never managed a budget?

Talk about any spending you tracked, such as supplies or events, and say how you'd learn the finance process quickly.

Should I mention HR tasks?

If the role includes them, yes, such as onboarding or benefits admin. Be clear about what you've done and where you'd need support.

What should I ask at the end?

Ask what the biggest frustration in the office is right now and what the last office manager did well.
