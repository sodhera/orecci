LPN interview questions: what they ask and how to answer

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

LPN interviews test whether you work safely inside your scope: how you run an accurate med pass on a busy hall, what you report to the RN and when, what you do when you're asked to do something outside your license, and how you keep long-term care residents' routines on track. Answer with a real shift, say what you checked, who you told and what happened.

What the director of nursing is listening for

LPN interviews, especially in long-term care, are often with the director of nursing or a unit manager. They're asking themselves: will this nurse pass meds safely on a hall where they barely know the residents yet, catch a change early, and tell the RN at the right time? Show you know your scope, your routine and your reporting line.

Scope of practice varies by state, so speak in terms of your state's rules and the facility's policies. "Within my scope in this state" is a fine phrase to use.

Scope of practice

Expect a direct question, often followed by a scenario where you're asked to do something you shouldn't.

Usually covered by "what you do" answersUsually covered by "what I'd hand to the RN" answers
Med passes and treatments as orderedInitial comprehensive assessments, where your state reserves them for RNs
Collecting data: vitals, observations, wound measurementsChanging the care plan
Reporting changes promptlyTasks your state or facility limits to RNs, such as some IV tasks

Check your own state's rules before the interview so your examples match.

Example answer to the scenario:

"If I were asked to do something outside my scope, I'd say so politely and explain why, offer to help with the parts I can do, and get the RN or supervisor involved right away. I'd rather slow things down for a few minutes than put a resident or my license at risk."

The med pass

Walk through your routine and the safety checks inside it.

  1. Get report and check for new orders and held medications before starting.
  2. Plan the hall: time-critical meds first, such as insulin with meals or specific timed medications.
  3. Use the rights of medication administration at each resident: right resident, drug, dose, route, time and documentation.
  4. Check before giving: vitals or blood sugar when required, and whether the resident can swallow safely.
  5. Watch them take it, then document right away, not at the end of the pass.
  6. Report problems such as refusals, vomiting or anything unusual.

A line that lands: "If I'm interrupted mid-pass, I go back and recheck before continuing, even if it costs a minute."

Reporting to the RN

This is where good LPNs stand out. Show you report early and clearly, in a structure like SBAR.

Example answer (made-up details):

"During my morning pass, a resident who usually chats with me was sleepy and hard to wake. Her blood sugar was normal, but her temperature was up and her urine output overnight was low according to the CNA. I held her sedating medication per the order's parameters, and went straight to the RN rather than finishing the hall first. I told her: 'Mrs. B in 12 is newly lethargic, temp 101.2, low urine output overnight, sugar normal. I'm worried about an infection. Can you assess her and call the provider?' The RN assessed her and called the provider, who ordered labs. It turned out to be a urinary tract infection, and she was back to herself in a few days."

Long-term care routines

Interviewers want to know you can keep 20 or more residents' routines steady while handling surprises. Mention:

  • Knowing residents' baselines so you notice change.
  • Working closely with CNAs: who needs what at breakfast, who's at risk of falls.
  • Keeping families informed within your role, and passing clinical questions to the RN or provider.
  • Finishing documentation during the shift, not after it.

Questions people ask

How do I answer if I'm a new LPN?

Use clinical rotation examples for med passes, reporting and difficult residents. Say what you learned and how you'd ask for help in your first weeks.

What should I say about a resident who refuses medication?

Ask why, explain the medication in simple words, try again later if appropriate, and never force or hide it. Document the refusal and tell the RN or provider.

Do LPN interviews include clinical questions?

Sometimes: normal vital sign ranges, signs of low blood sugar, or what you'd do for a fall. Answer briefly and include who you'd notify.

What should I ask at the end?

Ask about the resident-to-nurse ratio, how many CNAs work your hall, orientation length and how the RN supervisor is reached during the shift.
