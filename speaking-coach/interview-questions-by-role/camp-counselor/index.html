Camp counselor interview questions and how to answer them

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Summer camp counselor interviews ask how you'd comfort a homesick camper, how you'd break up a conflict between kids, how you'd keep campers safe, and what activities you could lead. Answer with the words you'd actually say to a child, a calm step-by-step approach to conflict, safety habits like headcounts, and one activity you could describe and run.

What the camp director is listening for

Camp interviews are often relaxed, sometimes in a group or on a video call, and many applicants are students looking for a summer job. Don't let the friendly tone fool you. The director is asking themselves whether parents could trust you with their child for a week, whether you'll keep the energy up through long days, and whether you'll work well with the other counselors. They want warmth, common sense and safety.

Use the words you'd actually say to a child. Interviewers can picture you in the cabin if you speak the way you'd speak to a nine-year-old.

A homesick camper

This is the most common camp question. A good answer comforts without promising a phone call or a trip home.

Example answer (made-up details):

"I'd sit with them away from the other kids so they don't feel embarrassed and say something like, 'It's normal to miss home, lots of people do on the second night. What do you miss most?' I'd listen, then help them think about tomorrow: 'We've got the lake in the morning, and you're on my canoe team.' I'd keep them busy and close to me the next day, and check in at bedtime. If it lasted more than a couple of days or they stopped eating, I'd tell my unit leader, following the camp's process for contacting parents."

A conflict between kids

Show a calm sequence, and that you treat both kids fairly.

  1. Stop it. Separate them if it's physical. Safety first.
  2. Cool down. Give each a minute before talking.
  3. Listen to each side without interrupting or taking sides.
  4. Help them solve it. "What could we do so you both get a turn?"
  5. Follow up later in the day, and tell your supervisor if anyone was hurt.

"At my after-school job, two boys were fighting over who got the soccer ball. I took the ball, gave them each a minute, and asked each what happened. Then I asked them to come up with a fair way to share. They picked teams and took turns as captain. By the end of the week they were playing on the same team."

For being left out, mention how you'd quietly pair the camper with a kind cabin-mate or give them a job they're good at.

Safety

Directors want habits, not just "I'd be careful." Mention the camp's rules first, then your own habits.

  • Headcounts before leaving, at every stop and on return.
  • Buddy system at the waterfront and on hikes.
  • Knowing where every camper is at all times, and telling another counselor if you have to step away.
  • Following the camp's rules on waterfront, weather, food allergies and medication.
  • Reporting any injury or concern to your supervisor straight away.

If asked what you'd do if a camper tells you something worrying about home, the answer is simple: listen, don't promise to keep it secret, and report it to your supervisor right away, following the camp's procedure.

Activity ideas

Have one activity you can describe in detail: what you need, how long it takes, how you'd explain it to the kids and how you'd adjust it for different ages.

"I could run a 30-minute scavenger hunt in the woods with picture clues for younger campers and riddles for older ones. Teams of four, one counselor per team, and everyone back at the flagpole when I blow the whistle. If it rains, the same hunt works inside the dining hall."

Mention any skills you have: songs, sports, crafts, music, swimming, drama.

Mistakes to avoid

  • "I just love kids." Give an example that shows it.
  • Promising campers things the camp doesn't allow, like phone calls home.
  • Handling serious concerns alone. Always involve your supervisor.
  • Talking only about fun. Directors hire safety and reliability as much as energy.

Questions people ask

What if I've never worked at a camp?

Use babysitting, coaching, tutoring, volunteering or older-sibling experience. Show how you kept kids safe and engaged.

Should I bring anything to a camp counselor interview?

Any certifications, such as first aid, CPR or lifeguarding, and a list of references. Be ready to describe or even lead a short activity or song.

How do I answer "Why our camp?"

Mention something specific about the camp: its activities, its age group or a value on its website, and connect it to your own experience or interests.

How do I answer a question about a camper refusing an activity?

Find out why, gently: fear, tiredness or feeling left out. Offer a smaller role, like keeping score, and invite them to join when they're ready.
