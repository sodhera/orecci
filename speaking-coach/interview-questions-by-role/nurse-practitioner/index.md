# Nurse practitioner interview questions and how to answer them

Nurse practitioner interviews test whether you think like a provider. Expect to talk through how you reach a diagnosis, how you decide what to prescribe (and what not to), how you work with physicians, and how you've made the jump from RN to NP. Answer case questions out loud step by step, so the interviewer can follow your reasoning, not just your conclusion.

## What the interviewer is listening for

As an RN you carried out the plan. As an NP you make it. Interviewers, often a medical director or lead NP, want to hear that shift in your answers. They listen for a clear process, safe limits, and the confidence to own a decision without being reckless.

## Diagnostic reasoning

Expect a short case: "A 45-year-old comes in with chest pain. What do you do?" Don't jump to the answer. Talk through it in order:

1. **Rule out the dangerous causes first.** Say which ones you're worried about and what would send the patient straight to the emergency department.
2. **History.** The questions you'd ask, and why each one matters.
3. **Exam.** What you'd look and listen for.
4. **Differential.** Your top three possibilities, most likely first.
5. **Tests and plan.** What you'd order, what you'd start, and when you'd see them again.
6. **Safety net.** What you'd tell the patient to come back for.

Example opening for a primary care NP:

> "First I'd make sure this isn't a cardiac event. If the pain is crushing, radiating, or they're sweaty or short of breath, they go to the ED, not through my workup. If they're stable, I'd ask when it started, what makes it worse, whether it's tied to exertion or eating, and their risk factors. My differential would include musculoskeletal pain, reflux and cardiac causes, and I'd get an ECG in clinic before deciding which way to go."

Interviewers care less about whether you reach their exact diagnosis than whether you'd keep the patient safe while you work it out. Saying "I'd consult" at the right point is a strength.

## Prescribing decisions

The classic is the patient who wants antibiotics for what looks like a virus. They're testing whether you can say no and keep the relationship.

> "I'd start by acknowledging how miserable they feel. Then I'd explain in plain words why antibiotics won't help a viral infection and what will, and give them clear signs that would mean coming back, like a fever after day five. I'd offer a follow-up call. Most people accept it when they feel heard and have a plan."

For controlled substances, show that you follow your practice's policy and your state's rules, check the prescription monitoring program where your state has one, and document your reasoning. Don't describe rules you aren't sure of; say you'd confirm the requirements for the state you're licensed in.

## Working with physicians

Scope and collaboration rules differ by state and by employer, so interviewers want to know how you'd work within theirs.

> "I want a collaborative relationship whatever the formal arrangement is. I'd consult early on cases outside my experience and bring a proposed plan, not just the problem. If I disagreed with a physician, I'd ask about their reasoning first. If it was about patient safety, I'd say so directly and document it."

## From RN to provider

This question checks self-awareness. Name a real change in how you think.

| As an RN I... | As an NP I... |
| --- | --- |
| Noticed the change and called the provider | Decide what the change means and what to do |
| Followed the plan | Own the plan and its risks |
| Educated patients on the medication | Choose whether they need a medication at all |

> "The biggest change is living with uncertainty. As a nurse I always had someone to call. Now I'm the one being called. What helped in clinical was saying my plan out loud to my preceptor before acting, and I still do a version of that with colleagues on hard cases."

## Mistakes to avoid

- Jumping to a diagnosis without ruling out emergencies.
- Saying you'd prescribe what the patient asks for to keep satisfaction scores up.
- Describing your RN experience only as tasks, not judgment.
- Pretending you'd never need to consult.

This is interview preparation, not clinical guidance. Practice your case walk-through out loud until it flows. A spoken workup sounds very different from a written one. ## Questions people ask

### What do new grad NP interviews focus on?

Your clinical rotations, how you'd handle the step up to provider, and what support you'd need. Be honest about wanting mentorship; many practices see that as a sign of good judgment.

### How do I answer "How many patients can you see a day?"

Give a realistic number from your clinicals and say how you'd build up. Ask what their expectation is and how quickly new NPs ramp up.

### Will they ask clinical knowledge questions?

Often, especially in specialty roles. Expect common conditions in that specialty and how you'd manage them. Show your reasoning and say when you'd look something up.

### How do I talk about a missed diagnosis?

Tell it plainly: what you missed, how it was caught, what happened to the patient, and the specific habit you changed afterwards.
