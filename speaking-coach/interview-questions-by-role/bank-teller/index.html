Bank teller interview questions and how to answer them

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Bank teller interviews test three things: that you are careful with cash, that you will follow procedure when something looks wrong, and that you can talk to customers about fees and products without sounding pushy. Give answers that show the steps you would take, who you would tell, and how you would keep the customer on side.

What the branch manager is listening for

A teller handles other people's money all day, so the interview leans on trust. The questions sound simple, but each one is checking whether you would cut a corner. Cashier, retail or restaurant experience counts for a lot here: if you have counted a till, dealt with a short drawer or refused a sale, use that story.

"Your drawer is off at the end of the day"

This is the most common scenario question, usually with a number: you're $20 short. The wrong answers are covering it from your own pocket or quietly hoping it evens out tomorrow. Both hide the problem, and banks need to see every difference.

Example answer:

"First I'd recount the drawer, slowly, by denomination, because most differences are a miscount. Then I'd go back through the day's transactions for a $20 that went out twice or a deposit keyed wrong. If I still couldn't find it, I'd tell my supervisor straight away and follow the branch's process for a difference, even if it's small. I'd never put my own money in to make it balance. If it's off, the bank needs to know why."

Notice the order: recount, retrace, report. Interviewers want to hear all three.

Spotting fraud and scams

Expect a question about a suspicious check, a mismatched ID, or a customer who might be the target of a scam. The scam version is often an older customer withdrawing a large amount in cash, looking nervous, sometimes with someone waiting or on the phone.

Example answer:

"I'd slow down without making them feel accused. I'd make friendly conversation: 'That's a big withdrawal, anything exciting planned?' If they mention someone asking them to pay in cash or gift cards, or they say they've been told not to tell the bank, those are red flags. I'd follow our procedure, which usually means bringing in my supervisor before I complete it. It's their money, but my job is to make sure they're making the choice and not someone else."

For a suspicious check, keep it procedural: compare the ID to the person in front of you, check the details on the check, and get a supervisor before cashing anything you are unsure about. Don't say you would confront anyone.

Referring products without being pushy

Many teller roles include referral goals, and interviewers ask how you feel about that. Saying you hate sales is a red flag; saying you love selling can sound wrong too. The honest middle is that you listen for a need and point people to help.

Example answer:

"I see it as noticing things. If someone's paying an overdraft fee every month, or depositing large checks into an account that earns nothing, I'd mention it in one sentence and offer to introduce them to a banker. If they say no, that's fine. I'd rather make a referral that actually helps than five that annoy people."

An upset customer about fees

Example answer:

"I'd let them finish first, because people usually want to be heard before anything else. Then I'd look at the account with them and explain exactly what the fee was for, in plain words. If I have the authority to reverse it, or my supervisor does, I'd ask. If not, I'd say so honestly and show them how to avoid it next time, like a low-balance alert. What I wouldn't do is blame the bank or promise a refund I can't give."

How to prepare

  1. Write out your cash-handling routine from your last job in four or five steps. You'll use it in several answers.
  2. Prepare one story each for: a mistake you caught, an unhappy customer, and a time you followed a rule someone didn't like.
  3. Practice the drawer, fraud and fee scenarios out loud until the steps come in order. The interview answer review shows your pace and filler words when you say them.
  4. Have one reason for this bank: its community role, its hours or a branch you've used.

Questions people ask

Do I need banking experience to be a teller?

Usually not. Cash handling and customer service from retail, hospitality or a cashier job are what most branches look for. Show you're careful and good with people.

Will there be a math test?

Some banks give a short counting or arithmetic test, others don't. Practice counting change and adding figures quickly, and say in the interview that you double-check.

How do I answer "Why do you want to work at a bank?"

Talk about the parts of the job you'd do well: accuracy, routine, helping people with something important to them. Add one reason for this specific bank.

What if I don't like selling?

Say you're comfortable spotting needs and making referrals when they help the customer. That is what most teller referral goals ask for.

How should I talk about a past cash mistake?

Pick a small one, say how you noticed it, how you reported or fixed it, and what you do differently now. Owning it is the point.
