# Loan officer interview questions and how to answer them

Loan officer interviews ask how you assess a borrower, how you tell an applicant they've been turned down, how you build a referral pipeline and how you stay compliant. Answer with the factors you weigh, the exact words you'd use to deliver a no while keeping the door open, and the referral sources and numbers you'd actually work.

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence. Updated 2026-10-22. Source: https://www.orecci.com/speaking-coach/interview-questions-by-role/loan-officer/

## What the hiring manager is listening for

A branch manager or sales manager hiring a loan officer wants someone who brings in business, judges risk sensibly, and never cuts a corner on compliance to close a deal. They also want to hear that you can talk to nervous borrowers in plain language, because many of them are making the biggest financial decision of their lives.

## Assessing a borrower

Show a framework, then show judgement. Many lenders talk about the "five Cs": character, capacity, capital, collateral and conditions. Use whatever your lender's framework is, and give an example of weighing them against each other.

| Factor | What you look at | What to say in the interview |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Capacity | Income, debt-to-income, stability of earnings | "I look at whether income is steady, not just high." |
| Credit history | Payment record, recent new debt | "A late payment two years ago worries me less than three new cards last month." |
| Capital | Savings, down payment, reserves | "Reserves tell me if one bad month becomes a default." |
| Collateral | Value and condition of the security | "I want the appraisal before I promise anything." |
| Conditions | Purpose of the loan, the wider market | "A business loan for a second location gets more questions than one for equipment." |

Example answer (made-up details):

> "A self-employed applicant had a strong credit score but income that dropped 30% the year before. Rather than decline on the number, I asked for 18 months of bank statements, which showed the dip was one lost contract that had since been replaced. Underwriting approved it with a slightly larger down payment."

## Turning down an applicant

This tests honesty and empathy at once. Interviewers want to hear you give the real reasons, clearly, and leave the person with a next step.

There are rules on this too. In the US, Regulation B requires lenders to notify applicants of a decision within 30 days of a completed application, and an adverse action notice must give specific reasons or tell the applicant how to ask for them. Your lender's process will set the details. In the interview, focus on the conversation:

> "I'd call them rather than let the letter arrive cold. I'd say: 'I'm sorry, we can't approve the loan as it stands. The main reason is that your monthly debt payments are too high compared with your income. You'll get a letter with the details. If you pay down the car loan, I'd be glad to look at it again, and I can walk you through what that would take.'"

Don't hint at approval while you're still waiting on underwriting. That's how angry customers are made.

## Building referrals

For mortgage and commercial roles especially, this is where your pay comes from. Give a plan with names of sources and a weekly rhythm.

1. **Real estate agents:** meet two or three a week, ask what slows their deals down, and answer their calls fast.
2. **Past clients:** check in at the one-year mark; ask for introductions.
3. **Builders, accountants, financial planners:** people whose clients need financing.
4. **Community:** a homebuyer seminar at a library or a workplace.

> "In my last role I tracked every referral source in a spreadsheet. After six months, I saw that two agents sent most of my closed loans, so I spent more time with them and stopped going to events that produced nothing."

## Compliance

Expect a scenario: a borrower asks you to leave out a debt, or a manager pushes you to close by month-end. Answer without hesitation.

> "I'd tell the borrower I can't leave it off, because it would make the application inaccurate, and that it could put them in a loan they can't afford. Then I'd look for honest options, like paying the debt down first."

Mention any licensing you hold or are working toward, and how you keep up with rule changes through your compliance team and training.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Sounding like you approve everyone** to hit targets.
- **Hiding behind "the system declined it"** instead of explaining reasons in plain words.
- **A referral plan with no numbers or names.** Say who, how often, and how you'd measure it.
- **Hesitating on an ethics scenario.** The right answer here should come quickly.

Telling someone no in a way they'd still recommend you takes practice out loud. Speaking Coach can play the disappointed applicant.

## Questions people ask

### Do I need a license to be a loan officer?

It depends on the country, the type of lending and the employer, and mortgage roles often have their own licensing rules. Check the job post and your regulator's requirements, and say in the interview exactly where you are in the process.

### How do I answer if I don't have lending experience?

Use sales, banking or customer service experience: a time you explained something financial, built a client base, or said no to a customer while keeping them happy.

### How are loan officers paid, and should I ask?

Many roles mix salary with commission or are mostly commission. It's fine to ask how compensation works, usually after they've shown interest in you.

### What questions should I ask the interviewer?

Ask where most of their loan officers' business comes from, how long a typical file takes to close, and what support you'd get from processing and underwriting.
