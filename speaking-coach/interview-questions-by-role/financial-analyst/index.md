# Financial analyst interview questions and how to answer them

FP&A financial analyst interviews ask you to explain how you build a forecast, why actuals differed from budget, how you present numbers to leaders who aren't in finance, and how a model you built works. Lead with the answer in one sentence, then the drivers behind it, and say your assumptions out loud so the interviewer can test them.

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence. Updated 2026-10-22. Source: https://www.orecci.com/speaking-coach/interview-questions-by-role/financial-analyst/

## What FP&A interviews test

A financial planning and analysis interview usually mixes technical questions, a case or modeling task, and conversations with the finance manager and a business partner. The technical part checks you can do the work. The spoken part checks whether a sales director or COO would understand you and trust you. Many candidates prepare the first and lose on the second.

## Building a forecast

*"Walk me through a forecast you built."* Don't start with the spreadsheet tabs. Start with what the forecast was for, then the drivers.

Example answer (figures are illustrative):

> "I owned the quarterly revenue forecast for our subscription business. I built it from drivers rather than last year plus a percentage: new customers from the sales pipeline at historical win rates, churn by customer size, and price increases by contract renewal date. Each month I compared actuals to each driver, not just the total, so we could see whether a miss came from new sales or from churn. Over the year we got the quarterly forecast within a few percent, and when it missed, we knew which driver to fix."

For a new product with no history, say what you'd borrow: a comparable product's ramp, market sizing, and sales capacity, then a range rather than one number.

## Explaining a variance

This is the most important spoken skill in FP&A. *"Revenue came in 8% under budget. Explain it."*

A structure that works in about 90 seconds:

1. **The headline.** "We missed by 8%, about $1.2 million, and most of it is timing, not lost demand."
2. **The bridge.** Break the gap into a few drivers: volume, price, mix, timing.
3. **One-off or ongoing.** Which parts will come back next quarter?
4. **What happens next.** The action or the forecast change.

Example answer:

> "We were $1.2 million under budget. About $700,000 is two enterprise deals that slipped into next month; both are signed now. About $300,000 is lower volume in the small-business segment, which has been soft for three months, so I'd treat that as ongoing and lower the forecast. The last $200,000 is price mix: more customers chose the cheaper plan. My recommendation is to keep the full-year number but cut small-business by 5%, and I'd like sales to look at why the cheaper plan is winning."

Leaders want the conclusion first. The [BLUF](/speaking-coach/glossary/bluf/) habit, bottom line up front, is worth practicing until it's automatic.

## Presenting to leadership

Example answer:

> "I start with the one thing they need to decide or know, then two or three numbers that support it, and I keep the detailed tables in the appendix. I use their words, like 'bookings' if that's what sales calls it. And I say what I'm unsure about, because if they find a weak assumption before I mention it, they stop trusting the rest."

See [how to present to executives](/speaking-coach/presentations/board-presentation/) for structure.

## The model walk-through

You may be asked to walk through a model, sometimes one you built at home for the interview. Use this order and keep each part short:

| Step | What to say |
| --- | --- |
| Purpose | "This model answers whether we should open a second warehouse." |
| Inputs | The 4–6 assumptions that matter most, and where they came from |
| Logic | How inputs flow to outputs, in plain words |
| Output | The answer and the range |
| Sensitivity | Which assumption moves the answer most |
| Checks | How you tested it: balance checks, tie-outs, a second pair of eyes |

The interviewer will pick one assumption and ask, "What if that's wrong?" Have the sensitivity ready.

## Mistakes that cost offers

- Reading numbers in order instead of saying what they mean.
- Explaining a variance only as "the business underperformed."
- Not knowing which of your assumptions the answer depends on.
- Saying a forecast was never wrong. Every forecast is; talk about how you learned from it.

## How to prepare

1. Pick one forecast, one variance and one model from your work. Remove anything confidential.
2. Practice the variance answer out loud with a timer: headline in the first ten seconds.
3. Explain your model to a friend outside finance. Where they get lost, simplify.
4. Rehearse with an interviewer who interrupts. Speaking Coach can play a COO who asks "So what do I do about it?" and follows up on your actual answer.

## Questions people ask

### How technical will an FP&A interview be?

Expect questions on the three statements, forecasting methods and spreadsheet skills, and often a case. Senior roles lean more on business partnering and presenting.

### What if I get a variance number wrong in the interview?

Correct it calmly as soon as you notice. Showing that you check your work is a point in your favor.

### How do I answer "Tell me about a time your forecast was wrong"?

Say what you missed, how much it cost in accuracy, which driver caused it, and what you changed in the model or process afterwards.

### Should I bring a model to the interview?

Only if asked. If you do, make sure every assumption is yours and you can explain each one without looking.

### How long should my answers be?

Most answers should be 60 to 90 seconds, with the conclusion in the first sentence. Longer case answers are fine if you signpost the steps.
