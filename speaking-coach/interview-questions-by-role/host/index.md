# Host and hostess interview questions and how to answer them

Restaurant host interviews check how you sound in the first ten seconds with a guest and how you stay organized when the door is busy. Expect questions on quoting wait times, calming an angry walk-in, rotating sections fairly between servers, and greeting people. Answer with the exact words you would say at the stand.

## Why a host interview is really a voice test

The host is the first and last person a guest talks to. Managers know they can teach the seating chart in a shift. What they can't teach easily is a warm, steady voice when the door is three deep and someone is annoyed. So expect role-play: "I'm a guest, I've just walked in, go."

For a first job, that is good news. You don't need experience to do well; you need a few good lines and the calm to say them.

## First impressions: the greeting

When they say "Greet me as if I just walked in", do three things in about five seconds: look up, smile, and speak first.

> Example line: "Hi, welcome in! Do you have a reservation with us tonight?"
> If they don't: "No problem. Just the two of you? Let me check what we have."

Avoid starting with "Name?" or "How many?" It sounds like a ticket counter. If you're on the phone when someone walks in, make eye contact and raise a finger or say "I'll be right with you," so they know they've been seen.

## Managing a waitlist

"How do you quote a wait time?" Interviewers want to hear that you base it on the floor, not a guess, and that you quote a little long rather than short.

1. **Look at the floor:** which tables have paid, which have desserts, which just sat.
2. **Quote a range, slightly generous:** "About 25 to 30 minutes."
3. **Offer something:** the bar, a text when the table's ready, a phone number.
4. **Update before they ask.** If it's running long, go to them first.

> Example answer: "I check which tables are on dessert or have the check, then quote a bit long. If I think it's 20 minutes, I say 25 to 30. Guests are happy when they're seated early and upset when they're seated late, even if the total is the same."

## The angry walk-in

"A party of five is furious about a 45-minute wait." Don't argue about the wait, and don't promise a table you can't give. Acknowledge, explain once, give choices.

> Example answer: "I'd say: 'I know, 45 minutes is a long wait on a Friday, and I'm sorry. I can put you at the bar now and move you when a table opens, or text you so you can take a walk. Which would you like?' If they're still angry, I'd get the manager, not because I can't handle it but because they can offer things I can't."

Giving two options moves the guest from complaining to choosing. More ways to handle this are on [how to handle an angry customer](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/how-would-you-handle-an-angry-customer/).

## Seating rotation

Servers' income depends on your seating, so this question checks fairness and awareness.

| Situation | What a good host does |
| --- | --- |
| Normal rotation | Seat sections in turn, so no server gets two tables at once |
| One server just got a party of eight | Skip them for the next one or two tables |
| A server is in the weeds | Hold their next table for a few minutes and tell the guest it's being set |
| Regulars ask for a server | Honor it if you can, and adjust the rotation to stay fair |
| A server complains they got too many | Show them the chart calmly; fix it if they're right |

> Example answer: "I keep a rotation on the seating chart and track covers, not just tables, because a four-top is more work than a two-top. If a server just got a big party, I skip them once. And I watch the floor, not only the chart; if someone looks buried, I give them a minute."

## Reservations that aren't ready

Apologize, give a time, and offer somewhere to wait: "Your table's just finishing dessert. It'll be about ten minutes. Can I get you started at the bar?" Then check back at ten minutes, not twenty.

## If this is your first job

Talk about school, sports, volunteering or babysitting: anything where you dealt with people, kept things organized or stayed calm. Show your availability clearly, including weekends. The [server interview questions](/speaking-coach/interview-questions-by-role/server/) page is worth reading too, because many hosts move into serving.

## How to practice

Practice the greeting and the angry walk-in out loud until they sound natural, not memorized. Ask a friend to play the guest and refuse your first offer. Speaking Coach's built-in "Handle a rude customer" scene lets you practice with an AI guest who reacts to what you actually say.

## Questions people ask

### What should I wear to a host interview?

Something close to the restaurant's front-of-house look: neat, clean and simple. If you're unsure, a plain dark top and trousers works almost anywhere.

### Do I need experience to be a host?

Usually not. Hosting is a common first restaurant job. A friendly greeting, reliability and clear availability matter more.

### How do I answer "Why do you want to be a host?"

Be honest and positive: you like meeting people, you want a fast-paced job, or you want to move into serving later. Mention something you like about their restaurant.

### What's the hardest part of hosting?

Usually a full waitlist on a busy night. Saying so, and explaining how you'd stay organized, shows you understand the job.
