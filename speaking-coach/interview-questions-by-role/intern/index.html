Internship interview questions and how to answer them

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Internship interviews ask about you, a class or personal project, why this company, what you want to learn and how you work with others. Nobody expects job experience, so answer with projects, coursework, part-time jobs and clubs, and show one specific reason you picked this company plus a clear idea of what you want to learn there.

What the interviewer is actually judging

An internship interviewer knows you have not done the job before. They are judging three things you can show without experience:

  • Can you learn? Do you pick things up, ask good questions and fix your own mistakes?
  • Can you finish something? A project with a result tells them more than a list of courses.
  • Will you be easy to work with for ten weeks? Short, honest, specific answers signal this better than enthusiasm alone.

So the trick is not to apologise for having little experience. Treat your coursework, a part-time job, a club role or a side project as your experience, and talk about it the way a professional would talk about work.

Answering with class projects

"Walk me through a project" is the question most interns either nail or ramble through. Use four beats and keep it to about 90 seconds:

  1. The goal. One sentence: what the project was for.
  2. Your part. What you did, using "I", not "we". Interviewers will ask, so say it first.
  3. One hard moment. A problem you hit and how you got past it.
  4. The result. A grade, a demo, a number, or what someone did with your work.

Example answer for a business student:

"For our marketing class, four of us had to build a launch plan for a local bakery's new delivery service. I ran the customer survey: I wrote twelve questions, got 85 responses through campus groups, and turned them into three charts for our deck. Halfway through we realised most responses were from students, not the bakery's real customers, so I asked the owner to hand out a QR code at the till for a week. That gave us 40 more responses from actual regulars, and the owner used our pricing slide when he launched."

If the project was a group effort and you were not the leader, that is fine. Name your piece clearly. More on this in how to talk about your final year project.

Why this company?

"I want to gain experience" is true for every applicant at every company. Give one reason that only fits them, and one reason that fits you.

WeakStronger
"You're a big, well-known company.""Your team builds the app my whole dorm uses for laundry, and I want to see how a product that small gets made."
"I'm passionate about finance.""Your internship rotates through audit and advisory, and I don't know yet which I prefer. I'd like to find out with real clients."
"I saw the posting on LinkedIn.""I met two of your interns at the career fair and they said interns ship real work in week two. That's what I want."

Spend 20 minutes on their website, a recent news item and the job post before you answer this. The page on why you want this internship has more examples.

What you want to learn

This question checks whether you have thought about the internship as more than a line on your CV. Name one hard skill and one soft skill, both connected to the role.

Example answer for a software internship:

"Two things. In class I've only written code that I alone read, so I want to learn how code review works on a real team, and how to take feedback on it. And I've never deployed anything people actually use. If I could get one small feature into production by the end of the summer, I'd count that as a win."

When you have almost no experience

Some questions ask for stories you think you don't have, like conflict, a deadline or a mistake. Look wider:

  • Part-time jobs. Retail, food service and tutoring all have deadlines, difficult customers and teamwork.
  • Clubs and sports. Organising an event, managing a budget, or keeping a team going after losses.
  • School itself. Juggling three deadlines in one week is a real time-management story.
  • Family responsibilities. Caring for a sibling or helping run a family business counts.

Then say what you learned in one sentence. "I learned to ask for help on day one instead of day four" is a better close than a long reflection.

How to prepare

  1. Pick two projects and write the four beats for each.
  2. Write one "why this company" reason that would not work for any other employer.
  3. Prepare two questions to ask them, such as "What did last year's interns work on?"
  4. Say every answer out loud and time it. Aim for 60–90 seconds; the speech length calculator shows how many words that is.
  5. Practice once with follow-ups. Speaking Coach can play the interviewer, read your CV and ask about your real projects, so you get used to "and what did you do?"

Questions people ask

What if I have no work experience at all?

Use projects, clubs, volunteering and coursework. Interviewers ask about experience to hear how you think and work, and school gives you plenty of examples of both.

How long should my answers be?

Most answers should be 60 to 90 seconds. Project walkthroughs can run to about two minutes if the interviewer asked for detail.

Is it okay to say I'm not sure what career I want?

Yes, if you pair it with what you want to find out. "I'm deciding between data and product, and this internship touches both" sounds curious, not lost.

Should I mention my GPA?

Only if it helps or they ask. If it is low, point to a subject you did well in or a project that shows your ability.

What should I wear to an internship interview?

Match what people at the company wear, one step smarter. If you can't tell, business casual is a safe default.
