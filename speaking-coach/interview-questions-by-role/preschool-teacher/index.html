Preschool teacher interview questions and how to answer them

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Preschool and early years interviews want to hear what learning looks like in your room, how you stay calm with a child in full meltdown, how you talk to parents (including about hard things) and how you keep every child safe. Answer with one real moment from a classroom, describe what the children did, and name the learning or safety habit behind it.

What early years interviewers listen for

Preschool directors hear "I love kids" from every candidate. What they want is someone who can explain why a messy sand tray is learning, who doesn't raise their voice when a child loses control, and who notices the child wandering toward the gate. Many interviews also include a short observation or a session with the children, so your answers should match how you actually behave in the room.

The more specific you are about ages and stages, the better. "Two-year-olds" and "four-year-olds" are different jobs, and saying so shows you know that.

Play-based learning

This is the question that separates early years teachers from general applicants. Don't just say "children learn through play." Pick one activity and name the learning in it.

ActivityWhat children are learningWhat you might say or do
Block buildingBalance, counting, shapes, taking turns"Your tower has six blocks. How many will it hold before it falls?"
Pretend caféVocabulary, numbers, social rolesWrite a menu with them, take an order, ask for change
Water and sandVolume, cause and effect, fine motor skills"Which cup fills the bucket fastest?"
Outdoor climbingGross motor skills, judging riskStay close, let them try, name what they did

Example answer (made-up details):

"In my four-year-old room, the block corner is where a lot of maths happens. One morning two children were arguing about whose tower was taller. I asked how they could find out. They tried standing next to it, then lined up the blocks and counted. I took a photo, wrote down what they said and put it in both children's learning journals as an early measuring observation. That's how I show parents learning in play."

Mention how you record learning: observations, photos, learning journals or whatever system the setting uses. Directors want to know you can show progress, not just provide fun.

A toddler meltdown

Here they are testing your calm and your understanding of development. A two- or three-year-old in meltdown can't reason. Show that you help them settle first and talk later.

  1. Keep everyone safe: move other children or objects away.
  2. Get low, stay close, keep your voice quiet and slow.
  3. Use very few words: "You're really cross. I'm here."
  4. Wait. Don't bargain or lecture in the middle of it.
  5. When they're calm, name what happened and give them a way back in.

"A two-year-old in my room screamed and threw cars when it was time to tidy. I moved the other children to the carpet with my co-teacher, knelt near him and said, 'You wanted to keep playing. You're angry.' I stayed close and quiet. After a couple of minutes he leaned into me. Then I said, 'Let's put two cars away together,' and he did. The next day I gave him a five-minute warning before tidy-up and it went much better."

Talking with parents

Show two sides: the everyday updates parents love, and the harder conversations.

For everyday contact, mention short pickup chats, a daily note or app update, and sharing one specific thing their child did. For a harder conversation, such as a concern about speech or development, show care and facts, not a diagnosis.

"I'd ask the parent for a quiet time, not a rushed chat at pickup. I'd start with what their child enjoys, then share what I've noticed with examples, like 'He uses about ten words, and most of his friends are using short sentences.' I'd ask what they see at home, and suggest they talk to their doctor or health visitor. I'm not there to diagnose, just to share what I see and keep working with them."

Safety

Be concrete. Talk about the habits you do without thinking: headcounts at every transition, checking the outdoor area before children go out, allergy lists posted where food is served, never leaving children unsupervised and following ratio and supervision rules. Say how you'd report an incident or an injury in line with your setting's policy.

Questions people ask

What's different about a preschool interview compared with a school teacher interview?

Preschool interviews focus more on play, routines, care and parents, and less on formal assessment and test results. See the teacher interview questions for school roles.

What if I'm asked to do an activity with the children?

Keep it short and simple, like a story with props or a song with actions. Get down to their level, learn names and let them talk.

How do I answer "Why early years?"

Tell one real moment that made you choose it. See why you chose this career for structure.

What should I ask at the end?

Ask about ratios in each room, how they plan, how they share learning with parents and what training they offer staff.
