UX designer interview questions and how to answer them

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

UX designer interviews test how you think out loud. Expect to present one or two case studies, describe research that changed your design, explain how you work with engineers when something can't be built, and solve a whiteboard or design challenge live. Answer with the decisions you made, the evidence behind them and what you would do differently.

Where UX candidates lose points

Most UX loops include a portfolio or case study presentation, one or more conversations with designers, product managers and engineers, and often a live design challenge. Across all of them, interviewers are scoring the same thing: your process and judgment, explained clearly.

The most common way candidates lose points is showing final screens and process artifacts (personas, journey maps, sticky notes) without saying what they decided because of them. A persona nobody used is decoration.

Presenting a case study

Keep the first pass to 10 to 15 minutes per case study, leaving time for questions. A clear order:

  1. Context: the product, the users and your role. "I was the only designer on a team of six."
  2. The problem, with evidence: "Forty percent of users dropped at the delivery step" (use your real data, or describe what you saw if you had none).
  3. What you learned: the research and the key insight.
  4. Options and trade-offs: two or three directions and why you chose one.
  5. Outcome: what changed after launch, and what you'd do next.

Example opening: "This is a grocery delivery app where most first orders were abandoned at checkout. I was the lead designer, working with a PM and four engineers. I'll show how five usability sessions changed what we thought the problem was, and the trade-off we made to ship in one sprint."

Say "I" for your decisions and "we" for the team's. Interviewers notice when they can't tell which is which. The presentation structure guide helps if your case study sprawls.

Research that changed a design

This is the question that shows whether you design from evidence. A strong story has a belief, a finding that challenged it, and a change you made.

Example answer: "We assumed people abandoned checkout because of the delivery fee, and the PM wanted to test free delivery. In five moderated sessions, four people stopped at the delivery slot picker instead. They couldn't tell which slots were available and gave up. We redesigned the picker to show only open slots, without touching the fee. Checkout completion went up noticeably in the following month, and we saved the cost of a free delivery test."

If your research didn't change anything, say what it confirmed and how that made the team more confident. That counts too.

Working with engineers

"Tell me about a time an engineer said your design couldn't be built." They want collaboration, not a battle you won.

Example answer: "I'd designed an animated progress bar for onboarding. The engineer told me it would take a week because of how our screens were built. I asked what would be cheap, and he suggested a simple step counter. We tested both as prototypes with three users, they understood both equally well, so we shipped the counter. Now I check with engineering at the sketch stage, not after high-fidelity."

Mention habits that make handoff smoother: involving engineers early, annotating edge cases and error states, and being around during the build.

The whiteboard challenge

The prompt is often deliberately vague, like "Design a parking garage app." They are watching how you frame it, not how good the drawing is. Talk through these out loud:

StepWhat to sayRoughly
Clarify"Who's this for: drivers, the garage owner? What's the goal?"5 minutes
Pick a user and a problem"Let's focus on a commuter who's late and can't find a space."3 minutes
Sketch the main flowThree or four key screens, not every screen15 minutes
Edge casesNo spaces, payment fails, no signal underground5 minutes
Measure"I'd know it worked if time-to-park dropped."2 minutes

Narrate your thinking even when you're unsure: "I'm torn between a map and a list here. A map is faster for people who know the garage." Silence is the biggest risk in this round. The technical interview guide has more on thinking aloud.

Accessibility and measurement

For accessibility, give concrete practices: color contrast, text that scales, labels for screen readers, focus order, not relying on color alone, and testing with real assistive technology users when you can. For measurement, name a metric tied to the problem, like task completion or support tickets, not just "user satisfaction".

How to practice

Present your case study out loud, timed, at least twice. Then have someone interrupt with "Why that?" and "What did the data say?" In Speaking Coach you can add your case study slides as a PDF, swipe through them as you speak, and answer the questions your audience would ask.

Questions people ask

How many case studies should I prepare?

Two strong ones you can present in depth, plus one shorter backup. Pick projects with real trade-offs, not just the prettiest screens.

What if my project had no measurable outcome?

Say so honestly, then say what you would have measured and what qualitative signals you saw.

Should I use a slide deck or my portfolio site?

A short deck usually works better for presenting, because you control the order and pace. Keep the site ready for questions.

How is UX different from graphic design in interviews?

UX interviews focus more on research, flows and evidence; graphic design interviews focus more on visual choices. The graphic designer questions page covers the visual side.
