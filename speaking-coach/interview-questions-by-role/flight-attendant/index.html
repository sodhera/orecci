Flight attendant interview questions and how to answer them

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Flight attendant interviews ask why you want to fly, how you balance safety with service, how you would handle an unruly passenger and how you get on with crew you barely know. Show warmth and firmness in the same answer: be kind to the passenger, but never let a safety rule bend, and bring in the senior crew member when it escalates.

What airline interview days look like

Many airlines hire cabin crew through an assessment day rather than a single interview. A typical day mixes a short presentation or self-introduction, a group exercise, and then a one-to-one or panel interview for the people who move on. Some airlines start with a recorded video interview first.

That means you are being watched long before your formal questions. How you talk to other candidates in the waiting room and during the group task is part of the assessment. If your airline uses an assessment day, read what happens at an assessment centre.

Safety comes before service

This is the core of the job, and interviewers test it with a simple scenario: a passenger refuses to put their bag in the overhead bin or fasten their seatbelt for takeoff. Weak answers are either too soft ("I'd let it go so they're happy") or too harsh ("I'd tell them it's the law and walk off").

The answer they want is polite, firm and escalating.

Example answer:

"I'd go to them, crouch to their eye level and ask quietly so it doesn't become a scene. I'd explain why: the bag could block the aisle in an evacuation. If they still refuse, I'd offer to help find space or check it, so they have a way to say yes. If they still won't, I'd tell the senior crew member, because we can't leave the gate until it's done. I'll be as friendly as possible about it, but it's not optional."

The structure is worth remembering: ask kindly, explain why, offer a choice, escalate.

The unruly passenger

Expect "How would you deal with a drunk or abusive passenger?" Show you would protect other passengers and the crew, not try to win an argument. Use real experience from hospitality or retail if you have it.

Example answer for a candidate from bar work:

"I worked three years in a busy pub, so I've stopped serving people before. On board I'd stop serving alcohol first and do it quietly, offering water and food instead. I'd keep my voice low and my words short, and I wouldn't argue about how much they've had. I'd tell the senior crew member straight away, because they decide what happens next and may inform the captain. If the passenger becomes a threat, I'd follow the airline's procedure for that exactly."

Conflict on a long trip

Crews change constantly, so you may spend four days with people you met at briefing. Interviewers ask "Tell me about a disagreement with a coworker" to see whether you deal with it directly and privately.

Example answer:

"At the hotel I worked at, a colleague kept leaving her side of the breakfast setup to me. On the third morning I asked if we could talk after service. I said what I'd noticed, without guessing why, and she told me she'd been covering reception at the same time. We split the tasks differently and it was fine for the rest of the season. On a trip I'd do the same: talk early, in private, and keep it out of the cabin."

The group exercise

Group tasks are usually about planning something together under a time limit. Assessors look for how you work with people, not whether your idea wins.

Why do you want to be a flight attendant?

"I love to travel" is the answer every assessor hears, and it says nothing about the work. Talk about the parts most people don't mention: looking after people in a small space, staying calm when something goes wrong, and working as a tight team. Then add one real reason for their airline, such as its routes, its training or how its crew treated you on a flight.

How to prepare

  1. Write four stories: great service, a difficult customer, a disagreement with a colleague, a time you followed a rule that annoyed someone.
  2. Practice the safety scenario until "ask, explain, offer a choice, escalate" comes out naturally.
  3. Prepare a 60-second self-introduction for the group stage.
  4. Say your answers out loud, with a smile in your voice. Speaking Coach can play a passenger or an interviewer and follow up on what you said, so you practice staying warm while holding a line.

Questions people ask

Do I need customer service experience?

Most airlines prefer it, because the job is customer service in a tight space. Hospitality, retail, care work and nursing all count. Use those stories in your answers.

What if I've never handled an emergency?

Use any time you stayed calm when something went wrong, such as a first aid moment, a lost child in a shop or a kitchen fire. Then say you would follow your training and the senior crew member's lead.

How do I answer questions about irregular hours?

Be realistic. Say what you would put in place, such as sleep routines and support at home, and that you understand early starts, nights and holidays are part of the job.

Should I memorize answers for the panel?

Memorize the structure and your stories, not the sentences. Assessors follow up, and memorized answers fall apart when they do.

How do I stand out in the group stage?

Be the person who makes the group work better: listening, including others and keeping to time. Being the loudest person rarely helps.
