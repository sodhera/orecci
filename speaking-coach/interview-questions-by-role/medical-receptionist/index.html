Medical receptionist interview questions and how to answer them

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Medical receptionist interviews test whether you can run a busy front desk safely: check patients in quickly and accurately, answer insurance questions without guessing, recognize when a phone call needs a nurse right away, and protect patient privacy at a crowded counter. Answer with a real desk situation, the exact words you used, and who you handed it to.

What the practice manager is listening for

A medical receptionist is the first and last person every patient talks to. Practice managers want someone accurate with details, calm with upset people and clear about what is not theirs to decide: clinical questions go to clinical staff, coverage questions go to the insurer or billing. Showing that you know where your role ends is one of the strongest things you can do in this interview.

If you've worked a front desk in a hotel, a gym or retail, those stories count. Connect them to the medical setting.

Patient check-in

Walk through it in order and show you care about getting details right.

  1. Greet and confirm identity with name and date of birth.
  2. Check that demographics and insurance are current, and scan new cards.
  3. Collect forms and signatures the visit needs.
  4. Collect the copay or explain the balance, following office policy.
  5. Let clinical staff know the patient is ready, and tell the patient about any wait.

A line that lands: "Most billing problems start at check-in, so I ask about insurance changes every visit, not just the first."

Insurance questions

Patients often ask "Will my insurance cover this?" You shouldn't guess. Show what you do instead.

Example answer (made-up details):

"A patient asked if her plan would cover an MRI the doctor ordered. I told her honestly that I couldn't promise coverage, because it depends on her plan and sometimes on prior authorization. I checked what I could see in our system, told her our referral coordinator would confirm whether authorization was needed, and gave her the number on the back of her card so she could ask her insurer directly. She called back two days later to say it was approved, and thanked us for not promising something we couldn't."

Triage calls

This is the question that separates good candidates. Receptionists don't assess symptoms, but they must recognize when a call needs a clinician now. Show that you follow the office's protocol and never sit on it.

Caller saysWhat you do
Chest pain, trouble breathing, signs of strokeFollow the office protocol for emergencies, which usually means telling them to call emergency services, then alerting a clinician
A new or worsening symptom they're worried aboutTransfer to or message the nurse right away, not book the next free slot
A medication questionTake a message for the nurse or provider; don't advise
A routine follow-upBook it

Example line: "I'm not able to judge symptoms, so I'm going to get the nurse on the line right now."

Privacy at the desk

Expect a question about HIPAA, the US federal law whose Privacy Rule governs how protected health information is used and shared. You don't need to quote the law. Show the habits that protect privacy at a crowded counter:

  • Asking patients to confirm details quietly or in writing rather than out loud.
  • Keeping screens angled away from the waiting room and locking them when you step away.
  • Not leaving charts, labels or messages where others can read them.
  • Verifying who someone is before giving information over the phone, and checking whether the patient allowed it.
  • Taking a sensitive conversation to a private spot.

Example answer:

"A man called asking for his adult daughter's test results. I explained kindly that I could only share them with people she'd authorized, checked her file, and he wasn't listed. I offered to pass on a message asking her to call us. He was frustrated, but I kept it calm and friendly."

Questions people ask

Do I need medical terminology to be a medical receptionist?

It helps, and some employers ask for it. If you're new to healthcare, mention any course you've taken and show that you know what not to answer.

How do I answer "How do you handle a long wait?"

Tell patients early and honestly how long the wait is, give updates, and offer to rebook if they can't stay. Add one example of doing it.

What if I've only worked reception outside healthcare?

Use those stories for check-in, phones and difficult customers, and add one sentence on how you'd handle privacy and clinical questions differently in a medical office.

What should I ask at the end?

Ask about daily patient volume, which systems they use, how phone triage questions are handed to nurses, and how new staff are trained on privacy.
