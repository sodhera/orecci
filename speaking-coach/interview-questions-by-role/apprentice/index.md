# Apprenticeship interview questions and how to answer them

Apprenticeship interviews ask why you want this trade or company, what you already know about the work, how you learn best, how you would balance college days with work, and how you show you are reliable. Back each answer with something real, like a hands-on hobby, a part-time job or a school project, and show you expect to start at the bottom and learn.

## What employers want from an apprentice

An employer taking on an apprentice is investing years of training in you. They already know you can't do the job yet. What they want to find out is simple:

- **Do you really want this trade or field,** or did you just need something to do?
- **Will you turn up,** on time, every day, including the cold Monday in February?
- **Will you listen, learn and ask** when you don't know, rather than guess?

Every answer should help them say yes to one of those. This applies whether the apprenticeship is in a trade like plumbing or welding, or in an office field like accounting, IT or business administration.

## Why this trade, and why this company?

Weak answers sound like they could be for any job: "I like working with my hands." Stronger answers have a moment and a reason.

Example answer for a mechanic apprenticeship:

> "My uncle has an old pickup that's always breaking, and since I was fourteen I've helped him fix it on weekends. The first time we replaced the brake pads I realised I liked figuring out why something wasn't working more than anything I did at school. I've looked at your garage's reviews and a lot of people mention you explain the problem before you fix it. That's the kind of mechanic I want to learn to be."

For "why us", find one real detail: the kind of work they do, how long apprentices stay, a project they finished locally, or something an employee told you. For more on the trade question, see [why did you choose this career](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/why-did-you-choose-this-career/).

## Apprenticeship versus full-time study

Interviewers often ask why you didn't choose university or full-time college. Don't run down studying. Say what pulls you toward learning by doing.

> "I learn best when I can see the result of what I'm doing. In school I did well in design tech because I could build something and test it. An apprenticeship means I learn the theory and use it the same week. I also like that I'll be earning and getting real experience from the start."

If you tried a course and left, say so honestly and briefly, then say what you learned about how you work best.

## Learning on the job

Expect "How do you learn best?" and "What would you do if you didn't understand an instruction?" These check whether you'll ask or bluff. On a site or in a workshop, bluffing can be dangerous.

Example answer:

> "I learn best by watching once and then doing it with someone checking. If I didn't understand an instruction, I'd ask straight away and repeat it back: 'So you want me to cut these to 600 and stack them by the door?' I'd rather look new than get it wrong and waste materials or make it unsafe."

The "repeat it back" detail is the strongest part. It shows a habit, not just a good attitude.

## Balancing college days and work

Most apprenticeships mix work with classroom learning or college days. Interviewers want to hear that you've thought about the load.

| Weak | Stronger |
| --- | --- |
| "I'll manage." | "I'd do coursework on set evenings, the way I fitted homework around my Saturday job." |
| "College is the easy part." | "I'd take the theory seriously, because it's what lets me understand why we do things on site." |
| "I'll catch up on weekends." | "If a work deadline clashed with an assignment, I'd tell my supervisor and tutor early, not the day before." |

## Proving you're reliable

You can't prove reliability with adjectives. Use a track record, however small:

- A part-time job you've kept for a year or more.
- A paper round, sports training or volunteering you never missed.
- A school attendance record you're proud of.
- Looking after a younger sibling before school every day.

Example answer:

> "I've worked Saturdays at a garden centre for two years. I've been late once, when the bus broke down, and I messaged my manager before my shift started. They've started giving me the opening shift because they know I'll be there."

## How to prepare

1. Research the trade: what a normal day looks like, the tools, the hours.
2. Write one "why this trade" story and one "why this company" detail.
3. Prepare examples for reliability, feedback and something you made or fixed.
4. Prepare one question to ask, such as "What do your best apprentices do in their first month?"
5. Practice out loud. Speaking Coach can play the interviewer and follow up on your answers, so you're ready when they ask "and why does that matter to you?"

## Questions people ask

### What if I have no experience in the trade?

That's expected. Talk about anything practical you've done, such as fixing a bike, building furniture or helping a relative with a job, and show you've researched the work.

### Is it okay to say I'm nervous?

Yes. A short "I'm a bit nervous because I really want this" is honest and often lands well. Then answer the question.

### How should I dress for an apprenticeship interview?

Smart and clean, a step above what you'd wear on the job. If you're unsure, ask the person who invited you.

### What questions should I ask at the end?

Ask about the work and the training: who you'd shadow, what a first week looks like, and what past apprentices went on to do.

### What if they ask about my grades?

Be honest. If a grade was low, say what you're doing about it and point to the subjects or practical work where you did well.
