School counselor interview questions and how to answer them

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

School counselor interviews focus on four situations: what you do when a student tells you something serious, how you guide students toward college or careers, how you explain the limits of confidentiality, and how you handle a parent who disagrees with you. Answer each with your steps in order, the words you'd actually say to the student or parent, and how you'd follow your school's procedures.

What the panel needs to trust

A school counselor panel usually includes a principal, another counselor and sometimes a teacher or district staff member. They are trusting you with students on their worst days. So they listen for two things: that your process is safe, and that students and parents would feel heard by you.

Always talk about students without names or details that could identify them, and remember that reporting rules differ by state, country and district. Say you'd follow the law and your school's procedures, and show you know who to involve.

A student disclosure

This is the question that matters most. Panels want a calm, ordered answer.

  1. Listen and stay calm. Let the student talk. Don't push for details or ask leading questions.
  2. Don't promise secrecy. Be honest that you may need to share it to keep them safe.
  3. Reassure them. They did the right thing by telling you.
  4. Keep them safe now. For self-harm, follow the school's risk assessment procedure and don't leave them alone if they're at risk.
  5. Report and inform. Follow your mandated reporting duties and school protocol, and tell the right administrator.
  6. Document what was said, in the student's words, and what you did.
  7. Follow up with the student over the next days.

Example answer (made-up details):

"A ninth grader came to my office after class and said she'd been cutting. I thanked her for telling me and asked if she was safe right now. I explained, gently, that I'd need to involve her parents and our school's support team because her safety came first, and that I'd be with her through it. I stayed with her while I followed our risk assessment protocol, then called her mother together with her. I wrote up what she said in her words and checked in with her each morning that week."

College and career guidance

Show that you meet students where they are, especially those without family experience of the process.

"A junior told me college wasn't for people like him. I didn't argue. I asked what he liked doing outside school. He fixed bikes for neighbors. We looked at three paths together: an apprenticeship, a technical program at the community college and a four-year engineering program. I helped him with the forms step by step and invited his mother to a meeting in the evening, since she worked days. He chose the technical program and enrolled."

Mention how you reach every student, not just the motivated ones: classroom lessons, small groups, family nights, scheduled check-ins by grade.

Confidentiality

Panels may ask you to say, as if to a student, what you tell them at the start. Keep it short and clear.

"What you tell me stays between us, with a few exceptions. If you tell me someone is hurting you, you're thinking of hurting yourself or someone else, or someone else is in danger, I have to share it with people who can help. If that happens, I'll tell you first."

With teachers, show that you share what helps them support the student without giving away private details. If the panel pushes on a gray area, the ethical dilemma guide shows how to reason out loud.

Parent conflict

Show listening first, then shared goals.

"A father was angry I'd suggested his daughter drop an advanced class because she was overwhelmed. I asked him to tell me what worried him. He feared it would hurt her college chances. I showed him that colleges look at the whole transcript, and that her grades in her other classes were slipping. We agreed she'd stay for three more weeks with tutoring, and we'd all meet again. When we did, he suggested the change himself."

Questions people ask

What's the difference between a school counselor and a mental health counselor interview?

School counselor interviews cover academic and career guidance and school systems, as well as wellbeing. Mental health counselor interviews go deeper on clinical work. See mental health counselor questions.

How do I answer a question about a large caseload?

Show a system: tiered support, group work, scheduled check-ins and working with teachers so the students most at risk are seen first.

What if I'm a new counselor with only internship experience?

Use internship cases, and be clear about what you did under supervision. Panels hiring new counselors expect it.

What should I ask at the end?

Ask about caseload size, how crisis response works, how counselors are involved in scheduling and what support exists from social workers or psychologists.
