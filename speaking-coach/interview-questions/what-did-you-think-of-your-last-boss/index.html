What did you think of your last boss? How to answer it

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Name one genuine thing your last boss did well and what you learned from it, then, if needed, describe what you work best with as a preference, not a complaint. Even with a bad manager, stick to facts about style ('he was very hands-on') rather than judgments ('he was a micromanager'). Interviewers are checking whether you'll talk about them that way one day.

Why it's a trap, and why it's fair

This question sounds like an invitation to vent. It isn't. The interviewer may become your manager. Whatever you say about your last one tells them:

  • How you talk about people who aren't in the room.
  • What kind of management you need, and whether they can give it.
  • Whether you take responsibility, or blame others when things go wrong.

You don't have to pretend your boss was great. You do have to sound fair. The rule is simple: describe, don't judge.

Describe, don't judge

The same manager can be described in a way that sounds bitter or a way that sounds mature. Here's how to translate:

Judgment (avoid)Description (use)
"He was a micromanager.""He liked to review work closely before it went out."
"She never gave feedback.""She was very hands-off, so I learned to ask for feedback directly."
"He was disorganised.""Priorities often changed late, so I got good at re-planning quickly."
"She played favourites.""She worked most closely with the longer-serving team members."
"He was never around.""He managed three teams, so I had a lot of independence."
"She was terrible at her job."Don't say it. Find one fair thing instead.

Every description is true and neutral, and several turn into a skill you gained.

A three-part answer

  1. One genuine strength. Something specific they did well. "She was excellent with clients. I watched her calm down some very angry ones."
  2. What you learned from them. "I picked up how she prepared for those calls, with three key points written down beforehand."
  3. Optional: what you work best with. Phrase it as your preference. "I work best with regular feedback, so I'd usually set up a short check-in."

Stop there. Don't add a "but" with a list of problems.

Example answers

These are examples with made-up details. Base yours on something genuinely true about your manager.

Example answer about a good boss

"I learned a lot from her. She was very clear about what good work looked like, and she gave feedback on the same day, not weeks later. I copy that now when I'm reviewing a junior colleague's work. If anything, she sometimes took on too much herself, which taught me to ask early if I can help."

Example answer about a difficult boss

"He was very detail-focused and liked to see work before it went to clients. Early on I found that slow, but I learned a lot about checking my own work from his edits. I started sending him drafts earlier, which meant fewer changes at the end. I do my best work with a bit more independence once trust is built, and I think that's something we got to by the end."

Example answer about a hands-off boss

"She managed a big team and gave us a lot of freedom. That suited me in most ways, because I like owning my work. It also taught me to ask for feedback rather than wait for it. I'd book a short call every two weeks to check I was on track. Here, I'd love to understand how you like to give feedback."

Example answer if you've had several managers

"I've had three managers in two years because of restructuring. Each was different. The one I learned most from was very structured about weekly priorities, and I still plan my week the way she did."

Mistakes to avoid

MistakeWhy it hurtsTry instead
Venting about a bad bossThey imagine you saying it about themDescribe style neutrally
"They were perfect"Sounds rehearsed and hard to believeGive one real strength and one learning
Blaming them for why you're leavingSounds like you avoid responsibilityTalk about what you're moving towards
Sharing private detailsUnprofessionalKeep it to work style
Long stories about one bad weekDraws attention to conflictTwo or three sentences, then stop

If there was serious misconduct, like harassment or something illegal, you don't need to explain it here. A short neutral line ("There were some difficulties in the team, which is one reason I'm looking to move") is enough.

Practice it out loud

Tone matters more than words in this answer. A neutral sentence said with a sigh still sounds bitter. Say your answer out loud and record it; listen for any edge in your voice. Then practice the follow-up: "What would you have changed about how they managed you?" Speaking Coach's AI interviewer can ask that kind of follow-up based on what you said.

Questions people ask

What if my boss really was terrible?

Find one fair thing, even small, and one thing you learned, like how to stay organised without support. Leave the rest out.

Is it okay to say anything negative?

One mild point, phrased as a preference or a lesson, is fine. "I'd have liked more regular feedback" is acceptable; "she never told us anything" isn't.

What if I'm leaving because of my boss?

Talk about what you're looking for next, not what you're escaping. See why are you leaving your current job.

What if I never had a direct manager?

Talk about whoever reviewed your work: a team lead, a client, a supervisor or a professor. The same rule applies.

How long should the answer be?

About 30 to 45 seconds. Short answers here sound more confident and give less room for awkward follow-ups.
