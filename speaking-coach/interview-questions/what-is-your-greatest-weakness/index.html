What is your greatest weakness? How to answer, with examples

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Name a real weakness that isn't central to the job, give one short example of when it showed up, and explain the specific habit you now use to manage it, with a sign that it's working. Skip disguised strengths like 'I'm a perfectionist': interviewers hear them as dodging the question.

What the interviewer is testing

Nobody expects you to confess something that ruins your chances. The question checks three things:

  • Self-awareness. Do you know where you fall short, or do you think you have no gaps?
  • Honesty. Will you give a straight answer to an uncomfortable question?
  • Coachability. When you notice a problem, do you do something about it?

The weakness itself matters less than what you say after naming it. Half your answer should be about what you do now.

How to pick your weakness

Run each candidate weakness through three filters:

  1. Is it real? Something a past manager or colleague would recognise. If you'd be surprised to hear it in a reference call, it's not your weakness.
  2. Is it outside the core of this job? A weakness in public speaking is survivable for a data analyst and fatal for a sales trainer.
  3. Are you actively working on it? You need a concrete habit and some evidence, not "I'm trying to be better".

The same weakness can be safe for one job and risky for another:

Nervous presenting to large groupsAnalyst, developer, lab workSales, teaching, training
Saying yes to too many requestsMost individual rolesProject or account management, unless you show the fix clearly

Losing focus on long, detailed documentsCreative, client-facing rolesAccounting, legal, compliance

A four-part answer

  1. Name it plainly. One sentence, no hedging. "I've tended to take on more than I can deliver well."
  2. Show it once. A short moment when it caused a problem. This proves it's real.
  3. Say what you do now. A specific habit, tool or routine, not a personality change.
  4. Give the evidence. What's different since. Keep it honest: "it's better", not "it's solved".

Aim for 45–60 seconds. Most people spend too long on the weakness and rush the fix. Flip that ratio.

Example answers

Made-up examples to show the structure. Use a weakness that is actually yours.

A graduate: speaking up in meetings

"I find it hard to speak up in group discussions, especially with people more senior than me. In my placement last summer, I had a better way to set up a tracking sheet and only mentioned it to my supervisor a week later, after the team had built it the other way. Since then I prepare one point before every meeting and make myself say it in the first ten minutes. In my final-year project group I ended up running our weekly check-ins, which I wouldn't have volunteered for a year earlier."

Mid-career: taking on too much

"I say yes too easily. Last year I agreed to help two other teams with reporting on top of my own work, and my own monthly report went out three days late. Now I keep a simple list of everything I've committed to, and when someone asks for something new, I check it and tell them honestly when I could do it, or ask my manager which to drop. I haven't missed a reporting deadline since I started doing that."

A new manager: delegating

"Delegating has been my weak spot since I moved into a team lead role. I used to keep the tricky client work because it was faster to do it myself, and two people on my team told me in a review that they weren't getting a chance to grow. I now hand over one complex account each quarter with a short checklist and a weekly fifteen-minute check-in instead of doing it myself. One of those team members now runs our largest account on her own."

Other ways the question is asked

  • "What's an area you're working to improve?"
  • "What would your last manager say you need to work on?"
  • "What feedback have you had that was hard to hear?"

The same answer works for all of them. For the feedback version, it helps to also read how do you handle criticism. And if they ask for two weaknesses, have a second, smaller one ready, such as a technical skill you're still learning.

How to practise it

This is the answer people most often deliver too fast, because they want it over with. Say yours out loud, time it, and check the fix takes at least as long as the weakness. Questions people ask

Can I say my weakness is public speaking?

Yes, if the job doesn't depend on it and you can describe what you're doing about it, such as volunteering to present team updates. Avoid it for sales, teaching or client-facing roles.

Should I use a weakness I've already fixed?

A weakness you've mostly fixed is ideal, as long as you describe it honestly as something you still watch. Claiming it's completely gone can sound too neat.

How honest should I be?

Fully honest about something real, but selective about which real thing you choose. You don't need to share your biggest flaw, only a genuine one.

Is "I'm too detail-oriented" a good answer?

Only if you make it concrete and show the cost, for example spending too long on small formatting while a bigger deadline slipped. As a one-line answer, it sounds like a dodge.

Can I mention a technical skill gap?

Yes, it's often the safest option, such as a software tool or a language you're learning. Just make sure the skill isn't listed as essential in the job post.
