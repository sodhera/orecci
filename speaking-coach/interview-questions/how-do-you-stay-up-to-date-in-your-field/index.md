# How do you stay up to date in your field? Example answers

Describe a real learning habit with two or three named sources you actually use, such as a newsletter, a professional body, a community or a course, and how often you use them. Then give one recent example of something you learned and applied at work. The applied example matters most: it proves the habit is real.

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence. Updated 2026-10-22. Source: https://www.orecci.com/speaking-coach/interview-questions/how-do-you-stay-up-to-date-in-your-field/

## What the interviewer wants to know

Every field changes: tools, rules, research, customer expectations. The interviewer wants to know whether you'll keep up without being told to. They're also checking whether your answer is real. "I read a lot of articles" can't be checked. "I read the release notes for every major version of the framework we use" can.

So the answer has three parts: **habit**, **sources**, **application**.

## The three-part answer

1. **The habit.** How and when you learn. "I spend about twenty minutes most mornings on industry reading, and I do one course a year."
2. **Two or three named sources.** Real ones you could discuss. Mix the types in the table below.
3. **One thing you applied recently.** What you learned, where, and what changed when you used it.

Keep it to about 60 seconds. The applied example should get the most time.

## Kinds of sources to name

Name specific ones you actually use. If they ask "What did you read there last week?", you need an answer.

| Source type | Examples of what to name | Signals |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Professional body or association | Your national nursing, accounting, HR or engineering body and its journal or updates | You take the profession seriously |
| Official documentation and release notes | The changelog for the tools or software you use daily | Practical, hands-on learning |
| Newsletters and blogs from practitioners | One or two you open every week | Steady habit |
| Podcasts | A show in your field you listen to on the commute | Learning fits into your routine |
| Communities | A Slack group, forum, local meetup or online community for your role | You learn from peers |
| Courses and certifications | A course you finished this year, a certification you're working toward | Structured learning |
| Conferences and webinars | One you attended or watched recordings from | Awareness of where the field is going |
| Colleagues | A monthly knowledge-share you run or attend at work | You learn and teach |

Two or three named sources is enough. A long list sounds made up.

## Example answers by field

These are examples with made-up details. Name the sources you really use.

### Software developer

> "I follow the release notes for the main tools we use, our framework and our database, because breaking changes usually show up there first. I also read a weekly newsletter on backend engineering and I'm in a local developers' meetup that meets once a month. Recently I read about a new caching option in our framework's latest version. I tried it on one slow endpoint and response time dropped by about a third, so we've started rolling it out."

### Nurse

> "I keep up mainly through my hospital's practice updates, the professional nursing body's journal, and the mandatory training each year, which I treat as a chance to ask questions rather than a box to tick. Last year our trust updated its sepsis screening guidance. I made a one-page summary for our ward's night team, because they'd missed the briefing, and the charge nurse now uses it for new starters."

### Digital marketer

> "I read two marketing newsletters every week, one on search and one on paid social, and I listen to a podcast on email marketing on my commute. I also do one short course a quarter. A few months ago I learned about testing subject lines with smaller audience splits before a full send. I tried it on our monthly newsletter, and open rates went up over the next three sends compared with our average."

### Accountant

> "Tax and reporting rules change every year, so I set aside time after each budget announcement to read my professional body's summary and our firm's technical update. I also do more continuing education hours than I need to, mostly on areas clients ask about. Last year a change to how a type of expense was treated affected several small business clients. I flagged it early and we adjusted their quarterly estimates before it caused problems."

### Graduate or career changer

> "I'm early in this field, so I learn on purpose. I'm halfway through an online course in data analysis, I follow two analysts who write about their work every week, and I try out what I learn on public datasets. Last month I learned how to build a simple cohort analysis, and I did one on a public bike-share dataset to make sure I understood it."

## Mistakes to avoid

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Try instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "I google things when I need to" | Reactive, not a habit | Describe a routine, even a small one |
| "I read a lot of articles" | Vague and impossible to check | Name the source |
| Naming a famous source you don't actually read | One follow-up question exposes it | Only name what you use |
| A long list of sources | Sounds rehearsed | Two or three, plus one example |
| No applied example | Sounds like reading for its own sake | One thing you learned and used |
| "My company sends us on training" | Passive | Show what you choose to learn yourself |

## Follow-ups to expect

- "What's a trend in our industry you think matters?"
- "What did you read last week?"
- "Is there something you disagree with that people in your field believe?"

Have one opinion ready about a change in your field. It doesn't need to be bold, just yours. If they ask for a time you had to pick up something new fast, see [learning something quickly](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/tell-me-about-a-time-you-learned-something-quickly/).

## Say it like a habit, not a list

This answer can sound recited if you list sources in one breath. Practise saying it out loud, slowing down for the applied example, which is the part people remember. Speaking Coach can ask the question and follow up with "What did you read there recently?", which is where a padded answer gets caught. ## Questions people ask

### What if I don't really have a learning habit?

Start one before the interview. A week of reading a relevant newsletter and one short course gives you something honest to say, and it's useful anyway.

### Should I mention social media as a source?

Yes, if it's specific: "I follow a few practising engineers who post about their projects." "I scroll LinkedIn" sounds weak.

### Is it okay to mention AI tools for learning?

Yes, if you describe how you use them well, such as checking facts against original sources or using them to get started on a new topic.

### How many sources should I name?

Two or three. More than that sounds like a list you prepared rather than a habit you have.

### What if my field doesn't change much?

Most fields change more than they seem to: regulations, tools, customer expectations. Pick the part that does change and talk about that.
