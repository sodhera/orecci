Why were you laid off? How to explain a layoff in an interview

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Explain a layoff in two neutral sentences: what the company cut and why, and how your role was affected. Then add one line on what you did well before it and one on what you've done since. A layoff is a business decision about roles, not a judgment of your work, so say it plainly and move on.

A layoff is not a firing

A layoff, or redundancy, means the company cut the role, often several roles at once, for business reasons: budget, restructuring, a closed office, a cancelled product. Being fired is about a person's performance or conduct.

Many people who were laid off answer as if they'd been fired: apologetic, defensive, over-explaining. That makes an ordinary event sound like a problem. Interviewers see layoffs all the time. What they want to know is simple:

  • Was it really about the role, not you?
  • Were you doing good work before it happened?
  • What have you done since?

If you were actually let go for performance and it was called a layoff, don't use the word "layoff". Use the honest version on the page about being fired.

The two-sentence explanation

Write your explanation in exactly two sentences, and learn it so well you can say it calmly.

  1. What the company did, and why. "In March, the company cut its customer success team by half after losing two major clients."
  2. How it affected you. "My role was one of the ones removed."

That's the whole explanation. Then add:

  • One line on your work before it. "In my two years there, I kept a portfolio of 40 accounts with the best renewal rate on the team."
  • One line on what you've done since. "Since then I've completed a certification in our CRM system and done two months of freelance onboarding work."

Total: around 25–35 seconds.

Different kinds of layoffs, and what to stress

SituationWhat to sayWhat to stress
Large public layoffName the scale: "one of about 800 roles"It was company-wide, not personal
Whole team or office closed"The whole London office closed"No one was kept, so no selection
Small cut at a small company"They cut three roles, including mine"Your results before the cut
Your role merged or moved"My role moved to the head office in another city"You chose not to relocate, or weren't offered it
You took voluntary redundancy"I took a voluntary package when it was offered"Why it made sense, and your plan

If only some people were kept and you weren't one of them, expect a follow-up like "Why you and not others?" Answer honestly and briefly: "They kept the two people with the longest tenure" or "They kept the roles that supported the core product, and mine was on the side project."

Example answers

These examples use invented details. Use your own.

A large tech layoff (software engineer)

"In January the company cut around 15 percent of staff after a funding round fell through, and my whole platform team was removed. I'd been there three years and led the migration to our new payment system, which went live without downtime. Since then I've been contributing to an open-source project in the same area and I've done a short contract helping a startup set up their payment testing. I'm looking for a permanent role where I can go deeper into that work."

A closed location (retail supervisor)

"The store where I worked closed in the summer when the chain shut twelve of its smaller branches. I'd been a supervisor there for four years and trained most of the newer staff. I helped run the closing stock-take and stayed until the last day. Since then I've been doing seasonal shifts at a garden centre while looking for a supervisor role, and this one stood out because of the team size."

A small company cutting back (marketing)

"The agency lost its largest client last year, and they reduced the team from twelve to eight. They kept the account managers and cut two content roles, including mine. Before that, I'd written the client case studies that brought in two new accounts. Since then I've freelanced for three small businesses, which has given me more experience with paid social, and that's a big part of this job."

A long gap after the layoff

"I was laid off from my analyst role eight months ago when the company closed its regional office. The job market in my field has been slow, so I've used the time to finish a data visualisation course and build two public dashboards, which I can show you. I've been selective because I want a role I'll stay in."

If the gap has grown long, the page on explaining a gap in your resume has more ways to frame the time.

What "staying active" can look like

You don't need anything impressive. Any of these shows you've kept your skills moving:

  • A course or certification, especially one the job post mentions.
  • Freelance, contract, part-time or volunteer work in your field.
  • A project you can show: a portfolio piece, a dashboard, a writing sample.
  • Helping a former colleague's business or a community group.
  • Keeping up with your field through reading or events, if you can name something specific you learned.

Name one or two. A long list sounds like you're filling time.

Questions people ask

Do I have to say I was laid off?

You don't need to lead with it, but if they ask why you left, say it. It's a common and accepted reason, and hiding it creates more questions than it answers.

What if I was laid off twice?

Explain both briefly and point to what was the same: the company's situation, not your work. Then talk about what you're looking for to make the next role last.

Should I say how many people were cut?

Yes, if it's large or public. "One of about 200 roles" makes it clear it wasn't personal.

What if I'm still bitter about it?

That's normal, but don't let it into the interview. Practise the two sentences out loud until your voice sounds neutral. Listening back to a recording helps you hear any edge.

How do I bring it up in 'Tell me about yourself'?

Mention it in one short clause near the end, such as "my role was cut in a restructure in March", then go straight to what you're looking for now. See tell me about yourself for the full structure.
