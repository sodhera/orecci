Tell me about a time you faced an ethical dilemma: how to answer

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Choose a real situation where doing the right thing had a cost, such as pressure from a manager, a customer or a friend, but where you acted well and nobody's privacy is at risk. Tell it in STAR form, spending most of the time on how you weighed the options and what you actually did. End with the outcome and the principle you'd apply again.

What the interviewer is listening for

This is an integrity question. The interviewer isn't trying to catch you out. They want to see three things:

  • You notice when something isn't right, even when it's small or normal in your workplace.
  • You can reason through it, weighing what's at stake for different people.
  • You act, in a way that's proportionate and professional, even when it costs you something.

A real dilemma has a pull in both directions: loyalty to a colleague against honesty to a customer, a target against a rule, kindness against fairness. If the right answer was obvious and cost nothing, it isn't a dilemma, and the interviewer will notice.

Choosing a dilemma that's safe to share

This is the hardest part. Use this table to filter your options.

Safe to shareRisky to share
Being asked to round numbers up in a report, and pushing backA crime you witnessed and didn't report
A customer overpaid or was undercharged, and you corrected itAnything involving confidential details you'd have to reveal
A friend at work asked you to cover for themA story where you did the wrong thing and never fixed it
A supplier offered a gift that broke policyNaming a real company or person in a bad light
Being asked to share information you shouldn'tAnything still under investigation or legal dispute
A colleague took credit for someone else's workEthics of politics or religion
You noticed a safety shortcut becoming routineA dilemma where the "ethical" choice was to break your employer's rules

Also avoid stories where you come across as a lone hero who reported everyone. Proportion matters: most good stories involve a conversation, not a dramatic escalation.

How to tell it

Use STAR, with the weight on your reasoning:

  1. Situation. The setting, in one or two sentences. Keep names out.
  2. The tension. Why it was hard. What were you being pulled toward, and what was the cost of doing the right thing?
  3. Action. What you did, step by step. Who you talked to, what you said, and why that route.
  4. Result. What happened, including any cost to you.
  5. The principle. One sentence on what you'd do again.

Aim for 90 seconds to two minutes. Part 2 is what separates good answers from rehearsed ones: say honestly why it was tempting to do the easy thing.

Example answers

These are examples with made-up details. Use your own experience.

Sales: the end-of-quarter report

"At the end of a quarter, my team was a little short of target. My manager suggested I log two deals as closed because the clients had verbally agreed and would sign the following week. It was tempting. It would have helped the whole team's bonus, and the deals probably would close. But the contracts weren't signed, and our reporting rules were clear. I told my manager privately that I wasn't comfortable logging them, and suggested we note them as 'committed' in the forecast with the expected dates. He wasn't happy, and we missed target that quarter. One of those two deals fell through. My manager later told me he was glad we hadn't counted it. I'd do the same again: I'd rather report a miss than a number that isn't true."

Retail: the friend on the till

"A coworker I was friendly with was regularly giving discounts to her friends that weren't allowed. She asked me not to say anything. I didn't want to get her in trouble, and I knew she was struggling for money. I talked to her first, privately, and said it had to stop because it put both of us at risk. She stopped for a while, then started again. At that point I told our supervisor, and I told my coworker I was going to before I did. It changed our friendship, which was hard. But I think talking to her first was the right step, and so was not letting it go on."

Graduate: the group project

"In a final-year group project, one member copied a section from an online source with only small changes. The deadline was the next morning, and rewriting meant staying up late. Two of the group wanted to submit it anyway. I said I wasn't willing to have my name on it, and offered to rewrite the section with them that night. We finished at about 2 a.m. The project got a good grade, and I've kept that standard: if my name is on something, I need to be able to stand behind it."

Healthcare: the shortcut

"On a busy ward, I noticed a habit of skipping a second check on certain medications during the night shift because staffing was tight. Nobody was being careless on purpose, it had just become normal. I raised it at the team huddle as a question rather than an accusation: 'Are we sure we're all doing the second check at night?' The charge nurse agreed to review it, and we changed the night routine so a second nurse was always available for those checks. Raising it in the team, not going over people's heads, made it easier for everyone to fix."

Mistakes to avoid

MistakeWhy it hurtsTry instead
"I've never faced an ethical dilemma"Sounds naive or evasiveLook for everyday ones: pressure, shortcuts, loyalty
A dilemma with no real tensionSounds rehearsedSay honestly why the wrong choice was tempting
Naming the people or companyIndiscreet; they'll wonder what you'd say about themUse roles: "a colleague", "my manager"
Escalating immediately and dramaticallyCan sound rigid or unkindShow proportion: often a private conversation first
A story where you did wrong and never fixed itRaises real concernsChoose one where you acted, or later put it right
Sounding preachyPuts the interviewer offKeep the principle to one plain sentence

This page offers general interview advice only. If you're facing a real ethical or legal issue at work now, your employer's policy, an HR team or a qualified adviser is the place to go.

Practise the hard part out loud

The tension and the conversation you had are the parts people rush, because they're uncomfortable. Practise saying them slowly and plainly. If you want a partner, Speaking Coach can ask the question and follow up with "Why didn't you just go to HR?" or "Would you do the same thing again?", then show you where you sped up or hesitated. Questions people ask

What if I can't think of a dilemma?

Think about times you were asked to bend a rule, cover for someone, round a number, accept a favour or keep quiet. Most people have one of those.

Should the story end well for me?

Not necessarily. A story where doing the right thing cost you something, like a missed bonus, is often stronger.

Can I use a story from outside work?

Yes, if you have little work experience. Make sure it's still appropriate for an interview and shows the same reasoning.

What if the dilemma involved my current employer?

Keep it general and avoid anything confidential or still ongoing. If in doubt, choose a different story.

How is this different from a hypothetical ethics question?

"What would you do if..." asks for your reasoning in the abstract. This asks for proof from your past. Prepare one real story; it works for both.
