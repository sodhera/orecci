Aren't you overqualified for this job? How to answer the concern

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Treat 'overqualified' as a polite way of saying the interviewer is worried you'll want more money, get bored, or leave when something better comes up. Name your real reason for wanting this level of role, answer the specific worry directly, and show how your extra experience helps them without threatening anyone on the team.

What "overqualified" usually means

When someone asks "Aren't you overqualified?", they rarely doubt your skills. They have one or more of these worries:

Hidden worryWhat they're thinking
Pay"They're used to earning more. They'll be unhappy with our salary, or ask for more soon."
Boredom"This work will feel too easy. They'll lose interest and do it badly."
Leaving soon"They're taking this until a better job comes along."
Fitting in"They'll second-guess the manager, who may have less experience than them."
Flight to a bigger role"They'll want promotion faster than we can give it."

Your answer works best when you work out which worry is behind the question and answer that one. If you aren't sure, you can ask, kindly: "That's fair to ask. Is the concern more about salary or about whether I'd stay?"

The core of your answer: your real reason

Interviewers can tell a real reason from a polished one. Some common, legitimate reasons for stepping back or sideways:

  • You want to stop managing people and go back to hands-on work.
  • You want more predictable hours, less travel or less stress, for family or health.
  • You're changing industries and are happy to start lower to learn.
  • You've moved to a new area or country and want to build from a solid base.
  • You care about this organisation's mission more than your title.
  • You're nearing retirement and want meaningful work without the pressure of a senior role.

Say yours in one or two sentences. Don't apologise for it.

Then answer each worry

Pick the lines that match the worry. Don't use all of them, or it sounds like a prepared defence.

  • Pay: "I've looked at the range for this role and I'm comfortable with it. My priority right now is the work and the hours, not getting back to my last salary." Only say this if it's true. The page on salary expectations covers how to discuss the number.
  • Boredom: "There's a lot here that's new to me: your systems, your customers, the industry. I'd expect to be learning for the first year at least."
  • Leaving soon: "I'm not job hunting for a bigger title. I've done the senior role and I know what it costs. This is the role I want."
  • Fitting in with the manager: "I've been a manager, which means I know how useful it is to have someone on the team who just gets things done and doesn't need to be in charge."
  • Promotion: "If there's a chance to grow later, great, but I'm not taking this as a stepping stone."

Example answers

These examples use invented details to show the shape.

Stepping back from management (operations)

"I can see why you'd ask. I ran a team of twelve for six years, and I found I missed the actual operations work: planning routes, fixing problems with suppliers. I spent most of my time in meetings and on staffing. I want to be hands-on again, and this role is exactly that. I've looked at the pay range and I'm comfortable with it. And from the other side, I know how much a manager values someone experienced who doesn't need to be in charge. I'd happily share what I know if it's useful, but I'd follow your manager's lead."

Changing industries (finance to nonprofit)

"On paper, yes. I've spent ten years in corporate finance. But I don't know the charity sector, and I'd rather start at a level where I can learn how funding and grants really work here than come in as a director who doesn't. I'd bring strong budgeting skills from day one, and I'd be learning the rest for a good while. I'm not planning to leave once I've learned; I want to build a career in this sector."

Lifestyle change (former teacher, admin role)

"I taught for twelve years and was head of department for four. I loved it, but the hours were affecting my family, and I made a deliberate choice to step into a role with predictable hours. I'm not looking for another leadership position. What I bring is organisation, calm with difficult conversations, and a lot of practice with deadlines. Those transfer straight into this job."

Near retirement (retail)

"I've had a long career in store management and I'm in the last few years of my working life. I want to keep working with customers and stay active, without the responsibility of running a store. I'm not looking to climb. I'm looking for a team I can be part of and a job I can do well for the next five or so years."

Mistakes to avoid

MistakeWhy it hurtsInstead
"I just need a job right now"Confirms you'll leave when something better comesGive your real reason for this level
Downplaying your experienceSounds false and wastes your strengthsOwn it, and show how it helps them
Arguing that you're not overqualifiedDismisses a fair concernAgree it's a fair question, then answer it
Hinting you'd want the manager's jobThreatens the person hiring youSay you're happy to follow their lead
Promising you'll never ask for a raiseNot believableSay you're comfortable with the range

The tone to aim for

This answer can easily sound either arrogant ("well, yes, I'm much more experienced than this needs") or desperate ("please, I'll take anything"). Aim for settled and sure. Practise it out loud and listen for whether you sound like you've made a choice. If you have a hiring manager who may be younger or less experienced than you, rehearse saying the line about following their lead until it sounds natural, not tactful.

Questions people ask

Should I remove experience from my CV to look less qualified?

Trimming older or less relevant roles is common, and a focused CV is a good idea. Don't hide experience in a way that becomes awkward if it comes up in references or interview questions.

What if I really am taking this job temporarily?

Be careful. If you plan to leave within months, it's fairer to say you're looking for a fixed-term or contract role. For a permanent role, only claim you'll stay if you mean it.

What if the salary really is much lower than I'm used to?

Decide your real limit before the interview. If you can accept it, say so plainly. If you can't, the role may not be right.

How do I answer if the interviewer is younger than me?

The same way, without referring to their age. Show respect for their role and focus on what you'd bring to their team.

Is 'overqualified' sometimes a cover for age discrimination?

It can be, and rules on this vary by country. In the interview itself, your best move is still to answer the business worries directly. If you believe you've been treated unfairly, an employment adviser in your country can explain your options.
