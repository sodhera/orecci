What did you like least about your last job? How to answer safely

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Name one honest dislike about the work itself, such as a structure, a process or a lack of a certain kind of task, that this new job doesn't have. Say it briefly and neutrally, add what you did to make the best of it, and link it to why this role suits you better. Never make the answer about a person.

Why interviewers ask it

This question invites you to complain, and some candidates take the bait. The interviewer is checking:

  • Fit. If you hated something about your last job and this job is full of it, you'll be unhappy here too.
  • Professionalism. Will you speak badly about an employer? If so, you'll probably speak badly about this one later.
  • Self-awareness. Can you separate a genuine mismatch from a passing frustration?

The best answers are honest, small and about the work, not about people.

Pick the right kind of dislike

Some dislikes are safe and even helpful. Others will hurt you.

Safe to sayRisky to say
Limited contact with customers or end users"My manager was terrible"
Little chance to work across teams"My coworkers were lazy"
Work was very repetitive, with little problem solving"I hated the work" (if this job is similar)
Slow decision-making with many layers of approval"The company was a mess"
A focus on one small part of a project"The pay was bad" (it sounds like you're only here for money)
Mostly remote when you wanted in-person teamwork (or the reverse)Long complaints about workload or hours

Then check it against this job. The dislike should be something this job doesn't have. If you say you disliked repetitive work and this job involves a lot of data entry, you've just talked yourself out of it.

A three-part answer

  1. The dislike (one sentence). Neutral words, about the work or structure.
  2. How you handled it (one or two sentences). What you did to make it better or work around it. This keeps the answer positive.
  3. The link (one sentence). Why this role is a better fit.

About 30–45 seconds.

Example answers

These examples use invented details to show the shape. Make sure yours is true and that the new job really differs.

Wanted more customer contact (back-office to account management)

"What I liked least was how far I was from the customer. I processed orders accurately, but I rarely found out whether the customer was happy. I started asking the sales team for feedback on the accounts I handled, which helped, but it's one reason this account role appeals to me. I'd be the person talking to the customer directly."

Slow approvals (marketing, moving from a large company to a smaller one)

"The thing I found hardest was how long decisions took. A simple social post could need four sign-offs, so we often missed the moment. I learned to plan further ahead and batch approvals, which got us faster. But I'm excited that in a team your size, I'd be closer to the people making decisions."

Narrow scope (engineering)

"In my last role I worked on one small module of a large product, and I didn't see how it fitted into the whole. I made a point of joining the wider design reviews to understand the bigger picture, which taught me a lot. This role covers a feature from design to release, and that's the kind of ownership I'm looking for."

Repetitive tasks (graduate, from a part-time job)

"In my part-time retail job, what I liked least was that most shifts were the same routine, with little chance to solve problems. I volunteered to help with stock planning to get more variety, and I enjoyed that much more. That's partly why I'm applying for this planning role."

Working mostly alone

"My last role was almost entirely solo. I'd get a brief and deliver it without much contact with the team. I missed bouncing ideas off people, so I set up a weekly check-in with two colleagues. From what you've said, this team works together closely, which suits me better."

Mistakes to avoid

MistakeWhy it hurtsInstead
Criticising your manager or coworkersYou sound hard to work withTalk about the work or the structure
Naming something this job has plenty ofShows poor fitCheck the job post first
"Nothing, I loved it"Not believable, and dodges the questionPick a small, honest dislike
A long listSounds like a complaint sessionOne dislike only
Bitter toneUndoes even good wordsPractise until it sounds matter-of-fact
Leaking confidential problemsShows poor discretionKeep internal issues private

Watch your tone

The words in this answer can be perfect and still sound negative if your voice tightens or speeds up. That often happens when the dislike is still raw. Record yourself saying it and listen back: does it sound like an observation or a grievance? If it's a grievance, choose a different, smaller dislike. The page on what you thought of your last boss deals with the people side, which you should keep out of this answer.

How it fits with other questions

This question often appears alongside why are you leaving your current job and what is your ideal work environment. Keep your answers consistent: if you're leaving because you want more ownership, your least-liked part might be narrow scope, and your ideal environment might mention ownership. Three answers that point in the same direction are far more convincing than three unrelated ones.

Questions people ask

What if I really did dislike my manager?

Don't say it. Find something about the work or structure that also bothered you, and use that. If they ask about your manager directly, answer briefly and neutrally.

Can I say the pay was too low?

It's risky as your main dislike. If pay is a real reason you're moving, mention it lightly elsewhere, along with growth or scope, not as the thing you liked least.

What if I can't think of anything I disliked?

Think about what you'd change if you could: the tools, the meetings, the variety of work. Something small and honest is enough.

Should the dislike be connected to why I'm leaving?

Ideally, yes. It makes your story consistent. But it doesn't have to be the only reason you're leaving.

Is this the same as 'What's the worst part of your job?'

Very close. Use the same approach: a small, honest, work-focused dislike that this new role doesn't have.
