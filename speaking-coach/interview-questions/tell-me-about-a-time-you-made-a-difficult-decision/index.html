Tell me about a time you made a difficult decision: examples

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Pick a decision that was yours to make, where every option had a real cost and you didn't have all the facts. Say what the options were, what you knew and didn't know, how you chose, and what happened. Then own the outcome, including any part that went badly. Interviewers are judging your reasoning under uncertainty, not whether you got lucky.

What makes a decision "difficult" in the interviewer's eyes

A difficult decision is not the same as a stressful day. For this question, it should have three features:

  • It was your call. You made it, or you were the one recommending it and your recommendation was followed. Group decisions where you just voted are weak.
  • Every option cost something. If one choice was obviously right, there was no decision. Choosing between disappointing a client and burning out your team is a decision.
  • You didn't know everything. You had to decide before all the facts were in. This is the part interviewers care most about, because most real work decisions look like this.

Then they want to see that you owned the result, good or bad, without excuses.

Lay out your decision before you tell it

How to structure the spoken answer

  1. The decision and the stakes (15 seconds). "I had to decide whether to… If I got it wrong, …"
  2. The options and their costs (15 seconds). Two options is usually enough. Three at most.
  3. The missing information (10 seconds). "What I didn't know was…" Say it plainly. It makes the decision sound real.
  4. How you chose (20 seconds). The one or two factors that tipped it. Who you consulted, if anyone.
  5. What happened and what you own (15 seconds). The result, including the downside.

About 75 seconds in total. Use STAR as the backbone, but make sure "how you chose" gets more time than "what happened."

Example answers

These examples are made up to show the structure.

Choosing which client to let down (account manager)

"Two of my clients needed the same designer in the same week, and we couldn't hire freelance help in time. One was our largest client, with a routine campaign. The other was a smaller client with a product launch they'd announced publicly. I didn't know whether the large client would accept a delay; they'd never been asked before. I decided the launch couldn't move but the routine campaign probably could, so I called the large client myself, explained, and offered to deliver in two parts. They agreed, though they weren't happy, and I could hear it. The launch went out on time. I own that I put strain on our biggest relationship, so the next month I set up a shared calendar with them so they'd see our capacity before booking."

Stopping a project (team lead)

"Three months into building an internal reporting tool, I had to decide whether to finish it or switch to a ready-made product a vendor had just released. We'd already spent a lot of time, and my team was proud of what they'd built. I didn't know if the vendor tool would handle our data, and the trial would take two weeks. I ran the trial with one person while the rest kept building, so we wouldn't lose time either way. The vendor tool handled about 90 percent of what we needed, so I stopped our build. Two people on the team were disappointed, and I told them directly why. We moved them onto the integration, which used much of what they'd built."

A graduate decision

"In my final-year project, two weeks before the deadline, I had to decide whether to cut one of our three experiments or ask for an extension that would cost marks. I didn't know whether the third experiment would even give usable results. I chose to cut it and write up the first two properly, and I explained the gap in the report as future work. We submitted on time with a strong mark, though the examiner asked about the missing experiment in the viva. I'd make the same call, but I'd have planned the experiments in order of importance from the start."

Mistakes to avoid

MistakeWhy it hurtsInstead
A decision with an obvious right answerThere's no judgment to showChoose one where both options hurt
A personal life decision (moving cities)Hard to link to the jobUse a work or study decision if you have one
Blaming bad informationSounds like you dodge ownershipSay what you didn't know and that you chose anyway
Making it a group decisionYour judgment disappearsSay "I decided" or "I recommended"
Only happy endingsCan sound rehearsedInclude one cost you accepted

When they push back

Expect follow-up questions, such as:

  • "What would you have done with more time?"
  • "Who disagreed with you?"
  • "Would you make the same decision today?"

The honest answer to the last one is often "mostly yes, with one change." That shows you've reflected without second-guessing yourself. If you freeze on follow-ups, the guide on thinking on your feet gives you a short way to buy a few seconds and answer clearly.

Questions people ask

Does the decision have to have worked out?

No. A decision that went partly wrong, told with clear reasoning and real ownership, can be stronger than a lucky success. Don't pick a disaster that cost people their jobs.

Can I use a decision about firing someone?

Only if you're interviewing for a management role and can tell it with respect and without detail that identifies the person. Focus on the process you followed.

How is this different from a question about using data?

A data decision shows analysis. A difficult decision shows judgment when the data runs out. You can mention data, but the hard part should be the trade-off.

What if I haven't made big decisions in my career?

Scale counts less than the reasoning. Deciding which task to drop on a busy day, or whether to escalate a customer, is a real decision if both options had a cost.

Should I mention that I asked others for advice?

Yes, briefly. Consulting people shows good sense. Just make clear that the final call was yours.
