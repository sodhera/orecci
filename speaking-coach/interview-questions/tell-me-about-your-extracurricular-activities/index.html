Tell me about your extracurricular activities: interview answers

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Pick one or two activities where you had a real role, and tell one specific story from each: what you were responsible for, what you did, and what changed. Then say which skill it shows and how it fits the job. Treat clubs, sports and volunteering like work experience, because for a student they are the closest thing to it.

Why interviewers ask

When you have little or no work experience, your activities outside class are the best evidence of how you will behave at work. Did you stick with something? Did you take responsibility? Did you work with people you didn't choose? Did anything change because of you?

The interviewer is not counting clubs. Two activities with real stories beat eight memberships listed on a CV.

Choose what to talk about

Look for activities where at least one of these is true:

  • You had a defined role (captain, treasurer, events head, volunteer coordinator).
  • You did something specific you can describe (organised, built, raised, trained, fixed).
  • There is a result you can point to (an event happened, a number went up, a problem got solved).
  • You stuck with it for more than a few months.

Then match it to the job.

A simple structure

  1. The activity and your role. One sentence. "I was on the organising team of our college tech fest for two years, and events head in my final year."
  2. One specific moment. What you were responsible for, what went wrong or what you did. Two or three sentences.
  3. The result. What happened. A number if you have a real one.
  4. The link. "That's the kind of coordination this role needs."

About 60 to 90 seconds for one activity. If they want a second, they'll ask.

Example answers

These use made-up details. Swap in your own.

Fest organiser, for an operations or consulting role

"The activity I'm proudest of is our college tech fest. In my final year I was events head, which meant coordinating 12 events across two days with about 40 volunteers. Two days before the fest, our main sponsor pulled out of funding the prize money. I went through the budget with our treasurer, cut decorations, and pitched three local businesses, two of whom covered it. Everything ran on schedule. It taught me to stay calm and reshuffle a plan fast, which I think is a big part of operations work."

Athlete, for a sales role

"I played basketball for my university for four years. The part I'd highlight is my second season, when I barely got minutes. Instead of quitting, I asked the coach what I'd need to improve, and spent the summer on my shooting. By my third season I was starting. I think that's relevant to sales, where you hear no a lot and have to keep working on the parts you can control."

Volunteer, for a customer service or healthcare role

"For two years I volunteered at a community kitchen on weekends. I started serving food and later coordinated the Saturday shift of about ten volunteers. A lot of it was dealing calmly with people who were stressed or upset, and making sure nobody was turned away when we ran low. That patience with people under pressure is what I'd bring to this role."

If you don't have many activities

Not everyone had time for clubs. If you worked, cared for family or commuted long hours, those count too:

"I didn't do many clubs because I worked 20 hours a week in a supermarket to pay my fees. In my second year I became a shift lead, so I handled cash-up and opening on weekends. It taught me to manage my time hard, which I think shows in the fact that I kept my grades up throughout."

Self-driven projects also work: a blog you wrote, an app you built, a YouTube channel you ran, a language you taught yourself.

Mistakes to avoid

MistakeWhy it hurtsTry instead
Listing every clubSounds like membership, not contributionPick one or two and go deeper
Inflating your roleOne follow-up exposes itDescribe exactly what you did
Only saying "it taught me teamwork"Generic and unprovenA specific moment that shows the teamwork
Talking only about funMisses the point of the questionMention the enjoyment, then the responsibility
Political or religious groups as your main exampleCan invite bias or debateChoose another activity if you have one

Practise the story, not the list

In campus placement rounds, activities often come up in the HR interview after the technical rounds, and in group discussions your activities can give you examples to draw on. Practise telling one activity story out loud until the moment and result come out clearly. Speaking Coach can play the HR interviewer and follow up on your story ("What did you personally do?"), which is where weak answers usually fall apart.

Questions people ask

Do school activities count, or only college?

College activities are better for a graduate role. Mention school ones only if they were significant, such as a national-level competition, or if you carried them on.

What if I was just a member, not a leader?

That's fine. Describe something specific you did as a member: an event you helped run, a match you played, a task you owned. Leadership is not the only skill worth showing, though if you did lead, see how to answer leadership questions.

How many activities should I mention?

Lead with one strong story. Mention a second only if asked or if it shows a very different skill.

Can I talk about online communities or gaming?

Yes, if you had a real role, such as moderating a community, organising tournaments or building something. Describe it in the same structure.

Should extracurriculars go on my resume?

Yes, especially as a student. List your role and one result for each, so the interviewer has something to ask about.
