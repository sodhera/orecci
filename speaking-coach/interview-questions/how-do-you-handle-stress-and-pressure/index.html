How do you handle stress and pressure? Interview answer examples

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Describe two or three concrete habits you use when pressure builds, such as how you prioritise, how you keep people informed and how you reset, then prove them with one short story where the pressure was real. Don't claim you never feel stressed; show that you stay effective while you do.

What the interviewer wants to hear

Every job has pressure. In some roles, such as emergency care, call centres, kitchens, dispatch, retail at peak or trading, it's the job, every day. The interviewer wants to know:

  • Do you have a method? Not "I just cope", but specific things you do.
  • Do you keep working well, or do quality and temper slip?
  • Do you tell people early when something's going to slip?
  • Can you recover so pressure doesn't build up week after week?

"I don't get stressed" is the one answer that worries them. Everyone does. They want to know what you do about it.

Pick your habits

Choose two or three habits you really use. Specific beats general.

  • Triage. "I list everything, then sort it into must happen today, can wait, and someone else can do."
  • Next action only. "When it feels like too much, I stop looking at the whole list and do the next concrete step."
  • Say it early. "If something is going to slip, I tell my manager or customer as soon as I know, with a new time."
  • Checklists. "When I'm rushed, I rely on the checklist, not my memory."
  • A short reset. "One slow breath before the next call," or "a five-minute walk at lunch on a heavy day."
  • Ask for help. "I'm specific: can you take these two, so I can finish this one?"
  • Debrief afterwards. "After a bad day, I note what caused it so the next one goes better."

Match the habits to the role

Different jobs feel pressure differently. Lead with the habit that fits.

A three-part answer

  1. Your habits: two or three, in one or two sentences each.
  2. A proof story: one real moment, about 30 seconds, showing the habits working.
  3. Recovery: one line on how you reset afterwards, so it's sustainable.

Total: 60–90 seconds.

Example answers

Made-up examples to show the structure. Use your own habits and story.

Line cook

"The main thing is preparation. If my station is fully set up before service, the rush is just cooking, not hunting for things. During service I call out early if I'm running low or behind, so the head chef can adjust the tickets. Last Mother's Day we did nearly double our usual covers, and one cook went home sick at six. Because I'd prepped extra that morning, I took over the grill and my own station, and we kept tickets under fifteen minutes. Afterwards I always write down what ran out, so the next busy day goes smoother."

Call centre agent

"I take each call as its own thing. Between calls, I take one breath and clear my head, so an angry caller doesn't carry into the next one. If a call gets heated, I slow my voice down and repeat back what they've said, which usually calms things. In my current job I handle billing complaints, and on the day our system double-charged a few hundred customers, I took over 70 calls. I kept my notes clear so nobody had to call back twice. At the end of a day like that I go for a walk before I go home."

Project manager

"I sort everything by what will actually hurt if it's late, and I tell people early when something's going to slip. Last spring, a supplier delay meant our product launch was going to miss its date by two weeks. I told the sponsor the same day, with two options, and we agreed to launch with a reduced range on time and add the rest later. The team didn't have to work weekends, and the launch still hit its sales target for the month."

Graduate

"During my final year I was doing my dissertation and working twenty hours a week in a café. What worked was planning the week every Sunday, deciding the three most important things, and doing the hardest work in the morning before shifts. When my dissertation data came in late, I told my supervisor right away and agreed a revised plan rather than hoping I'd catch up. I submitted on time and kept every shift."

How to practise it

The irony of this question is that it's asked when you're already under some pressure. Practise it out loud until the habits come out in the same order every time, and the proof story has a clear ending. If you tend to speed up when nervous, check your pace with the pace checker. Questions people ask

Is it okay to admit I get stressed?

Yes. Admitting it, then describing what you do about it, sounds more honest and self-aware than claiming you're never stressed.

What's the difference between stress and pressure?

Pressure is the situation: a deadline, a queue, high stakes. Stress is how you feel about it. Interviewers use the words interchangeably, so you can too.

How is this different from "Tell me about a time you met a tight deadline"?

That question wants one story. This one wants your general habits first, then a story as proof. You can reuse the same story for both.

What if pressure genuinely is hard for me?

Be honest that it's something you manage actively, then focus on your habits. Consider whether the role's daily pressure suits you; it's a fair question to ask yourself before accepting.
