Why is your GPA low? How to answer questions about bad grades

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Answer in three short steps: own the grades without excuses, give the real reason in one sentence, and move straight to stronger evidence such as an upward trend, good grades in core subjects, projects, internships or certifications. Keep the explanation to about 20 seconds and spend the rest on proof. Never lie about your GPA or backlogs; they are easy to check.

What the interviewer is checking

If you got the interview, your grades were not a dealbreaker on paper. The question is about how you handle a weak spot. They want to see:

  • Honesty. Do you own it, or blame professors, the system, or bad luck?
  • Self-awareness. Do you understand what went wrong?
  • Evidence you can do the job anyway. What else shows your ability?

The candidates who struggle with this question usually spend a minute defending the grade. The ones who do well spend 20 seconds on it and a minute on what proves they are capable.

The three-step answer

  1. Own it. "You're right, my GPA isn't as strong as I'd like."
  2. Give the real reason, in one sentence. No long story. "In my first two years I didn't take my coursework seriously enough."
  3. Point to stronger evidence. This is the main part. Choose whichever is true for you:
EvidenceHow to say it
Upward trend"My last four semesters averaged 8.2, up from 6.1 in my first year."
Strong grades where it counts"In the core subjects for this role, data structures and databases, I got A grades."
A project"My final-year project is the best evidence of what I can do. I built..."
An internship"At my internship my manager gave me the quarterly reporting to own by the second month."
Certifications or self-study"I completed a certification in cloud fundamentals while finishing my degree."
Work alongside study"I worked 20 hours a week throughout my degree to pay my fees."

Total length: 45 to 60 seconds. Then stop and let them ask. If a project is your strongest evidence, prepare it properly: see how to talk about your final year project.

Common reasons, and how to phrase them

Your reason should be true and brief. Here is how to say common ones without sounding like an excuse.

Real reasonAvoid sayingSay instead
Didn't take it seriously early on"College was boring""In my first two years I didn't put in enough effort. I changed that in third year."
Wrong subject choice"I hated my course""I realised late that my branch wasn't a good fit, and I put my energy into the areas I liked, which is why I'm applying here."
Worked to support yourself"I had no time""I worked part time throughout to pay fees. It cost me some grades, but taught me to manage a full week."
Family or health situationA detailed account"I had a difficult family situation in my second year. It's resolved now, and my grades since show that."
Too many activities"I was busy with fests""I spent a lot of time running the coding club. I'd balance it better now, but I learned a lot from it."
Exam anxiety or poor exam technique"I'm just bad at exams""My exam marks don't match my project and lab work, which is a fairer picture of how I work."

You don't have to share private health or family details. "A personal situation that is now resolved" is enough.

If you have backlogs or arrears

In campus placements in India, interviewers often ask about backlogs, also called arrears, separately from your CGPA. Some companies set eligibility rules about active or past backlogs, so check each company's criteria before the drive. The campus placement interview guide covers the rest of the process.

If you have cleared backlogs:

"I had two backlogs in my second year, in engineering maths and thermodynamics. I cleared both in the next attempt. That was the semester I realised I had to change how I studied, and I haven't had one since."

The key is the word "cleared" and what changed after. If you have an active backlog, say clearly when you will clear it and what you are doing to prepare.

Example answers

These are examples with made-up details. Use your own numbers.

Graduate with an upward trend

"My overall GPA is a 2.8, which I'm not proud of. My first year was rough. I was working nights and didn't manage my time. From my third year on I averaged a 3.5, and my capstone project on inventory forecasting got the top grade in my class. That's the level I work at now."

Engineering student with a low CGPA and good projects

"My CGPA is 6.5. Honestly, I spent more time building things than preparing for exams, which I'd balance differently now. But what I built is real. I made an attendance app that our department used for a semester, and I contributed fixes to two open source projects. I'd be happy to walk you through either."

Experienced hire asked about old grades

"My grades weren't strong, and that was a long time ago. Since then I've spent four years as an analyst, and my last two reviews rated me in the top band for quality of work. I think that's the better measure now."

Say it until it sounds calm

This is a question where your voice matters as much as your words. If you rush or your voice drops, it sounds like you are hiding something. Say your answer out loud until you can deliver it at a steady pace, then move on cleanly. Questions people ask

Should I put a low GPA on my resume?

That depends on the employer. If they ask for it on the application, you must give it. If it is optional, many candidates leave it off and lead with projects. Either way, be ready to discuss it.

What if the interviewer says my GPA is below their cutoff?

Stay calm and focus on evidence. "I understand. I'd ask you to look at my project and internship work, which I think show what I can do."

Can I mention my major GPA instead?

Yes, if it is higher and relevant: "My overall GPA is 2.9, but in my major courses it's 3.4." Don't hide the overall number.

Is it okay to mention mental health as the reason?

You can, briefly and only if you're comfortable: "I had a health issue that's now managed." You don't have to give details.
