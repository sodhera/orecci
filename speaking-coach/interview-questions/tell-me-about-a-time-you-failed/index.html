Tell me about a time you failed: how to answer this question

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Pick a real failure from work or study where the outcome clearly fell short, own your part in one plain sentence, then spend most of the answer on what you learned and what you now do differently, with proof that it worked later. Avoid failures that caused serious harm or broke rules, and ones so small they don't count.

Why interviewers ask about failure

Everyone who has done real work has failed at something. The interviewer wants to see:

  • Ownership. Can you say "I" without blaming others or circumstances?
  • Learning. Did anything change in how you work?
  • Perspective. Do you know what a real failure is, and can you talk about it calmly?
  • Risk-taking. People who never fail often never tried anything hard.

"I can't think of a time I failed" is the weakest possible answer. It sounds either dishonest or like you've never stretched.

Failure, mistake, or challenge?

These questions get mixed up, but a clear answer fits the right one.

QuestionWhat it's aboutExample
A time you failedAn outcome that fell short of a goalMissed a sales target, a launch nobody used
A time you made a mistakeSomething you did wrongSent the wrong file, entered a wrong figure
A challenge you facedA hard situation you got throughShort staffing at peak season

A failure can come from a mistake, but it doesn't have to. Sometimes you did reasonable things and still fell short.

Choose a failure of the right size

Too smallRight sizeToo big or risky
"I was late to a meeting once.""I missed my first quarter's sales target by a third."Anything that harmed someone's safety or health
"My presentation slides had a typo.""A training programme I designed had half the attendance we planned."Anything illegal, unethical or a breach of policy
"I failed my driving test." (for most jobs)"I lost a client we'd had for five years."A failure that got you fired, unless asked directly
"I burned the toast.""Our team's app feature launched and almost nobody used it."A core skill of this job that you still struggle with

The ideal failure is real, had consequences, and is far enough behind you that you can show what changed.

A four-part answer

  1. What the goal was and what happened. Short and factual. 15 seconds.
  2. Your part. One sentence that starts with "I". No excuses, no long context. 10–15 seconds.
  3. What you changed. The specific habit, process or approach. 20–30 seconds.
  4. Later proof. A time since then when the change paid off. 15 seconds.

Total around 60–90 seconds. Notice the weight: most of the answer is about what came after.

Example answers

Made-up examples to show the structure. Use your own real failure.

Sales representative: missed target

"In my first quarter as a sales rep, I hit only about two-thirds of my target. Looking back, my part was clear: I spent most of my time on a few big prospects who were never going to decide that quarter, because they were exciting, and I neglected smaller accounts that could have closed. After that, I started reviewing my pipeline every Friday and splitting my week between quick wins and long-term deals. The next two quarters I was over target, and I still use that Friday review."

Product manager: a launch nobody used

"I led a feature last year to let customers export their reports, and three months after launch, very few people had used it. I'd relied on a handful of loud requests from big customers and never checked whether most users wanted it. That was my call. Now I test demand before building: a simple mock-up, shown to at least ten users from different customer types. On my next feature, that test made us cut the scope in half, and usage was much higher."

Graduate: a group competition

"In my second year, I led our team in a business case competition, and we didn't make it past the first round. The feedback was that our recommendation was strong but we ran out of time and never presented the numbers. I'd been the one keeping time, and I let the first section run long because it was going well. Since then I rehearse anything timed with a clock running, and I set a hard stop for each section. We entered again the following year and made the final."

Event organiser: low turnout

"I organised a networking evening for our professional association, and we had 35 people when we'd planned for 100. I'd promoted it only through our newsletter, assuming members would read it, and I didn't check sign-ups until a week before. Now I set sign-up checkpoints three and two weeks out, and I use at least three channels. The next event had 110 sign-ups."

How to practise it

This is an answer where your voice can give you away: people drop their volume, rush the failure and hurry to the lesson. Practise saying the failure sentence slowly and clearly. Then make sure the "what you changed" part is the longest. Questions people ask

Can I use a failure from outside work?

Yes, if you're early in your career or the interviewer asks broadly. A university, sports or volunteering failure works, as long as you show what changed in how you work.

How do I talk about a failure without making myself look bad?

Choose a failure that's well in the past, own it in one sentence, and spend most of the answer on what you changed and the proof that it worked. Owning it well makes you look better, not worse.

What if they ask for another failure?

Have a second, smaller one ready, ideally from a different area such as a technical skill or a project, so the two don't sound alike.

Should I mention being fired as my failure?

Only if they ask about it directly. If they do, see have you ever been fired for how to answer it calmly.
