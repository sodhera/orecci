How do you prioritize your work? Interview answer with examples

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Describe your method in one or two sentences, for example ranking tasks by deadline and impact and checking with your manager when priorities clash, then prove it with a real busy week: what landed on your desk, what you did first, what you moved and who you told. Ending with what happened makes the method believable.

What they're testing

Every job has more work than time. The interviewer wants to know three things:

  • Do you have a method, or do you just do whatever shouts loudest?
  • Can you handle conflict between priorities, like two people who both want something today?
  • Do you communicate when something has to slip, instead of silently missing a deadline?

The third one is the most overlooked. A good prioritiser doesn't just pick an order; they tell the right people about it.

Step 1: Name your method in plain words

Pick one way of deciding what comes first, and describe it the way you actually use it. You don't need a famous framework. If you do use one, say how, not just its name.

MethodHow to say itGood for
Deadline plus impact"I sort by what's due soonest and what affects the most people if it's late."Most office roles
Urgent vs important"I separate urgent from important. Urgent and important goes first; important but not urgent gets a protected slot in my week."Managers, planners
Patient or customer safety first"Anything that affects safety comes first, then time-critical tasks, then everything else."Healthcare, care, aviation
Revenue or client impact"I start with whatever's closest to a client or a deal closing."Sales, account management
Blockers first"I clear anything that's blocking someone else before my own work."Engineering, operations, team roles
Daily top three"Each morning I write the three things that must be done today, and I do those before email."Anyone with lots of interruptions

Step 2: Show it in a real busy week

This is the part most candidates skip, and it's what makes the answer stick. Pick a specific week when too much landed at once, and describe it in four beats:

  1. What landed. Two or three competing demands, with rough times.
  2. How you ranked them. Apply your method out loud.
  3. What you moved and who you told. Name the conversation.
  4. The result. What got done, and whether anything slipped.

Keep the whole answer to about 75–90 seconds. That's around 180–220 words.

Example answers

These are examples with made-up details. Use your own busiest week.

Example answer for an office or operations role

"I rank by deadline and by how many people are affected if it's late, and I'm upfront when something has to move. A good example was a week in March. On Monday I had the monthly supplier report due Wednesday, my manager asked for a budget summary for a Thursday board meeting, and a warehouse system issue meant two of our biggest orders were stuck. The stuck orders came first because customers were waiting, so I spent Monday morning with IT and got them moving by lunch. The board summary had a hard deadline and a senior audience, so it came next. The supplier report had the most flexibility, so I asked the supplier manager on Monday afternoon if Friday would work. She said yes. Everything was done by Friday, and nobody was surprised."

Example answer for a nurse

"Safety first, then time-critical tasks, then everything else, and I re-prioritise every time something changes. On one shift I had six patients. Within half an hour, a patient's oxygen levels dropped, two patients were due IV antibiotics at fixed times, and a family wanted to talk about discharge plans. The deteriorating patient came first. I escalated to the doctor and stayed until she was stable. I asked a colleague to give one of the antibiotics on time and gave the other myself, ten minutes later. I told the family I'd be back within the hour, and I was. Everything was documented by the end of the shift."

Example answer for a software developer

"I clear blockers first, then work on whatever's committed for the sprint, and I tell my lead early if new requests threaten that. One sprint, I was halfway through a feature due Thursday when a teammate got blocked waiting on my review, and product asked for an urgent fix for a client. I did the review in 30 minutes, because it unblocked a whole day of someone else's work. Then I checked with my lead whether the client fix outranked my feature. It did, so we moved the feature to the following Tuesday and told product the new date. Both shipped without anyone being surprised."

When two people want different things

Interviewers often follow up with: "What if your manager and another manager both say theirs is urgent?" A strong answer:

"I'd lay out both requests, the deadlines and my estimate for each, and ask my manager to decide, or ask the two of them to agree. It's not my call to rank two senior people's work against each other, but it is my job to make the trade-off clear."

Mistakes to avoid

MistakeWhy it hurtsTry instead
"I just make a to-do list"Everyone does; it isn't a methodSay how you order the list
Naming a framework with no exampleSounds memorisedShow it in a real week
"I work late until it's all done"Sounds unsustainable, not organisedShow what you moved and who you told
Never mentioning communicationMisses the key skillName who you updated and when
Too many details about the tasksThe listener gets lostOne line per task, then the decision

Practise the follow-up, not just the answer

The prepared answer is the easy part. What catches people is the curveball: "And what if a third urgent request came in at 4pm?" Practise answering a couple of those out loud. Questions people ask

Should I mention tools like a planner or task app?

Briefly, as part of the habit: "I keep everything in one task board so I can see it all at once." The method matters more than the tool.

What if my job doesn't have competing priorities?

Every job does at some point. Think of a day when a customer, a colleague and your own task all needed you at once, even in an hourly role.

Is it okay to say I sometimes ask my manager to decide?

Yes, if you bring the options and a recommendation. It shows judgement about what's your call and what isn't.

How do I answer as a student?

Use a week with coursework deadlines, a part-time job and a group project. Show how you ranked them and who you told when something moved.

What if something did slip?

Say so, and show you told people early. A deadline moved with notice is a sign of good prioritising, not a failure.
