Do you prefer working alone or in a team? How to answer it well

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Say you're comfortable with both, then be honest about which you lean toward and show it fits the role. Give one short example of each: something you did well alone and something you did well with others. Read the job first, because a role that is mostly solo deep work and one that is mostly collaboration need different emphasis.

Why "both" is right, but not enough

Almost every job needs some of each. That's why "I like both" is the correct starting point. The trouble is that it's also the answer every candidate gives, and on its own it sounds like dodging.

The interviewer is really asking: will your natural way of working fit how this job actually runs? So the strong answer has three parts: both, an honest lean, and proof that you can do the side you lean away from.

Read the role's real mix first

Before the interview, work out roughly how the job splits. Job posts give clues: "independently", "self-starter" and "remote" lean solo; "cross-functional", "stakeholders" and "collaborative" lean team.

RoleTypical mixWhat to emphasise
Software developerLong focused coding, plus reviews, stand-ups and planningDeep solo focus, plus how you use code review and pairing
Accountant or auditorDetailed solo work, with team deadlines at month or year endAccuracy alone, reliability to the team at close
Sales representativeYour own territory and targets, with a team for handoffsSelf-drive, plus sharing what works
NurseConstant teamwork and handovers, with solo judgement at the bedsideTeam communication first, solo calm under pressure
DesignerSolo creation, team critique and stakeholder feedbackOwnership of your work, openness in critique
Customer serviceMostly solo calls, with escalations and shared knowledgeHandling customers alone, knowing when to escalate
Project managerMostly team, with solo planning timeTeam coordination; protected time to plan

If you lean the opposite way from the role, you don't need to hide it. Just show you're effective in the mode the role needs most.

A simple structure

  1. Both, briefly. "I work well both ways, and most jobs need both."
  2. Your honest lean. "If I'm honest, I do my best thinking alone, and then I like to test it with the team."
  3. One example of each. A sentence or two for each.
  4. Link to the role. "From what you've described, this role is mostly X with regular Y, which suits me."

About 45 to 60 seconds in total.

Example answers

These are examples with made-up details. Use your own experience.

Software developer who leans solo

"Both, but I'm honest that I do my best work with long stretches of focus. Last quarter I rebuilt our search indexing on my own over three weeks, and it cut search time in half. But I wouldn't ship something like that without the team. I asked for a design review before I started, and two people caught a problem I'd missed. From what you said about focus time in the mornings and reviews in the afternoon, that rhythm suits me well."

Sales candidate who leans team

"I like a mix, but I get energy from working with people. In my last role I had my own territory and targets, which I hit for six quarters running, so I can drive myself. But the thing I enjoyed most was our Friday deal reviews, where we picked apart lost deals together. I started sharing my call notes there and two newer reps used them. This role sounds similar: your own numbers, a team that learns together."

Graduate with mostly academic experience

"I've done both. My dissertation was eight months of mostly solo work, and I had to manage my own deadlines with only monthly check-ins. I also worked in a team of five on a market research project, where I ran the survey and pulled the findings together. I think I'm strongest when I own a clear piece of work but can check in with others often."

Nurse candidate

"Nursing is team work by nature, and I like that. Good handovers and asking for a second opinion keep patients safe. At the same time, on a night shift I'm often the one making the first call when a patient's condition changes, and I'm comfortable doing that until help arrives. I'd say I'm a team player who's confident on my own when it counts."

Follow-ups to expect

  • "Tell me about a time a team project went badly."
  • "What do you do when a teammate isn't pulling their weight?" See how to answer that one.
  • "How do you stay on track when nobody's checking on you?"

Have one short story for each. For the team side, a STAR-shaped story from working well in a team works well.

Practise both examples out loud

The difficulty here is fitting two examples into a minute without rambling. Say it out loud and time it. If it runs long, cut the setup of each example to a single sentence. Speaking Coach can ask the question and then follow up with "Give me an example of the other one," which is what real interviewers do.

Questions people ask

Is it bad to say I prefer working alone?

No, if you're honest and show you collaborate well when needed. For roles with lots of solo work, such as research, development or writing, it can be a plus.

What if the job is fully remote?

Emphasise that you can work independently and manage your own time, and also how you keep in touch with a team through written updates and calls.

Should I just say whatever the job post seems to want?

Match the emphasis, but stay honest. If you claim to love constant meetings and you don't, you may end up in a job that wears you out.

What if I'm an introvert?

You can say so and still give a strong answer. Introverts often do well in teams through listening and preparation. Focus on what you do, not on the label.

How is this different from "What is your work style?"

Work style covers how you organise and approach work in general. This question is specifically about solo versus collaborative work.
