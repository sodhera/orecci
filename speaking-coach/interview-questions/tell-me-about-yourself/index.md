# Tell me about yourself: how to answer, with example answers

Answer 'Tell me about yourself' with a 60 to 90 second pitch in three parts: what you do now, the past experience that got you here, and why this role is your next step. Include only the parts of your story that matter for this job, and end on the role so the interviewer has an easy follow-up.

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence. Updated 2026-10-22. Source: https://www.orecci.com/speaking-coach/interview-questions/tell-me-about-yourself/

## What the opener is really for

"Tell me about yourself" is usually the first real question, and it does two jobs. It tells the interviewer whether you can summarise yourself clearly, and it sets the agenda for the rest of the interview. Whatever you mention, they will probably ask about next.

That second point is your advantage. If you mention the warehouse system you rolled out or the 40 students you tutored, you have chosen your next question. A good opener is less a biography and more a menu of your three best topics.

What it is not:

- Your life story from school onwards.
- A reading of your CV. They have it in front of them.
- Personal details like age, family or hometown, unless they connect to the job.

## The present, past, future structure

Three parts, in this order. Starting with the present means your strongest, most relevant point comes first, when attention is highest.

| Part | What to say | Time | Roughly |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Present | Your current role or status and one thing you do well in it | 15–20 seconds | 35–45 words |
| Past | One or two earlier experiences that built the skills this job needs, with a result | 30–45 seconds | 70–100 words |
| Future | Why this role is the logical next step, and what draws you to it | 15–20 seconds | 35–45 words |

That adds up to 60–85 seconds, or about 140–190 words at a normal speaking pace. You can check your own numbers with the [speech length calculator](/speaking-coach/tools/speech-length-calculator/).

Three sentences to build from:

1. "Right now I'm a … where I …"
2. "Before that, I … , which is where I learned … For example, …"
3. "I'm looking for … , which is why this role caught my attention: …"

## Example answers

These are examples of the structure with made-up details. Use your own facts, and say them in your own words.

### A recent graduate (applying for a trainee site engineer role)

> "I've just finished my civil engineering degree, and the part I enjoyed most was the practical side: my final-year project was a drainage redesign for a car park on campus, which the estates team is now using to plan repairs. Before that I did a ten-week summer placement with a small contractor, mostly checking measurements and keeping the daily site diary. That's where I learned how much a clean record saves when something gets disputed later. I'm applying for this trainee role because I want to be on site every day, and your programme rotates trainees through groundworks and structures, which is exactly the range I want in my first two years."

### A career changer (teacher moving into learning and development)

> "I'm currently a secondary school science teacher, and for the last two years I've also run training for new staff on our learning platform. Before teaching, I worked in a lab, so explaining technical things to people who are nervous about them has been my job in both careers. The staff training is what made me want to move: I rebuilt our induction sessions, cut them from two days to one, and the number of help requests in the first term dropped by about half. I'm applying here because this role is about designing training for adults full-time, and your onboarding programme for new technicians is close to what I've been building on the side."

### An experienced hire (finance operations manager)

> "I lead a team of six in finance operations at a logistics company, where we handle supplier payments and month-end close for three countries. I've been there five years. Before that I spent four years in audit, which is why I'm careful about controls without letting them slow everything down. The project I'm proudest of is moving our month-end close from nine working days to five, mostly by fixing how invoices are approved. I'm interested in this role because you're consolidating finance teams after your recent acquisitions, and that's the kind of messy, multi-entity work I've done well and want to do at a larger scale."

## Adjusting it for the situation

- **Phone screen with a recruiter:** keep it to 30–45 seconds. Present and future, with one line of past. They are checking fit, not depth.
- **Hiring manager:** the full 60–90 seconds, with your best example in the past section.
- **Panel interview:** same answer, but look at each person in turn, and lean into the part that matches each panel member's area if you know it.
- **Internal role:** skip what they already know about you and focus on what you have done recently that they may not have seen.

If they ask you to go through your CV in order instead, that is a different, longer answer. See [walk me through your resume](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/walk-me-through-your-resume/).

## Mistakes to avoid

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Try instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Starting with "Well, I was born in…" | The relevant part arrives after they've stopped listening | Open with what you do now |
| Listing every job | Nothing stands out, and it runs long | Choose one or two experiences that match this role |
| Only adjectives ("I'm driven and passionate") | No proof, nothing to follow up on | One result with a number or a clear outcome |
| No ending | You trail off with "…so yeah, that's me" | Finish on why this role, then stop |
| Reciting a memorised script | Sounds flat and breaks if they interrupt | Memorise the three points, not the sentences |

## How to practise it

1. Write three bullet points, one per part. No full sentences.
2. Say it out loud from the bullets five times. Each version will come out a little different, which is what you want.
3. Time one run. If it's over 90 seconds, cut the past section first.
4. Record one attempt and listen for filler words and a rushed ending. The free [interview answer review](/speaking-coach/tools/interview-answer-review/) shows your pace and fillers in the browser.
5. Practise with someone who follows up on what you said. Speaking Coach has a built-in "Tell me about yourself" scene where the AI interviewer asks about the details you mention, so you can test whether your menu of topics holds up.

## Questions people ask

### How long should my answer to "Tell me about yourself" be?

Between 60 and 90 seconds for a hiring manager, and around 30–45 seconds in a quick recruiter screen. That's roughly 140–200 words at a normal pace.

### Should I mention hobbies or personal life?

Only if they connect to the job or show a relevant skill, and keep it to one line at the end. Family, age and religion are best left out.

### What if I don't have much work experience?

Use your studies, projects, part-time jobs, volunteering or clubs as the past section. Pick the one that shows a skill the job asks for, and give it a result.

### Is "Tell me about yourself" the same as an elevator pitch?

They're close cousins. An [elevator pitch](/speaking-coach/guides/elevator-pitch/) is shorter, around 30 seconds, and built for networking. The interview version is longer and ends on why you want this specific role.

### Should I change my answer for each interview?

Keep the structure and most of the present section, but swap the past example and the future link to match each job post.
