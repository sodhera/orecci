# Why do you want to change careers? Interview answers that work

Explain the switch as a decision you made, not an escape: what drew you to the new field, the concrete steps you've already taken toward it, and two or three skills from your old career that carry straight over. Talk about what you're moving toward rather than what you're leaving, and keep it to about a minute.

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence. Updated 2026-10-22. Source: https://www.orecci.com/speaking-coach/interview-questions/why-do-you-want-to-change-careers/

## The worry behind the question

When an interviewer asks a career changer this, they're usually weighing three risks:

- **Is this a whim?** Will you get bored and switch again in a year?
- **Are you running away?** Did you hate your old job, and will the same thing happen here?
- **Can you actually do it?** What do you bring, given you haven't done this job before?

A good answer answers all three without sounding defensive. You do that with evidence: steps you've already taken, and skills you've already used.

## The four-part structure

1. **The pull (one or two sentences).** What drew you to the new field. A specific moment works best: "I first got hooked when I built our school's online course in lockdown."
2. **The proof of commitment (one or two sentences).** What you've already done: a course, a certificate, a side project, volunteering, freelance work, informational chats with people in the field.
3. **The bridge (two or three skills).** What carries over from your old career, phrased in the new field's language.
4. **The fit (one sentence).** Why this role is the right first step.

About 60 seconds, roughly 140–160 words. Notice what's missing: a long account of what was wrong with your old career. One short clause is plenty.

## Translate your skills into their language

The bridge only works if the interviewer can see the skills apply. Use the words their job post uses.

| From | To | Old-career skill | How to phrase it for the new role |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Teacher | Instructional design | Lesson planning | "Designing learning for 30 people at different levels, with measurable outcomes" |
| Nurse | Health tech or customer success | Patient education | "Explaining complex information to anxious people and checking they understood" |
| Retail manager | Operations or HR | Scheduling and hiring | "Hiring and scheduling a team of 15 around changing demand" |
| Military | Project management | Mission planning | "Planning operations with fixed deadlines, risk assessments and handovers" |
| Hospitality | Sales | Upselling and service recovery | "Reading customers quickly and turning complaints into return visits" |
| Accountant | Data analysis | Reconciliations, Excel | "Finding the story in large datasets and checking every number" |
| Journalist | Marketing or communications | Interviewing and deadlines | "Turning interviews into clear stories, on deadlines measured in hours" |

## Example answers

These are examples with made-up details. Use your own move.

### Teacher to instructional designer

> "I loved the part of teaching where I designed how people learn, and that's what I want to do full time. It started when I built our school's online course during lockdown and realised I enjoyed designing it more than delivering it. Since then I've completed a certificate in instructional design and built three sample modules, one of which a local charity now uses to train volunteers. From teaching I bring eight years of designing lessons for very different learners, and testing whether they actually learned. This role, building onboarding for new staff, is the kind of work I've been preparing for."

### Hospitality to software sales

> "I want to move into sales because the part of hospitality I was best at was reading people and turning a complaint into a loyal guest. A friend in software sales showed me what an SDR's day looks like, and it was mostly the same skills: quick conversations, handling objections, following up. Over the last six months I've done a sales fundamentals course and I've been selling events packages at my hotel, where I closed most of the inquiries I handled. I'd bring that comfort with strangers and with hearing no."

### Engineer to product management

> "I've spent five years as a software engineer, and the part I've enjoyed most is the conversations before we build anything, working out what users actually need. Over the past year I've taken that on more formally: I've led discovery for two features on my team and written their specs. I'd bring a deep understanding of how engineering teams work and what makes a spec easy to build from. I'm looking for an associate PM role where I can use that while learning the commercial side."

## Mistakes to avoid

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Try instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Leading with what you hated | Sounds like escaping, not choosing | Lead with the pull toward the new field |
| "I've always wanted to do this" | Unconvincing if you never acted on it | Name the moment and what you did about it |
| No steps taken yet | Makes the switch sound like a whim | Do one small thing before interviews: a course, a project |
| Apologising for lack of experience | Draws attention to the gap | Name what you do bring |
| Old-career jargon | They can't see the connection | Use the new field's words |
| Expecting the old salary or level | Raises a fit worry | Show you understand you may start a step down |

## Practise the story, then the doubts

Career changers usually get a second, sharper follow-up: "What if you find you miss teaching?" or "Why should we take a chance on you over someone with experience?" Prepare one calm sentence for each. For the second, the structure in [why should we hire you?](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/why-should-we-hire-you/) works well.

Practise both out loud, because the doubts are where people start to ramble. In Speaking Coach, you can add your CV and the job post, and the interviewer asks about your real background, including the switch. Setting the pressure to Tough is useful here, since a skeptical follow-up is likely.

## Questions people ask

### Should I mention I was unhappy in my old career?

One short clause at most, such as "I'd gone as far as I wanted in teaching". Spend the rest of the answer on the new field.

### Do I need a qualification before switching?

Not always, but some concrete step helps a lot: a course, a project, volunteering or freelance work. It turns "I want to" into "I've started".

### How do I handle a pay or level drop?

If asked, say you understand you may start at a more junior level and that you're focused on learning quickly. Don't raise it yourself.

### What if I'm changing careers later in life?

Lead with experience as a strength: reliability, judgement, handling people. If they hint you might be too senior, see [are you overqualified for this job?](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/are-you-overqualified-for-this-job/)

### How do I fit this into "Tell me about yourself"?

Make the switch the thread of that answer: old career in one sentence, the turning point, steps taken, this role. See [tell me about yourself](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/tell-me-about-yourself/).
