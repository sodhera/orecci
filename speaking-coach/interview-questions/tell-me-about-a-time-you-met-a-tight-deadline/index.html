Tell me about a time you met a tight deadline: answer examples

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Tell one story with real timings: how much time you had, how much work there was, and the gap between them. Then spend most of the answer on how you closed the gap: how you planned the days, what you cut or simplified, and who you agreed that with. Finish with the delivery date and one number that shows the result held up.

What a strong answer proves

Anyone can say "I worked late and got it done". That's the weakest version of this answer, because it suggests your only tool is more hours. Interviewers want to see that you can plan under pressure and make smart trade-offs:

  • You saw the size of the problem early.
  • You broke the work down and put times on it.
  • You cut or simplified something, deliberately, and agreed it with the right person.
  • You delivered something that actually worked.

Numbers carry this story. "A short deadline" means nothing; "four days to do what normally takes three weeks" means a lot.

The structure, with timings

Use STAR, but make the plan and the trade-off the centre of the action.

  1. Situation (15 seconds). The deadline and why it was tight, in numbers. "The client moved the launch forward, so we had 5 working days instead of 15."
  2. Task (one sentence). Your part. "I was responsible for all the launch emails and landing pages."
  3. Action (45–60 seconds). Three beats:
  4. Plan: how you broke the work down and scheduled it.
  5. Cut: what you dropped, simplified or postponed, and who agreed.
  6. Protect: what you did to keep quality, such as a review step you didn't skip.
  7. Result (15 seconds). Delivered when, and one measure that it worked.

Aim for about 90 seconds, roughly 200–230 words.

Scope-cutting moves you can describe

The "cut" beat is where most candidates go vague. Here are concrete moves to borrow if they're true for you:

MoveHow it sounds in an answer
Deliver the core first"We shipped the three pages customers needed on day one and added the rest the week after."
Reuse instead of building"I adapted last quarter's template instead of designing from scratch, which saved two days."
Cut the nice-to-haves"I agreed with my manager to drop the animated version and keep a static one."
Run work in parallel"I sent the copy to legal before the design was final, so both reviews happened at once."
Ask for help early"On day one I asked for one extra person for two days, rather than asking on day four."
Shorten review cycles"We swapped a two-day approval for a 30-minute call with all three approvers."

Example answers

These use made-up details to show how the numbers and trade-offs fit in.

Example answer for a marketing role

"Our biggest client moved their product launch forward by two weeks, which left us five working days to deliver a campaign we'd planned over three. I was responsible for the email series and two landing pages. On the first morning I listed every piece, estimated each one in hours, and it came to about 60 hours of work for 40 hours available. I went to my manager with two options, and we agreed to launch with three emails instead of five and send the last two the following week. I reused our approved page template and sent copy to legal on day two instead of day five, so their review ran alongside the design. We launched on the Friday as planned, and the first three emails had our best open rates of the year."

Example answer for an accountant or finance role

"During a year-end close, our team lost a senior colleague to illness a week before the reporting deadline, and I picked up her reconciliations on top of mine, about 40 extra accounts. I sorted them by size and risk, and did the ten largest first, since they covered most of the balance. For the smaller ones, I agreed with my manager to use a lighter review process we'd used before for low-risk accounts. I also asked a colleague to do a second check on the large accounts, so quality didn't slip under pressure. We filed on time, with no adjustments from the auditors on those accounts."

Example answer for a student or graduate

"In my final year, a group member dropped out two weeks before our dissertation project was due, and his section, the data analysis, was barely started. I mapped what was left: about 30 hours of analysis plus writing. I proposed we narrow the analysis to the two research questions our supervisor said mattered most and drop the third. I emailed our supervisor that day to check, and she agreed. I scheduled two hours of analysis every evening and shared results daily so the others could write as I went. We submitted a day early and got the highest grade of any group that year."

If they ask about the time you didn't make it

Many interviewers pair this with the opposite question. Have a second story ready about a deadline you missed and what you did, covered in tell me about a time you missed a deadline.

Practise the numbers out loud

Numbers are easy to write and surprisingly easy to fumble when you're speaking. Say your answer out loud and check that each number comes out clearly, without "like, around maybe". The interview answer review shows your filler words and pace, which is useful here because deadline stories tend to speed up. Questions people ask

What if my deadline story involved working long hours?

Mention it briefly if it's true, but make the plan and the trade-offs the main point. Long hours alone suggest poor planning.

Can I use a deadline from school?

Yes, especially as a graduate. Exams, dissertations and group projects work well if you can show planning and a trade-off.

What if I can't remember the exact numbers?

Use honest approximations: "about a week", "roughly 30 accounts". Don't invent precise figures you'd struggle to explain.

Should the deadline be someone else's fault?

It doesn't matter much, but don't blame. "The client moved the date" is a fact; "my manager forgot to tell us" sounds like a complaint.

How is this different from handling pressure?

The pressure question is about how you stay calm; this one is about how you plan and deliver. See how do you handle stress and pressure?
