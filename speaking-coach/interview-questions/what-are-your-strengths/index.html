What are your strengths? How to answer, with examples to use

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Pick two or three strengths the job clearly needs, name each in plain words, and back each one with a short proof: a situation and what came of it. Three strengths with proof beat ten adjectives, and the whole answer should take about a minute.

Start from the job, not from yourself

You have dozens of strengths. The interviewer only cares about the ones their job needs. So pick your strengths from the job post, then find the proof in your own history.

  1. Read the job post and underline the skills and qualities mentioned more than once, or listed first.
  2. For each, ask: when have I done this, and what happened because of it?
  3. Keep the two or three where your proof is strongest. Drop the rest, even if they're true.

Here's how that mapping looks in practice:

Job post saysStrength to nameProof to give
"Manage multiple priorities in a fast-paced team"Keeping several things moving without dropping anyA week when you ran three deadlines at once and hit all three
"Build relationships with clients"Earning trust with people who start out scepticalA client who renewed or referred others after a rocky start
"Strong attention to detail"Catching errors before they cost moneyA mistake you spotted in a report, invoice or order, and what it saved
"Explain technical concepts to non-technical users"Making complicated things simpleA guide or session you created that reduced questions
"Self-starter"Finding and fixing problems without being askedSomething you improved that wasn't in your job description

Structure: name, prove, link

For each strength:

  1. Name it in plain words. "I'm good at calming people down when something's gone wrong."
  2. Prove it in two or three sentences: one situation, what you did, the result.
  3. Link it to the role in one line. "That's a big part of this job, from what you've described."

With three strengths, that's around 20 seconds each. If they ask "What's your greatest strength?" in the singular, give one strength and a fuller proof of about 45 seconds.

Example answers

These use made-up details to show the shape. Build yours from your own work.

Customer support role

"Two things stand out. First, I stay calm with upset customers. In my current job I handle escalated billing complaints, and last quarter I closed most of them on the first call without passing them up. Second, I write clear help articles. I noticed we kept getting the same five questions about refunds, so I rewrote those articles, and those tickets dropped noticeably the next month. You mentioned your team is building a help centre, so I think both would be useful here."

Junior software developer

"I'd say I'm quick to learn a new codebase, and I'm careful with testing. On my internship I joined a project halfway through and had my first change merged in my first week, because I read the tests before the code to understand what mattered. And I added tests to a payment module that had none, which caught a rounding bug before release. Your job post mentions a large existing product, so getting up to speed fast matters."

Operations coordinator

"My main strength is keeping a lot of moving parts organised. I schedule deliveries for around 30 stores, and I built a shared tracker so drivers and store managers see changes in real time instead of calling me. Missed deliveries went down within a couple of months. I'm also good at negotiating with suppliers. When our packaging supplier raised prices, I got two quotes and used them to keep our old rate for another year."

Recent graduate with little work experience

"I'm reliable and I'm good at organising people. For two years I worked weekend shifts at a supermarket while studying full-time, and I didn't miss a shift. At university I ran the hiking society's trips, which meant booking transport and managing money for groups of 25 to 40 students. I think both matter for a trainee role where you're learning while juggling a lot."

Strength words to avoid, and what to say instead

These words aren't wrong, but everyone uses them and they prove nothing on their own.

Overused wordProblemSharper version
Hard-workingExpected of everyone"I hit every deadline last year, including during our system migration"
PassionateSounds like enthusiasm, not skillName what you do: "I read every customer review we get"
Team playerVague"I'm the one who notices when a colleague is stuck and offers to pair"
Detail-orientedUnproven claim"I caught a pricing error that would have cost us a client"
People personDoesn't say what you do with people"I'm good at getting quiet customers to tell me what's actually wrong"
PerfectionistOften heard as slow"I check my work twice before it goes to a client"

Mistakes to avoid

  • Listing six or more strengths. It sounds rehearsed and none of them stick.
  • Strengths the job doesn't need. Creativity is lovely, but not the point for a payroll role.
  • No proof. If you can't give an example, choose a different strength.
  • Overselling. "I'm the best communicator on my team" invites a challenge. Let the example do the boasting.
  • Underselling. "I guess I'm sort of organised?" Say it plainly, then prove it.

How to practise it

Write your strengths and proofs on one card, then say the answer out loud until you can do it without the card. Listen for whether the proof sounds like a real story or a list. Questions people ask

How many strengths should I mention?

Two or three. Fewer can feel thin, more sounds like a list. If the question is "your greatest strength", give one.

What's the difference between strengths and skills?

Skills are things you've learned to do, like Excel or scheduling. Strengths are how you work, like staying calm or explaining clearly. A good answer usually mixes one or two of each, all with proof.

Can I use a strength from outside work?

Yes, especially if you're early in your career. Running a club, caring for a family member or a long-term volunteer role can all prove reliability, organisation or patience.

Should my strengths match my answer to "Why should we hire you?"

They should be consistent. Your why should we hire you answer usually picks the single strongest one and ties it to the role's biggest need.
