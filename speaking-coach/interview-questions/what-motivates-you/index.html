What motivates you? How to answer, with examples by job type

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Name one or two motivators this job genuinely offers, such as solving tricky problems, helping a customer leave happier or hitting a clear target, and prove it with a short example of when that motivation made you do better work. Avoid motivators the role can't give you, and don't lead with money or perks.

What the interviewer is checking

Motivation predicts how you'll work on an ordinary Wednesday, not just your first week. The interviewer wants to know:

  • Will this job give you what drives you? If it can't, you'll lose interest.
  • Do you know yourself? A vague answer suggests you haven't thought about it.
  • Is it believable? A motivator with a story behind it sounds true.

The trick is overlap. Your answer should sit where what really drives you meets what this job offers every day.

Match the motivator to the job

Look at what the job does all day, then pick the motivator that's true for you and fits.

Motivators that can hurt you

These aren't wrong to feel, but they need care in an interview.

MotivatorWhy it's riskyHow to handle it
MoneySuggests you'll leave for a higher offerFine as a secondary point; lead with the work
Constant praise or recognitionSounds high-maintenanceTalk about seeing results, which is its own feedback
Variety (for a routine job)The job can't give you thatPick a motivator the role really has, like accuracy or helping people
Flexibility or time offSounds like you want less workMention only if the role is known for it, and not first
Fast promotionImpatient, unrealisticTalk about learning and growing responsibility
"Being my own boss"You'll leave to start a businessTalk about ownership within the role
Competition (for a team role)May clash with collaborationFrame it as hitting goals with the team

A three-part answer

  1. Name the motivator: one, or two at most.
  2. Prove it: a short story about a time it made you do better work. About 30 seconds.
  3. Link it: where this job offers the same thing.

About 45–60 seconds in all.

Example answers by job type

Made-up examples to show the structure. Use a motivator and story that are true for you.

Sales development representative

"I'm motivated by clear goals I can measure myself against. At my current job in a call centre, I started tracking my own first-call fixes every week, even though nobody asked me to, and I pushed my rate up month after month. I like knowing exactly where I stand. That's a big reason I want to move into sales development: the daily targets for calls and meetings booked are the kind of scoreboard I work well with."

Care worker

"What keeps me going is seeing someone get a bit better or a bit more independent. One of the residents I look after had stopped walking to the dining room. We worked on it a few minutes a day for a month, and now she walks there for lunch every day and chats to everyone. That kind of progress is why I do this work, and your focus on helping people stay independent is why I applied here."

Software developer

"I'm motivated by hard problems that have a real answer. Last year we had a bug that crashed the app for a small number of users, and nobody could reproduce it. I spent two days narrowing it down and found it only happened on one older phone model with a particular language setting. Fixing it felt great. This role is mostly working on a large, older codebase, and I enjoy that kind of detective work."

Warehouse operative

"I like it when everything runs right, especially on a busy day. On my current site, I'm proud that my pick accuracy has stayed near the top of the board for the past year. It's satisfying when every order goes out correct and on time. Your warehouse runs to tight same-day deadlines, and that's the pace I like working at."

Repetitive or routine jobs

If the job is mostly routine, don't say you're motivated by variety or new challenges. Pick something the routine does offer: accuracy, speed, keeping customers happy, being someone the team can rely on. For more on this, see how do you handle repetitive work.

How to practise it

Say your answer out loud and listen for whether it sounds true. Motivation answers fail when they're generic ("I'm motivated by challenges"), and the interviewer can hear the difference between a rehearsed line and a real memory. Practise the follow-up too: "What demotivates you?" Have an honest, mild answer ready, such as unclear goals. Questions people ask

Is it okay to say money motivates me?

It's honest, but don't make it your only or first answer. Mention what you enjoy about the work first; money can come up later in the salary conversation.

What if I'm motivated by stability?

That's a fine answer for many roles, especially long-term ones. Frame it as wanting to build skills and relationships somewhere for the long run.

How is this different from "What are you passionate about?"

Motivation is what keeps you working well day to day. Passion is a deeper interest, which might be inside or outside work. They can overlap, but the motivation answer should tie closely to the daily job.

What if I'm a graduate with no work experience?

Use study, part-time work, sport or volunteering for your proof story. The motivator should still be something this job offers.

What demotivates you?

Answer honestly but mildly, with something this job doesn't have much of, such as unclear priorities, and say what you do about it.
