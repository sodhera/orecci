Are you willing to relocate? How to answer if you're unsure

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Answer with your honest position in one sentence: yes, no, or yes under specific conditions. If you're unsure, say you're open to it and name the one or two things you'd need to know, such as timing or where. Ask about relocation support as a practical question about how it works, not as a demand, and save detailed negotiation for after an offer.

Why they ask

Employers ask this for one of three reasons, and it helps to know which:

Why they askWhat they need to hear
The role is in another city right nowWhether you'll move, and how soon
The role may require moving later, e.g. rotations or a new officeWhether you'd be open to it in principle
They're checking flexibility in generalThat you're not rigid, even if you prefer to stay

If you don't know which it is, ask: "Is relocation part of this role from the start, or something that might come up later?" That one question changes what a good answer looks like.

Pick your honest answer

Don't say yes just to keep the process going. If you say yes and later turn down the offer because of the move, you've wasted both sides' time and possibly burned a bridge. Decide your position before the interview.

If the answer is yes

Say it clearly and add one detail that makes it believable:

"Yes. I've been planning to move to Denver anyway, and I could be there within six weeks of an offer."

"Yes, I'm single and flexible on location, and moving for the right role is something I've expected at this stage of my career."

If the answer is "it depends"

This is the most common honest answer. Say you're open, then name your conditions in a calm, specific way:

"I'm open to it. My partner works locally, so the timing and the city matter. Could you tell me which locations you have in mind and how soon the move would be?"

"For the right role, yes. I'd need about two months to make it work, because of my lease and my kids' school term."

Naming conditions doesn't make you look less committed. It makes you look like someone who has thought it through.

If the answer is no

Say no politely, show flexibility where you genuinely can, and connect to the role:

"I'm not able to relocate right now because of family commitments. I'm comfortable with regular travel, though, up to a few days a month, and I've worked well with remote teams before."

If the role clearly requires moving, it's better to be honest now. If relocation is optional, a clear no with flexibility elsewhere often doesn't hurt you.

How to ask about relocation support

Many people worry that asking about help with moving costs will cost them the job. It won't, if you ask at the right time and in the right tone.

  1. Timing. In early interviews, ask how it works generally. Leave specific amounts until there's an offer.
  2. Frame it as logistics. "How does relocation usually work for people joining from outside the area?" sounds practical, not demanding.
  3. Ask what's typical, not what you want. Let them tell you what they usually offer.
  4. Negotiate after the offer. Once they've chosen you, it's normal to discuss moving costs, temporary housing or a later start date.
Too soon or too demandingPractical and safe
"I'd need you to pay for the move.""Do you usually offer any relocation support?"
"What's the relocation budget?" (in a first interview)"How have other people who moved for this role handled it?"
"I won't move without temporary housing.""Would there be flexibility on the start date to allow time for the move?"

Mistakes to avoid

  • Saying yes when you mean no. It catches up with you at the offer stage.
  • Over-explaining your personal life. "My partner works here" is enough; you don't need to explain the whole situation.
  • Saying "maybe" with no detail. It sounds like you haven't thought about it. Name the condition.
  • Demanding a package before an offer. It can make you seem more focused on the move than the job.
  • Forgetting to ask where. "Relocate" might mean another neighbourhood or another country.

Practise the uncertain version

The hardest version to say well is "it depends", because people tend to ramble through their whole situation. Practise saying it in two sentences: your openness, then your condition or question. Then practise the follow-up, when the interviewer asks "What would make it work for you?" Questions people ask

Will saying no cost me the job?

If moving is essential to the role, it may. If it's optional or a future possibility, a polite no with flexibility elsewhere often doesn't. Ask which it is.

Can I say yes and decide later?

You can say you're open and still decide at the offer stage, but be honest that you'd need to look at details. Don't give a firm yes you might withdraw.

When should I ask about relocation costs?

Ask generally how it works in early interviews, and discuss specifics after you have an offer. That's also when you can discuss pay and start dates together.

What if I'd relocate only to certain cities?

Say so: "I'd be happy to move to Chicago or Austin; other locations I'd need to think about." Specific is better than vague.

What if I'm moving to the area anyway?

Say it early, with a date. It removes a big concern for local roles and makes you look like a low-risk hire.
