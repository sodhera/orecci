Tell me about a time you gave difficult feedback: answer examples

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Choose a time you told a peer or someone you managed something they didn't want to hear, about work that mattered. Say why you couldn't let it go, how you prepared, the actual words you used (a specific behavior, its effect and a question), how they reacted, and what changed afterwards. Quoting your own words is what makes this answer believable.

What the interviewer is checking

Managers ask this to see whether you'll deal with problems or avoid them. People hiring for a first management role ask it most, but it also comes up for senior individual contributors, team leads and anyone who reviews others' work.

They're listening for:

  • Courage. You said it, rather than hinting or going around the person.
  • Care. You said it privately, fairly and without making it personal.
  • Specifics. You talked about behavior and its effect, not personality.
  • Follow-through. You checked in afterwards and something changed.

The detail that sets strong answers apart is the words you actually used. "I gave them constructive feedback" could mean anything. "I said, 'In the last three client calls, you joined after they'd started. It made us look unprepared. What's getting in the way?'" shows exactly how you handle it.

The feedback itself: behavior, effect, question

The simplest way to give hard feedback, and to describe it, has three parts:

  1. Behavior. What you saw, with a fact. "The last two reports went out with the wrong totals."
  2. Effect. Why it matters. "The client noticed the second time and asked for a call with our director."
  3. Question. Invite their side. "What happened on those two?"

Then agree a next step together. Interviewers like this because it's specific, fair and repeatable.

A structure for your answer

  1. Context. Who the person was (peer or report), and the problem, in two sentences.
  2. Why you had to say something. The cost of staying quiet.
  3. How you prepared. Facts you gathered, when and where you chose to talk.
  4. What you said. One or two sentences, quoted.
  5. How they reacted. Be honest: defensive, upset, relieved.
  6. What happened next. The change, and how you followed up.

Example answers

These are made-up examples. Use a real situation of your own, with names and company details left out.

Example answer: feedback to a peer

"A colleague on my team was consistently late with his part of our weekly client report, which meant I was finishing it at 9 p.m. on Thursdays. I'd covered for him four weeks in a row and it was getting worse. I asked him for coffee and said: 'For the last four Thursdays your section has come in after six, and I've been finishing the report late. I don't think you're doing it on purpose. What's going on?' He was defensive at first, then explained that his manager had moved his client calls to Thursday afternoons. We agreed he'd send his part by Wednesday evening instead. It's been on time since, and we never needed to involve our managers."

Example answer: feedback to someone you managed

"One of my newer team members was talking over customers on support calls. Two customers had mentioned it in surveys. I listened to three of her calls first so I had examples, then booked a private 20-minute slot. I said: 'You clearly know the product well. On these three calls, you started answering before the customer finished, and in two of them you solved the wrong problem. I want to work on that with you.' She went quiet, then said she was worried about call times. We agreed she'd pause and repeat the problem back before answering, and I'd review two calls a week with her. A month later her survey scores were back to the team average."

Example answer for a graduate

"In a group project, one teammate's section was copied heavily from a website. If we'd submitted it, all four of us would have been in trouble. I messaged him to meet before our next session and said: 'Your part has a lot of text that matches this page word for word. We can't submit it like that. Can we rework it together?' He was embarrassed but agreed. We rewrote it over two evenings and submitted on time."

Mistakes to avoid

MistakeWhy it hurtsTry instead
No quoted wordsSounds vague or made upSay the one or two sentences you used
Feedback about personality ("he was lazy")Sounds like a judgment, not a factDescribe what they did and its effect
Feedback given in publicShows poor judgmentChoose a story where you spoke privately
Going straight to their managerLooks like avoiding the conversationShow you talked to them first
The person "completely changed" overnightHard to believeDescribe a realistic, gradual change
Making yourself the heroSounds self-servingGive the other person credit for changing

Practice the hard sentence

Most people can tell this story, but stumble when the interviewer asks "What exactly did you say?" Practice that line out loud until it sounds natural, not rehearsed. Keep the full story to 90 seconds.

Questions people ask

Should I pick a peer or a direct report?

Pick whichever matches the job. For a management role, use a direct report. For a team member role, a peer shows you can be honest without authority over them.

What if the feedback didn't work?

You can still use it if you show what you did next fairly: more support, a clear deadline, then escalation. Be calm and factual about it.

What if I've never given difficult feedback?

Think of a time you told a teammate, classmate or volunteer that something wasn't working. Feedback to a supplier or a client also counts.

How is this different from "How do you handle criticism?"

That question is about receiving feedback. This one is about giving it. Prepare both. See how do you handle criticism.

Should I mention how I felt?

One line is fine: "I was nervous about it." It makes you sound human. Then move on to what you did.
