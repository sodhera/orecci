# Why did you choose your major? How to answer as a graduate

Give the honest reason you chose your major in one sentence, then spend most of your answer on what the degree gave you, the skills, projects or ways of thinking, and how they fit this job. If your major is unrelated, name two transferable skills with proof. If you chose it for marks or family, say so briefly and focus on what you made of it.

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence. Updated 2026-10-22. Source: https://www.orecci.com/speaking-coach/interview-questions/why-did-you-choose-your-major/

## What the question is really about

The interviewer is not grading your choice at eighteen. They are checking two things: whether you make decisions for reasons you can explain, and whether you can connect what you studied to what they need. The second part matters more.

So the answer has a simple balance: about a quarter on why you chose it, three quarters on what you got from it and how it applies here.

## A three-part answer

1. **The reason.** One or two honest sentences. "I chose economics because I liked understanding why people make the decisions they do."
2. **What it gave you.** Two specific things: a skill, a course, a project, a way of thinking. Name them.
3. **The link.** One sentence on how those things fit this job. "That's most of what a pricing analyst does every day."

Keep it to 45 to 60 seconds, about 100 to 150 words when spoken.

## If your major matches the job

This is the easy case, which is why candidates often waste it with "Because I've always been interested in computers." Since every candidate in your batch has the same degree, go one level more specific:

- The area within the major you went deepest in.
- A project or elective that shows that depth.
- Why that area matters to this employer.

### Example: computer science for a backend role

> "I picked computer science because I'd built small websites at school and wanted to understand what happened behind them. Within the degree I went deepest on databases and distributed systems. My final-year project was a caching layer for a slow student portal, which cut page loads from about four seconds to under one in our tests. Your team runs high-traffic APIs, so that's the part of my degree I'd use most."

## If your major is unrelated

This is where the question earns its place. Don't apologise for your degree. Translate it.

| Major | Transferable skill | How to prove it in one line |
| --- | --- | --- |
| History | Research, weighing conflicting sources | "My dissertation compared 40 primary sources that disagreed with each other." |
| English or literature | Clear writing, argument | "I wrote a long essay every two weeks and had to defend it in seminars." |
| Psychology | Understanding behaviour, basic statistics | "I ran a survey study with 120 participants and analysed it myself." |
| Biology or chemistry | Careful method, data handling | "Lab work taught me to record everything and repeat tests before trusting a result." |
| Philosophy | Logic, structured reasoning | "Most of my degree was taking an argument apart and finding the weak step." |
| Fine arts or design | Taking criticism, iterating fast | "Weekly critiques meant reworking a piece five or six times." |

Pick the two that fit the job and add why you're now moving toward this field.

### Example: history graduate for a business analyst role

> "I chose history because I loved working out what really happened when the sources disagreed. That turned out to be good training for analysis. In my dissertation I worked through dozens of conflicting records, and I learned to build a case from messy evidence. In my last year I took an evening course in SQL and found I enjoyed doing the same thing with data. That combination of research and numbers is what this role asks for."

## If you didn't really choose it

In many places, including India, plenty of students pick a stream based on entrance exam ranks, marks cut-offs or family advice. Interviewers know this. Don't invent a passion you didn't have. Say it plainly, then show what you did with it.

### Example: engineering chosen for the rank

> "Honestly, I got into mechanical engineering based on my entrance rank, and I wasn't sure about it in first year. In second year we did a manufacturing project, and I found I really liked the process side: planning, timing, cost. I took electives in operations and did my internship in a plant's production planning team. That's why I'm applying for this supply chain role rather than a design role."

This answer is strong because it shows you took a choice that wasn't fully yours and steered it somewhere deliberate.

## If you changed your major

Treat the switch as a decision you made with information. Say what you learned from the first major that made you switch, and what you kept from it. One sentence on the first, the rest on the second.

## Mistakes to avoid

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Try instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "I've always been passionate about it" | Vague, and everyone says it | One real moment or reason |
| "My parents chose it" and stopping there | Sounds like you take no ownership | Say what you made of it afterwards |
| Apologising for an unrelated degree | Tells them it is a weakness | Translate it into two skills with proof |
| Listing every course | Long and unfocused | Two specific things that matter to this job |
| Criticising your college or professors | Sounds negative | Keep the focus on what you did |

## Practise the link out loud

The link sentence is the one people stumble over, because they have never said it. Say your answer out loud and listen for whether the connection to the job is clear in one sentence. If an interviewer pushes with "But why not work in history?", have a calm answer ready. ## Questions people ask

### What if I chose my major for money or job prospects?

That is a reasonable reason. Say it briefly ("I wanted a degree with clear job options"), then show what you came to enjoy in it.

### How long should my answer be?

About a minute. One sentence on the choice, the rest on what it gave you and how it fits.

### Should I mention my grades?

Only if they help, such as a strong grade in the most relevant course. If they ask about grades, see [how to answer questions about a low GPA](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/why-is-your-gpa-low/).

### What if I regret my major?

Don't say you regret it. Say what you learned that led you to this field instead, which shows the same honesty without sounding negative.

### Is a double major or minor worth mentioning?

Yes, if it supports this job. A minor in statistics can matter more to a data role than your main major.
