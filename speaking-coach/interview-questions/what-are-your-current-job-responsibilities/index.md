# What are your current job responsibilities? How to answer

Start with a one-line summary of what your role exists to do, then describe your two or three main responsibilities by what they achieve, each with a detail or number. Finish with one recent result and a sentence on how it connects to the new job. Aim for about a minute, and choose the responsibilities that match the role you're applying for.

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence. Updated 2026-10-22. Source: https://www.orecci.com/speaking-coach/interview-questions/what-are-your-current-job-responsibilities/

## What the interviewer is checking

They can read your job title. They're asking this to find out three things:

- **What you actually do day to day,** because job titles mean different things at different companies.
- **How big your scope is.** How many people, customers, accounts, systems or how much budget.
- **Whether you understand why your work matters,** not just what the tasks are.

The most common weak answer is reading the job description aloud: "I'm responsible for managing the inbox, scheduling meetings, preparing reports, liaising with stakeholders..." It's accurate and forgettable.

## Task list versus impact

The fix is to describe each responsibility by what it achieves.

| Task-list version | Impact version |
| --- | --- |
| "I handle customer complaints." | "I'm the escalation point for complaints the first line can't solve, about 30 a week, and I aim to close each within two days." |
| "I prepare monthly reports." | "I produce the monthly sales report the leadership team uses to set targets, so accuracy really matters." |
| "I manage social media." | "I run our three social channels, with a focus on turning followers into newsletter sign-ups." |
| "I supervise the evening shift." | "I run the evening shift of eight people, so I'm responsible for staffing, safety and hitting our close-out times." |
| "I write code for the payments team." | "I build and maintain the part of our system that processes refunds, which handles thousands of transactions a day." |

Same job, same tasks. The second column tells the interviewer what you're trusted with.

## A one-minute structure

1. **One-line summary.** What your role exists to do. "I'm a project coordinator in a construction firm, and my job is to keep three to four building projects on schedule and on budget."
2. **Two or three main responsibilities,** in impact form, most relevant to the new job first.
3. **One recent result.** "Last quarter I caught a supplier delay early enough to reorder, which saved about two weeks on one site."
4. **The link.** "That's the part of my role I'd do more of here."

That's about 130 to 160 words, roughly a minute at a normal pace. If you go past 90 seconds, you're listing.

## Example answers

These are examples with made-up details. Use your own role.

### Operations analyst

> "I'm an operations analyst at a regional logistics company. My job is to find where we're losing time or money in our delivery network. Day to day, that's three things. I build the weekly performance dashboard for our 12 depots. I investigate any depot that falls behind its targets. And I work with depot managers to test fixes. Last quarter I found that one depot's late deliveries were mostly from one loading bay layout, and the change we made cut late deliveries there noticeably within a month. That analysis-to-action loop is what I'd like to do at a bigger scale with you."

### Retail supervisor

> "I'm a shift supervisor at a supermarket, running the floor on weekday evenings with a team of about ten. My main responsibilities are making sure the shift is staffed and covered, handling customer issues that the team escalates, and closing the store safely with the cash reconciled. I also train new starters, and I've trained six this year. I'm applying for this assistant manager role because I want to take on the planning side, not just the shift."

### Junior software developer

> "I'm a developer on the checkout team of an online retailer. I own the code for discount codes and gift cards, which means building new features, fixing bugs, and being on call one week a month. I also review code for two newer developers. Recently I rebuilt how we validate discount codes, which cut a type of checkout error that used to generate a lot of support tickets. I'd like to bring that ownership mindset to your payments team."

## Choose what to include

You do more than you can describe in a minute. Pick based on the job you're applying for.

1. Read the new job's description and list its main responsibilities.
2. Find the overlap with what you do now.
3. Lead with the overlap, even if it's not the biggest part of your current job.
4. Leave out duties that have nothing to do with the new role, unless they show scope.

This is the same skill as [walking through your resume](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/walk-me-through-your-resume/), zoomed in on one job.

## Mistakes to avoid

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Try instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reading your job description | Long and forgettable | Two or three responsibilities by impact |
| Taking credit for the team's work | Follow-ups will expose it | "I" for your part, "we" for the team |
| Sharing confidential details | Makes them wonder what you'd share about them | Use rough sizes, not client names or internal figures |
| Complaining about your workload | Sounds negative | Describe scope neutrally |
| Going past two minutes | They lose the thread | Stop after the link; let them ask |
| Using internal jargon | The interviewer may not know your company's terms | Say it in plain words |

## Practise to stay under a minute

Most people underestimate how long this answer takes, because there's always one more duty to mention. Say it out loud with a timer. If it runs long, cut whole responsibilities rather than squeezing them in faster. The [guide to rambling less](/speaking-coach/guides/how-to-stop-rambling/) has more on trimming. Speaking Coach's interviewer can read your CV and the job post you add, ask the question, and afterwards show your pace and quote the part where you started listing.

## Questions people ask

### How long should my answer be?

About a minute. Long enough for two or three responsibilities and one result.

### What if my job title doesn't match what I really do?

Explain briefly: "My title is coordinator, but in practice I manage the project budget and the client relationship." Interviewers appreciate the clarity.

### What if I'm unemployed right now?

Describe your most recent role in the past tense with the same structure. If you've been doing something else, such as a course or freelance work, mention it briefly at the end.

### Should I mention responsibilities I don't enjoy?

Only if they're relevant to the new role. If the new job needs them too, mention them neutrally.

### Can I use numbers if they're confidential?

Use rough sizes or percentages instead of exact internal figures: "a team of about ten" or "a budget in the low millions."
