Why do you want this internship? How to answer, with examples

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Give three short parts: what specifically you want to learn, why this company or team is the place to learn it, and what you can contribute in the weeks you'll be there. Be concrete about each. 'To gain experience' is true for every applicant; naming the team's work, a skill you'll build and a task you could take on is what makes it yours.

What the interviewer is weighing

An internship is short. The interviewer knows you'll be learning most of the time, so they aren't expecting a finished professional. They're asking themselves:

  • Will this intern use the time well? Someone who knows what they want to learn gets more done.
  • Why us? Did you apply to fifty places with the same letter, or do you know what this team does?
  • Will they help, or just need help? Even a small contribution matters in a team of busy people.

The internship answer is different from a job answer because both sides know it's a short trade: your effort and fresh eyes for their teaching and experience. Say that trade clearly.

The three-part answer

  1. What you want to learn. One or two specific skills or areas, not "experience."
  2. Why here. Something real about this company, team or the work they do.
  3. What you bring. A skill, a tool or a habit you can put to use in the first weeks.

About 45 to 60 seconds in total.

PartWeak versionStrong version
Learn"I want to gain industry experience.""I want to learn how A/B tests are designed and read in a real product team."
Why here"You're a leading company.""Your growth team runs experiments on the checkout flow, which is exactly what I want to understand."
Contribute"I'm a hard worker.""I'm comfortable in SQL and Python, so I could take on data pulls and dashboards from week one."

Example answers

These are examples with made-up details. Research the real company before you adapt them.

Data analytics internship

"I want to learn how analysis turns into decisions in a real business, not just in coursework. I've read your team's blog posts on how you test pricing changes, and that's exactly the kind of work I want to see up close. In return, I'm comfortable with SQL and Python from two college projects, so I could take on data pulls and cleaning from the first week and free up time for the analysts."

Marketing internship at a small brand

"I want to learn how a small team runs campaigns across channels with a limited budget. At a big company I'd see one slice. Here I'd see how email, social and partnerships fit together. I've run our college society's Instagram for a year, growing it from about 400 to 2,500 followers, so I can help with content and reporting while I learn the rest."

Software engineering internship

"Two things. I want to work in a codebase with real users and real code reviews, which I can't get from college projects. And your team works on payments, which interests me because mistakes are expensive and the testing has to be careful. I've built a few full-stack projects with React and Node, so I can pick up smaller tickets early and learn your review process on real work."

Unpaid or nonprofit internship

"I want to understand how a nonprofit measures whether its programmes actually work. Your annual report described the reading programme's results in detail, and I want to learn how that data is gathered and used. I'm good with spreadsheets and survey tools, so I could help with the data side during the evaluation season."

If you're hoping for a full-time offer

In many internships, especially campus ones in India where a pre-placement offer (PPO) can follow a good stint, candidates want a job at the end. It's fine to show you'd like to stay, but don't make it the main reason. Learning and contributing come first; the offer follows from those.

"And if it goes well, I'd love to be considered for a full-time role. But my main goal for these eight weeks is to learn the work properly and be useful to the team."

How to find your "why here"

  1. Read the internship description and underline the team's actual work.
  2. Look at the company's website, blog, recent news or product.
  3. If you can, talk to a past intern. Ask what interns actually did.
  4. Pick one detail that connects to what you want to learn.

Fifteen minutes of research turns a generic answer into a specific one. See what to say when they ask what you know about the company for a research checklist.

Say it, don't just write it

Internship answers often come out rushed, because students try to fit everything in. Say yours out loud and time it; if it runs over a minute, cut the background. Questions people ask

Is it okay to say I want to explore whether this career is right for me?

Yes, if you frame it positively: "I want to see this work from the inside before I choose my specialisation." Avoid sounding like you'd be unsure the whole time.

How long should my answer be?

About 45 to 60 seconds. Three parts, each in two or three sentences.

What if I applied to many internships?

That's normal and they know it. You still need a specific reason for this one. Pick one detail about their work that genuinely interests you.

Should I mention the stipend?

Not in this answer. If pay matters to your decision, ask about it later or with the recruiter.

What if I don't have relevant skills yet?

Offer what you do have: reliability, quick learning with an example, a tool you know, or fresh perspective as a user of their product.
