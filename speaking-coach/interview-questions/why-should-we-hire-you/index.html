Why should we hire you? How to answer, with examples

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Answer "Why should we hire you?" in three parts: name the one or two things the job needs most, prove you have them with a short real example, and say what you would do with them in this role. Keep it under a minute and talk about the employer's needs, not your wish for the job.

What the interviewer is listening for

This question usually comes near the end. By then the interviewer has heard your background, so they are not asking for your CV again. They want to know three things:

  • Do you understand the job? Can you name what matters most in this role, in their words?
  • Can you prove it? Is there one concrete example behind your claim, or only adjectives like "hard-working" and "passionate"?
  • Will you sound sure of yourself without overselling? A calm, specific answer beats a long list.

A weak answer talks about what the job would do for you ("it would be a great opportunity to grow"). A strong one talks about what you would do for them.

A simple structure

  1. The need. "From the job post and what you've said today, the role needs someone who can X and Y."
  2. The proof. One short example: what the situation was, what you did, and the result. Use a number if you have a real one.
  3. The link. "That's what I'd bring here, starting with …" Name one thing you would do in your first months.

Aim for 45–60 seconds. That is roughly 100–150 words spoken at a normal pace. You can check your timing with the speech length calculator.

Example answers

These are examples of the structure, not scripts to memorise. Swap in your own facts.

A recent graduate

"The role needs someone who can turn messy information into clear reports, and who learns new tools fast. In my final-year project I took survey data from 300 students, cleaned it in Excel, and presented the findings to the student union, who changed their opening hours because of it. I'd bring that same habit of checking the numbers and explaining them simply to your weekly reporting."

A career changer

"You need someone who can calm upset customers and fix the problem on the first call. I spent five years as a nurse, where staying calm with worried people was the job. When I moved into a support role last year, I kept my first-call resolution above the team average within three months. I'd bring that patience, plus the medical knowledge your health clients ask about."

An experienced hire

"This team needs someone who has scaled a sales process before, not just worked inside one. At my last company I rebuilt our pipeline reviews and onboarding for new reps, and we went from four reps to twelve without the close rate dropping. I'd start by sitting in on your current deals for a month, then fix the two stages where deals stall most."

Mistakes to avoid

MistakeWhy it hurtsTry instead
"I'm a hard worker and a team player"Everyone says it, and nothing proves itOne example that shows the trait in action
Reciting your whole CVThey have already read itPick the one or two strengths this job needs
"I really need this job"It's about you, not themTalk about the problems you would solve
Comparing yourself to other candidatesYou don't know them, and it sounds defensiveTalk only about what you bring
Going past two minutesAttention drops and the point gets lostStop after the link; let them ask more

How to prepare in 15 minutes

  1. Read the job post and underline the two requirements that appear most or come first.
  2. For each, write one real example in three lines: situation, what you did, result.
  3. Pick the stronger example and say the whole answer out loud three times. Writing it is not the same as saying it.
  4. Time yourself. If it runs over a minute, cut the background, not the result.
  5. Practice once with someone, or an AI partner, who asks a follow-up such as "What would you do differently?"

Questions people ask

How long should my answer be?

About 45 to 60 seconds. Long enough for one real example, short enough that the interviewer can follow up on it.

Is it okay to say I'm the best candidate?

Avoid it. You don't know the other candidates, and it can sound arrogant. Show why you fit the role instead and let them draw the conclusion.

What if I don't meet every requirement?

Lead with the requirements you do meet, then mention briefly how you are closing the gap, for example a course you are taking. Don't apologise for the gap.

Can I use the same answer for every interview?

Keep the structure, but change the need and the example for each job. An answer that clearly fits their role is what makes it work.

Should I memorise my answer?

Memorise the three points, not the sentences. A memorised script sounds flat and falls apart if they interrupt you.
