What are you passionate about? How to answer in an interview

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Name one thing you genuinely care about and spend time on, show it with a specific detail, and then connect it to a quality you bring to work, such as persistence, curiosity or care for people. The passion doesn't have to be the job itself. A real interest told with a concrete detail beats a forced claim that you're passionate about spreadsheets.

Why interviewers ask it

This question is mostly about energy and honesty. When people talk about something they really care about, they speak faster, give more detail and sound more like themselves. The interviewer wants to see that version of you, and to learn what drives you when no one is making you do it.

It's also a small test of judgement. Can you share something personal without oversharing, and can you show how it relates to work without twisting it into a sales pitch?

Pick a genuine passion

Your passion can sit in one of three places. All three work if you connect them well.

Where it sitsExampleHow it links to work
The work itselfMaking confusing things simple; fixing broken processesDirect: "which is most of this job"
Something next to the workTeaching, mentoring, writing, data, designShows a skill you bring
Outside workLong-distance running, cooking, restoring furniture, a sports team you coachShows a quality: discipline, patience, care

Avoid passions that are controversial at work (politics, religion), risky to mention (gambling), or impossible to talk about for more than a sentence. And don't pick something you can't answer a follow-up about. If you say "reading" and they ask what you read last, you need an answer; see what is the last book you read?

The answer in three steps

  1. Name it. One passion, not a list. "I'm really into restoring old furniture."
  2. Show it with a detail. What you actually do, how often, something specific. "I've rebuilt six chairs from flea markets, and I'm halfway through a 1960s sideboard."
  3. Connect it to how you work. One sentence, lightly. "It's taught me patience with fiddly problems, which is a lot of what debugging is."

That's 30–45 seconds. The detail in step 2 is what makes it believable; the connection in step 3 is what makes it relevant.

Example answers

These are examples with made-up details. Use your real interest.

A passion for the work itself (customer support)

"I'm passionate about making things simple for people who are frustrated. In my current job I rewrote our five most-used help articles because customers kept calling about the same steps, and calls on those topics dropped noticeably over the next month. Honestly, the best part of my week is when someone says 'oh, that's it?' That's a big part of why I'm applying for a support role."

A passion next to the work (teaching, for an engineer)

"I love teaching. On Saturdays I volunteer at a coding club for twelve-year-olds, and watching someone get their first program running never gets old. It's made me better at work too, because explaining something to a twelve-year-old forces you to understand it properly. I'm usually the person on my team who writes the setup guide for new joiners."

A passion outside work (running, for a project role)

"I'm passionate about distance running. I've done three half marathons and I'm training for my first full one in the spring. What I like is the planning: you can't cram for a marathon, you build up week by week and adjust when something goes wrong. I think that's why I enjoy project work, where the plan only works if you keep checking it."

A graduate with limited work experience

"I'm passionate about local history. I started a small blog in my second year about the buildings in my town, and it now gets messages from people who used to live there. It's made me good at research and at turning a lot of material into a short, readable piece, which is what I'd love to do in a communications role."

Mistakes to avoid

MistakeWhy it hurtsTry instead
"I'm passionate about [exact job duty]" with no proofSounds forced and obviousShow something you did unprompted
Listing four passionsNothing landsPick one and go deeper
No detail ("I like travel")Generic; every second candidate says itName a place, a habit, a project
Forcing a link ("baking taught me spreadsheets")Sounds like a stretchLink to a quality, not a task
A passion that suggests you'll leavee.g. "my real dream is my startup"Choose one that fits alongside the job
Over five minutes on your hobbyLoses the thread30–45 seconds, then stop

Let the energy show

This is one of the few interview questions where it's fine to smile and speak a bit faster. Many people flatten their voice on every answer because they're nervous, so a real passion ends up sounding as dull as a job title. Record yourself telling a friend about your passion, then record your interview answer. If the second one sounds flatter, bring back some of the first. The speech review tool shows your pace and pauses, and in Speaking Coach the interviewer asks a follow-up about the detail you gave, which is a good test of whether it's real.

Questions people ask

Do I have to be passionate about the job?

No. Interviewers know most people aren't passionate about invoices. Pick something real and connect it to a quality you bring.

Is "What are you passionate about?" the same as "What motivates you?"

Close, but not the same. Passion is what you love doing; motivation is what makes you work hard. See what motivates you?

Can I say my family?

You can, but it's hard to add detail or link it to work without oversharing. A specific activity usually works better.

What if I don't have a passion?

Look for something you choose to spend time on: a show you analyse, a game you're good at, a skill you're learning. "Interest" is enough; it doesn't have to be dramatic.

Should I mention a side business?

Be careful. It can be a strength, but the interviewer may worry about your time and focus. Say how it fits alongside a full-time job if you mention it.
