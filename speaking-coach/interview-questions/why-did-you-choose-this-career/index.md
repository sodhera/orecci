# Why did you choose this career? How to answer with a real story

Tell a short, true origin story in four beats: the moment you first got interested, how you tested that interest, what keeps you in the field now, and where you want to take it next. Keep it to about a minute, make the first moment specific, and end on the future, so the story points toward this job.

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence. Updated 2026-10-22. Source: https://www.orecci.com/speaking-coach/interview-questions/why-did-you-choose-this-career/

## What the interviewer is really asking

They want to know if this career is a choice you'd make again. People who chose their field, even late or by accident, tend to stay in it, keep learning and handle the dull parts better. So the question is less "how did you get here?" and more "will you still care about this work in two years?"

A good answer does three things: it is true, it has one specific moment in it, and it ends pointing forward.

## The four-beat origin story

| Beat | What it covers | Length |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. The spark | The first moment you noticed the work. A person, a problem, a task | 1–2 sentences |
| 2. The test | How you checked it was real: a course, a job, a project | 1–2 sentences |
| 3. What keeps you | The part of the work you still enjoy, in concrete terms | 1–2 sentences |
| 4. Where next | The direction you want to grow, which this role fits | 1 sentence |

Total: 45 to 75 seconds, about 100 to 170 spoken words. The spark gets the most detail. Beat 4 is the one people forget, and it is the one that connects the story to the job.

## Example answers by field

These are examples with made-up details. Swap in your own moments.

### Nursing

> "When I was sixteen my grandmother was in hospital for three weeks, and I noticed the nurse who explained everything to her in plain words was the person she trusted most. I did a care assistant job the summer before university to check it wasn't just a feeling, and I loved the pace of it. What keeps me in nursing is that mix of clinical detail and talking people through scary moments. Next I want to specialise in cardiac care, which is why this ward appeals to me."

### Software engineering

> "I got into it by accident. I was doing data entry in a shipping office and wrote a small spreadsheet macro to save myself two hours a day. My manager asked me to do it for the whole team. I took an evening course to see if I could do more than macros, then moved into a junior developer role. I still love the moment something slow and annoying becomes automatic. I want to work on internal tools, which is most of what this team builds."

### Accounting

> "Honestly, I picked commerce at school because I was good at maths, not because I had a plan. The real moment came during my articleship, when I found a reconciling error that had been carried for three years. Tracing it back felt like solving a puzzle. What keeps me here is that the numbers always tell a story if you're patient. I want to move into audit for manufacturing clients, which is a big part of this practice."

### Teaching

> "I coached an under-twelve football team while I was at university, and I noticed I cared more about the kid who couldn't pass than about winning. I did a term of classroom volunteering to see if that carried over to maths, and it did. What keeps me in teaching is the moment a student explains something back to me in their own words. I'd like to take on a mentoring role for new teachers within a few years."

## If you fell into your career

Many people did, and pretending otherwise usually sounds false. You can say it honestly and still give a strong answer: name the accident briefly, then spend your time on the moment you decided to stay.

> "I didn't plan to work in recruitment. I took a temporary job after university to pay rent. About six months in, I placed a candidate who'd been out of work for a year, and she called to say her family could finally relax. That's when I decided this was my career, not a stopgap."

The decision to stay is your real origin story.

## Mistakes to avoid

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Try instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "It pays well" or "it's stable" as the whole reason | True for many, but says nothing about the work | Mention it if you like, then talk about the work itself |
| "My parents wanted me to" | Sounds like you had no choice and may not stay | Say what you found in it once you started |
| Starting from childhood and walking through every year | Long, and the point arrives too late | Start at the spark, skip the timeline |
| "I've always been passionate about..." | Vague and very common | One concrete moment instead of the word passion |
| No beat 4 | The story ends in the past | Finish with where you want to go next |
| A story that doesn't match the CV | Interviewers notice | Check your dates and steps line up |

## How this differs from similar questions

- "[Why do you want this job?](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/why-do-you-want-this-job/)" is about this role and company. This question is about the field.
- "[Why did you choose your major?](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/why-did-you-choose-your-major/)" is the student version, about a degree decision.
- "[Why do you want to change careers?](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/why-do-you-want-to-change-careers/)" is for when you are leaving one field for another.

You can reuse the same spark across these, but each needs a different ending.

## Practise it as a story, not a script

Origin stories sound best when you know the beats but not the exact words. Say yours out loud three or four times, each time a little differently. Then try it with someone who interrupts with "What made you stay?" Speaking Coach can play that interviewer and, afterwards, quote the line where your story lost its thread, with a suggestion for what to try instead.

## Questions people ask

### Can I say I chose it for the money?

You can mention it, briefly and honestly, but don't make it the whole answer. Follow with what you have come to value in the work itself.

### What if my reason sounds boring?

Most reasons do in summary. The fix is detail: a specific day, task or person. "I liked numbers" is boring. "I found a three-year-old error during my first month" is not.

### How long should the answer be?

About a minute. If your spark takes longer than 20 seconds to tell, trim it.

### Is it okay to talk about a family member's influence?

Yes, as the spark. Then show that you tested the choice yourself, so it sounds like your decision, not theirs.

### What if I'm not sure I'll stay in this career forever?

You don't need to promise forever. Say what you want to learn or achieve in the next few years in this field. That is honest and enough.
