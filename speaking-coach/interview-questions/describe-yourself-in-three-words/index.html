Why such a short question matters

"Describe yourself in three words" sounds like a quick icebreaker, but it tests two things: whether you know what the job needs, and whether you can be concise under pressure. Three well-chosen words with proof sound prepared and self-aware. Three generic words sound like everyone else.

How to choose your three words

  1. Start with the job post. List the qualities it mentions or clearly implies.
  2. Cover three angles. One word about how you work (thorough, quick), one about people (patient, approachable), and one about results or attitude (reliable, persistent).
  3. Check each word has proof. If you can't name a moment that shows it, swap the word.
  4. Choose plain words. "Steady" is easier to believe than "indefatigable".

A simple structure

"[Word one], [word two] and [word three]. [One sentence of proof for each.]"

Say the three words first, pause briefly, then prove each one in order. Proving them in the same order you said them makes the answer easy to follow. Keep it to about 30 seconds, roughly 60–80 words.

Example answers

Made-up examples to show the structure. Choose words and proof that are true for you.

Retail supervisor

"Approachable, organised and calm. Approachable, because new starters come to me first with questions, even when the manager's around. Organised, because I built the weekend rota template the shop still uses. And calm, because on our busiest Saturday before Christmas, when a till went down, I opened the spare and moved the queue without anyone waiting more than a few minutes."

Data analyst

"Curious, thorough and clear. Curious, because when sales dipped in one region I kept digging until I found a pricing error in one store's system. Thorough, because I check every report against the source before it goes out. And clear, because the regional managers tell me my summaries are the only ones they actually read."

Nurse

"Attentive, calm and dependable. Attentive, because I notice small changes, like a patient who's suddenly quieter than usual, and I check them. Calm, because I'm often the one other nurses call when a family member is upset. And dependable, because in three years on the ward I've never left a handover incomplete."

Graduate

"Reliable, curious and practical. Reliable, because I worked weekends all through university without missing a shift. Curious, because I took an extra statistics module just because I wanted to understand my dissertation data better. And practical, because I like turning ideas into something that works, like the booking system I set up for my running club."

Variations you might hear

  • "One word to describe you?" Pick your strongest word and give one sentence of proof.
  • "Three words your friends would use?" Same method, but the proof can come from outside work. Keep it relevant.
  • "Three words your manager would use?" Choose words a manager would really say; a reference check could confirm them. See how would your coworkers describe you for that angle.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Three synonyms. "Hard-working, dedicated and committed" says one thing three times. Cover three angles.
  • Words without proof. Three adjectives alone take five seconds and leave the interviewer with nothing.
  • Jokes or quirky words. "Caffeinated" might get a smile, but follow it with a real answer.
  • A long answer. This question is a test of brevity. Stay under 40 seconds.

How to practise it

Say the three words out loud first, by themselves. If you hesitate between words, you haven't decided yet. Then add the proof sentences and time the whole thing. Questions people ask

What are good words to describe yourself in an interview?

Specific, provable words that fit the job, such as reliable, thorough, approachable, calm, curious, persistent or organised. Avoid generic words like hard-working or passionate unless you add strong proof.

Do I need to explain each word?

Yes. One sentence of proof per word turns a list into evidence. Without it, the answer feels unfinished.

Can I use the same three words for every job?

Keep one or two that are core to you, but swap at least one to match each role. A nurse and a data analyst need different strengths.

Is it okay to use a word that sounds like a weakness?

Only if you frame it well. "Direct" can work if your proof shows it helping, such as raising a problem early that saved a project.
