If we offered you the job, would you accept? How to answer it

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Say you're very interested and give one specific reason this role appeals, then say you'd want to review the full written offer before saying a final yes. If you have other processes or a key question still open, mention it briefly and honestly. That answer shows commitment without giving up your chance to look at pay and terms.

Why they ask it, usually late

This question tends to come in a final round or near the end of an interview that's gone well. The interviewer may be close to offering you the job and wants to know:

  • Are you serious? They don't want to spend a week on an offer you'll turn down.
  • Is anything in the way? Salary, start date, another offer, a worry about the role.
  • How do you respond under a direct question? It's a small test of how you handle pressure.

It isn't a binding question, and they don't expect you to sign anything. But your answer shapes how they approach the offer.

The two traps

AnswerWhat the interviewer hearsWhat it costs you
"Yes, absolutely, right now!"Keen, but maybe desperateWeaker position if the offer is low
"I'd have to think about it."Unsure, possibly not interestedThey may move to another candidate
"I'm very interested, and I'd want to see the full offer."Serious, sensibleNothing, and it keeps you strong

The aim is the third row: clearly enthusiastic, and still clearly thinking.

A three-part answer

  1. Enthusiasm with a reason. "Yes, I'm very interested. After today, the part that excites me most is…" Be specific to this interview.
  2. The reasonable condition. "I'd want to look at the full offer, including salary and benefits, before saying a final yes."
  3. Anything open, briefly. Another process, a start date, or one question you still have. Say it honestly so it doesn't come up later as a surprise.

About 20–30 seconds. Then stop and let them respond.

Example answers for different situations

These are example answers. Adapt them to what's true for you.

You're keen and have no other offers

"Yes, I'm very interested. Hearing about the new clinic opening next year made me even more keen, because that's the kind of setup I want to help build. I'd want to see the full offer before I give a final answer, but I don't expect anything to stop me."

You're in other processes

"I'm very interested. This is one of the roles I'm most excited about because of how closely I'd work with customers. I'll be honest that I'm in the late stages with one other company, so I'd want to review your offer carefully when it comes. If it reflects the role, I'd be glad to move quickly."

If you're asked about other companies directly, see are you interviewing with other companies.

You have one real concern

"I'm very interested. The team and the projects are a great fit. The one thing I'd want to understand better is the on-call schedule. If that works out, and the offer's in the right range, I'd be very happy to accept."

Raising a real concern now is better than declining later. It gives them a chance to answer it.

You're a graduate with a campus offer process

"Yes, I'd be very happy to join. The training programme was one of the main reasons I applied. I'd just like to read through the offer letter before confirming, especially the location and joining date."

You genuinely aren't sure

"I'm interested, and today has answered a lot of my questions. I still want to understand how the role's targets are set, because that will matter for my decision. Could we talk about that before an offer?"

Only use this if it's true. It's better than a false yes followed by a refusal.

What to say if they make an offer on the spot

Occasionally an interviewer moves from the question to an actual verbal offer. You can still ask for time:

"Thank you, I'm really pleased. Could you send the details in writing? I'd like to look them over, and I can get back to you by [a specific day]."

Give a specific, short time frame, such as one or two working days. It shows respect for their process. If the offer is lower than you hoped, the page on the lowest salary you'd accept explains how to keep negotiation open.

Mistakes to avoid

MistakeWhy it hurtsInstead
A flat yes with no conditionsWeakens your position on pay and termsAdd "once I've seen the full offer"
A long pause, then "maybe"Sounds uninterestedLead with enthusiasm, then the condition
Hiding another offerCan surprise them later and damage trustMention it briefly and honestly
Making demands nowSounds like you're negotiating before an offerSave numbers for the written offer
Saying yes when you'd declineWastes their time and burns a bridgeBe honest about real concerns

Saying it with confidence

This question often catches people off guard at the end of a long interview, when energy is low. A quick, warm answer said without hesitation sounds far more committed than the same words after a long "um". Practise it out loud, especially the condition, so "I'd want to see the full offer" sounds natural and not like a retreat.

Questions people ask

Is saying yes binding?

No. It's a statement of interest, not a contract. Still, treat it seriously: don't say an enthusiastic yes if you already know you'll decline.

Should I mention salary in my answer?

Keep it light: "I'd want to see the full offer, including salary." Save specific numbers for when you have the written offer.

What if this isn't my first-choice company?

You don't have to rank companies aloud. Say what you genuinely like about this role and that you'd review the offer carefully. That's honest without being unkind.

How long can I take to decide after an offer?

It varies by employer. Asking for one to two working days is common and rarely causes problems. If you need longer, explain why and ask.

What should I say at the very end after this question?

Thank them and restate your interest in one sentence. The page on is there anything else you would like to add has short closing lines.
