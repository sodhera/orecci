Is there anything else you would like to add? How to close well

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Yes, say something: this is your last chance to shape what the interviewer remembers. Give a 20 to 30 second close that names the one or two reasons you fit the role, fixes or adds to any answer you were unhappy with, and ends with a clear statement that you want the job. Then stop talking.

Why "No, I think we covered everything" wastes the moment

Interviewers write their notes right after you leave. The last thing you say is fresh when they do. "No, that's everything, thanks" is polite, but it hands the summary to them, and they will write it from whatever stuck, which might be the answer you fumbled.

The question is also an open door. The interviewer is telling you: if we missed something important, now is the time. Most candidates say no. A calm, short close sets you apart without any trick.

Three ways to use the last word

Pick the one that fits how the interview went. You can combine the first and third.

OptionUse it whenWhat it sounds like
Closing summaryThe interview went fine and you want to leave a clear picture"If I had to sum up what I'd bring, it's..."
Repair an earlier answerOne answer came out weak, vague or wrong"I'd like to go back to the question about..."
Add something missingA strength or experience the job needs never came up"One thing we didn't get to is..."
Confirm interestAlways, as the last line"I'd really like this role."

The 30-second closing summary

Thirty seconds is about 65 to 80 words at a normal speaking pace. That is enough for four short moves:

  1. Thank them in one line. "Thanks, I've enjoyed hearing about the team."
  2. Name your fit in their words. Use a phrase they used today. If they said "we need someone who can calm down a messy project," say that back.
  3. Point to your proof. One example you already told, in a sentence. "Like the vendor migration I described, where I got three teams onto one schedule."
  4. Say you want it. "I'd really like to do this job." Plain words work better than "I'm very excited about this opportunity."

Do not introduce a brand-new long story here. A new example needs setup, and the close is not the place for setup.

How to repair a weak answer from earlier

Almost everyone has one answer they replay on the way out. You can fix it before you leave the room, and it shows you can reflect under pressure.

  1. Name the question briefly. "I'd like to add to my answer about handling a missed deadline."
  2. Say what you missed, without apologising at length. "I described the problem but didn't say what changed afterwards."
  3. Give the missing piece in two or three sentences. The result, the number, or the clearer example.
  4. Stop. Don't relitigate the whole answer.

Keep the repair under 30 seconds. If you need longer, you are giving a second full answer, and that can make the first one look worse.

Example: repairing a technical answer

"Yes, one thing. When you asked how I'd speed up the slow report, I went straight into indexes and missed the first step. I'd check the query plan before changing anything, because in my last job the slowdown turned out to be one bad join, not missing indexes. I'd just like you to know that's how I'd actually approach it."

Example closes

These are examples of the structure with made-up details. Change them to your own facts.

A graduate after a good interview

"Thank you, this has been really useful. You mentioned the role needs someone who can learn the tools fast and write clearly for clients. My final-year project was exactly that, learning a new analysis tool in three weeks and writing it up for a non-technical panel. I'd really like to start my career on this team."

An experienced hire who forgot a key point

"Just one thing we didn't cover. The job post mentions budget ownership, and we didn't talk about it. In my current role I manage a budget across four vendors and brought it in under plan two years running. I'd be glad to go into detail in a later round. And I want to be clear, I'd like this job."

A career changer

"Thanks for your time. I know my background is in teaching, not sales. What I hope came across is that I've spent eight years persuading thirty people at once to do something they didn't want to do. That's the part of this role I'm most sure I can do well."

How this differs from "Do you have any questions for us?"

Interviewers often ask both, in either order. "Any questions?" is your chance to learn about them. "Anything to add?" is your chance to add to what they know about you. Prepare for both. If they only ask one, you can fold a short summary into your answer before you ask your questions. See what to ask at the end.

Practice it out loud

A closing summary sounds easy until you are tired at the end of an hour. Say yours out loud three times before the interview, then once more in the waiting area in your head. Time it with the speech length calculator or simply a phone timer.

Questions people ask

Is it rude to say no?

No, it is not rude. It is just a missed chance. If you truly have nothing to add, say one line of interest: "Nothing new, but I want to say I'd really like this role."

How long should my closing statement be?

Twenty to thirty seconds, or roughly 50 to 80 words. Longer than a minute starts to feel like a speech.

Should I correct a factual mistake I made earlier?

Yes, briefly. "I said three years earlier, it's actually four." A quick correction shows care. Leaving it can cause confusion later in reference checks.

What if the interview went badly?

Use the repair option on the single answer that mattered most for the role, then confirm interest. One good repair can change how the interviewer remembers the conversation.

Can I ask about next steps here?

Yes, at the very end, after your summary. "What are the next steps from here?" is a normal closing question.
