Tell me about a time you showed leadership (with no title)

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Pick a moment when you moved a group forward without being asked: you saw what nobody owned, proposed a direction, got people to go along with it, and the result improved. Tell it with STAR in about 90 seconds, spend most of that time on what you said and did to bring people with you, and finish with a concrete result.

What counts as leadership when you're not the boss

Interviewers who ask this of non-managers are testing one thing: can you influence people you have no authority over? That is most of the leadership anyone does at work, even managers. You don't need a team reporting to you. You need a moment where things would have stalled, drifted or gone wrong if you hadn't stepped in, and other people chose to follow your lead.

Good leadership stories without a title usually look like one of these:

  • Nobody owned it. A shared problem everyone complained about, and you organised the fix.
  • The group was stuck. People disagreed or waited, and you proposed a plan they could agree on.
  • Someone needed help. You coached a new colleague or classmate until they could work on their own.
  • The lead was missing. Your manager was out, and you kept the work moving and the decisions sensible.

What does not count: doing your own work very well. That's a strength story, not a leadership one. If nobody else changed what they did because of you, pick another example.

Where to find your story

Look at the last two or three years, not your whole life.

Individual contributor at workSet up a shared process nobody had asked forShow that peers adopted it
Intern or new hireOrganised the other interns around a shared deliverableShow you were new and did it anyway
University group projectBroke a deadlock and split the workName the disagreement honestly
Retail, hospitality or hourly jobRan a shift or trained new starters when the supervisor was outGive the size: people, hours, customers
Volunteering or a clubRecruited people or fixed something that kept failingShow people chose to follow you
Sports teamChanged how the team trained or communicatedKeep the sport details short

How to structure it

Use STAR, but weight it heavily toward the action. Leadership lives in the action part.

  1. Situation (10–15 seconds). Where you were and what was going wrong. One or two sentences.
  2. Task (one sentence). What needed to happen that nobody was doing. "No one owned the handover, so I offered to."
  3. Action (45–60 seconds). The two or three things you did to get others on board: who you talked to first, what you proposed, how you handled the person who disagreed, how you followed up.
  4. Result (15 seconds). A number if you have a real one, plus what happened to the team: they kept the new process, the new starter worked alone in two weeks, the project shipped.

The full method is in our STAR method guide. The whole answer should run about 90 seconds, roughly 200–230 words.

Example answers

These show the structure with made-up details. Use your own story.

Example answer for an individual contributor

"On my analytics team, three of us built reports for sales, and every Monday we found we'd pulled slightly different numbers. Nobody was assigned to fix it. I suggested we agree on one shared definition for each metric. Before the team meeting I spoke to each colleague one on one, because I knew one of them had built the original reports and might feel criticised. I asked him to lead the definitions for the two metrics he knew best, and I drafted the rest. We tried it for one month. The Monday mismatches stopped, and our manager later asked us to roll the same definitions out to the finance team."

Example answer for a recent graduate

"In my final-year marketing project, our group of five was two weeks from the deadline with no agreed campaign idea, because two people each wanted their own. I suggested we stop debating and test both: each person pitched to five students outside the library and we counted reactions. It took one afternoon. One idea clearly won, and because we'd tested it rather than argued, the person whose idea lost was fine with it. I then split the remaining work by strengths and set two check-ins a week. We submitted three days early and got the top mark in our cohort."

Example answer for a retail job

"At the shoe store where I worked, our supervisor was off for two weeks during the back-to-school rush. I wasn't made shift lead, but I noticed new staff were stuck at the till while customers waited for fittings. I asked the manager if I could reorganise our breaks and pair each new starter with an experienced person for fittings. I showed the two new starters how to measure feet in ten minutes instead of guessing. Queue complaints dropped, and when the supervisor came back she kept the pairing system."

Mistakes to avoid

MistakeWhy it hurtsTry instead
Saying "we" the whole timeThey can't tell what you didSay "I" for your actions, "we" for the shared result
A story where you just worked hardNobody followed you, so it's not leadershipPick a moment where others changed what they did
Overruling peopleSounds bossy, not like a leaderShow how you got agreement
No resistance at allToo easy to be convincingMention the person or problem you had to win over
Apologising for having no titleUndercuts the whole answerJust tell the story; the title doesn't matter

Follow-up questions to expect

Leadership answers invite digging. Prepare one-sentence answers to:

  • "How did the others react at first?"
  • "What would you have done if they'd said no?"
  • "What would you do differently now?"
  • "Did anyone keep doing it after you left?"

Practising out loud with someone who asks these is the fastest way to find the gaps. In Speaking Coach, the interviewer follows up on what you actually said, so a vague action section gets questioned the same way a real interviewer would.

Questions people ask

Can I use an example from university or volunteering?

Yes, especially if you have under three years of work experience. Choose the example where the stakes and the number of people were highest, and keep the setting description short.

What if I genuinely have never led anything?

Look smaller. Training one new colleague, organising a shared document, or getting a stuck group chat to a decision all count. Leadership is about influence, not size.

How is this different from "What is your leadership style?"

This question asks for one past story. The leadership style question asks how you usually lead, and you answer with a description plus a short example.

Can I use the same story for teamwork and leadership questions?

Better not, since many interviews ask both. In the teamwork story you play your part well; in the leadership story you set the direction. Prepare one of each.
