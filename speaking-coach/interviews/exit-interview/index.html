What to say in an exit interview without burning bridges

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

In an exit interview, share a few specific, constructive observations that could help the team, keep personal complaints and anything about individual colleagues out of it, and assume what you say may be shared. Phrase criticism as what happened, its effect, and what would help, and end on genuine thanks, because references and future colleagues may depend on it.

What an exit interview is for

When you resign, many employers offer an exit interview, usually with HR, sometimes as a form instead. The company wants to know why people leave and what it could change. You want to leave well: good references, a good reputation and an open door.

Those goals mostly line up, as long as you remember one thing: assume nothing you say is fully confidential. HR may summarise feedback for managers, and a sharp comment can travel. Say only what you'd be comfortable hearing repeated, in your words, to the people involved.

What to share and what to keep to yourself

ShareKeep to yourself
Your main reason for leaving, brieflyA long list of every frustration
Specific, fixable issues (process, tools, workload)Complaints about individuals' personalities
What worked well and should continueGossip or other people's complaints
Patterns others will face tooAnger or sarcasm
Ideas that would help the next person in your roleDetails of your new salary or offer, if you'd rather not

Serious issues are different. If you experienced or saw harassment, discrimination or safety problems, an exit interview may not be the right or only place. Consider your company's formal reporting route, keep a written record, and get independent advice if needed.

Turning criticism into useful feedback

The difference between burning a bridge and helping is mostly phrasing. Use three parts: what happened, its effect, what would help.

What you might feelWhat you could say
"My manager was useless.""I'd have benefited from more regular check-ins. We met about once a quarter, and I often wasn't sure what mattered most."
"The workload was insane.""For most of the last year, the team covered two open roles. It meant evenings most weeks, and I think hiring sooner would help the people staying."
"Nobody gets promoted here.""I didn't see a clear path from my level to the next. Written criteria would have helped me plan."
"The tools are a joke.""The reporting tool took about a day a week to work around. Fixing that would free up time for the analysts."
"I'm leaving for more money.""The new role pays more, and that was part of it. I'd also say that earlier pay reviews might keep people longer."

Each version is specific, factual and points to something they could change. It's also much harder to dismiss than an angry comment.

Example answers to common questions

These are examples with made-up details. Use your real reasons.

"Why are you leaving?"

"Mainly for the role itself. It's a step up into managing a team, which I couldn't see happening here in the next year or two. I've learned a lot in this job, and it's what made me ready for that step."

"What did you like most about working here?"

"The team. People shared knowledge generously, and I learned more in my first year here than anywhere else."

"What could we improve?"

"Two things. Clearer criteria for moving up a level, so people can plan their growth. And regular one-to-ones: when we had them, it made a real difference to how clear my priorities were."

"How was your relationship with your manager?"

"Good on a personal level. I'd have liked more structured feedback. Monthly check-ins would have helped me know where I stood."

"Would you come back?"

"I'd certainly consider it. I'm leaving on good terms with a lot of respect for the team."

How much to say

Two or three points of feedback is usually the right amount. More than that, and the important ones get lost. Choose the ones that would most help the people staying. A useful test: would you be comfortable if your manager read this exact sentence?

If you're nervous, say your main points out loud beforehand. Feedback that sounds measured in your head can come out sharper when you're emotional. Speaking Coach can play the HR partner if you describe the meeting, and its feedback quotes what you said with a suggested way to phrase it.

A form instead of a meeting

Some companies send a survey instead. The same rules apply, and more strongly: written comments are easy to forward. Keep each answer short and factual, and leave a blank or "No further comments" if you have nothing constructive to say.

Ending well

Close on thanks that you actually mean:

"Thanks for asking. I've genuinely enjoyed working here, and I hope the feedback is useful for the team."

Then make the last weeks count: hand over properly, thank people individually, and if there's a leaving event, see what to say in a farewell speech.

Questions people ask

Do I have to do an exit interview?

Often it's voluntary, but check your company's policy. If you're unsure, you can attend and keep your answers brief and positive.

Should I be honest in an exit interview?

Be honest but selective. Share a few constructive, specific points, and leave out personal complaints. Honest doesn't have to mean everything.

Is an exit interview confidential?

It depends on the company. Assume feedback may be shared in summary, and say nothing you'd regret being repeated.

Can an exit interview affect my reference?

It could, if it goes badly. Calm, constructive feedback is unlikely to hurt you; angry comments about individuals might.

Do I have to say where I'm going next?

No. You can say "I've accepted a role at another company" without naming it, especially if you're not ready to share yet.
