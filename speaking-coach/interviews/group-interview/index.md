# Group interview tips: how to stand out without talking over

In a group interview, you stand out by contributing early, building on what others say, and helping the group work, not by talking the most. Speak within the first few minutes, keep each point short, use other candidates' names, invite quieter people in, and take a useful role such as timekeeper or summariser.

## What a group interview looks like

Several candidates are interviewed together, usually for roles where many people are hired at once: retail, hospitality, call centres, airlines, seasonal work and graduate programmes. A typical session includes some of these:

- **A round of introductions:** each person speaks for 30–60 seconds.
- **Shared questions:** the interviewer asks a question and goes around the room.
- **A group task:** plan an event, rank items for a survival scenario, design a promotion.
- **Short individual chats** at the end.

The assessors watch all of it, including the moments between tasks. They're usually looking at how you treat other candidates as closely as at what you say.

## Speak early, keep it short

The longer you stay silent, the harder it gets to start, and the more the group settles into a pattern without you. Aim to make your first contribution within the first few minutes of any task.

Your first point doesn't have to be brilliant. It can be procedural:

> "Shall we take a minute each to read the brief, then go round with first ideas?"

> "We have 20 minutes. Should someone keep an eye on time? I'm happy to."

After that, keep each contribution to about 15–30 seconds. Short points get picked up by others. Long speeches get waited out.

## Build, don't repeat

When someone has said something good, building on it shows you listen. When they've said your idea first, don't repeat it as yours.

| Moment | What to say |
| --- | --- |
| Agreeing and adding | "I agree with Sam about the budget. One thing to add: we could cut the printed flyers and use the store's social pages." |
| Disagreeing politely | "I see why that works for footfall. My worry is staff time on a Saturday. Could we do it on a weekday instead?" |
| Your idea was taken | "That's close to what I was thinking. The bit I'd add is…" |
| The group is going in circles | "We've got two strong options. Can we vote, then spend the time on the details?" |
| Coming back to a missed point | "Earlier Priya mentioned accessibility, and I don't think we covered it." |

Using other candidates' names is one of the easiest ways to show you were listening. Learn them in the introductions.

## Including quieter people

Bringing in someone who hasn't spoken is a visible, generous act, and assessors notice it.

> "Ana, we haven't heard your view yet. Which option would you pick?"

> "Before we decide, does anyone who hasn't spoken see a problem with this?"

Do it once or twice, not after every point. And ask a real question, so it doesn't sound like you're managing them.

## Roles you can take without being bossy

You don't need to be the leader to show leadership. These roles help the group and give you natural reasons to speak:

| Role | What you do | Example line |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Timekeeper | Track time and flag checkpoints | "Five minutes left. Should we agree on the final answer?" |
| Summariser | Pull ideas together | "So far we have three ideas: A, B and C." |
| Note-taker | Write the group's decisions | "I'll write these up so we can present them." |
| Questioner | Test the idea | "What would a customer think of this?" |
| Presenter | Report back to the assessors | "Shall I present? I'll make sure everyone's idea is in it." |

If you present the group's answer, credit others by name: "Jordan's idea about the loyalty card was the core of this."

## Your 30-second introduction

Group introductions are short and back-to-back, and people forget most of them. Make yours concrete. Example with made-up details:

> "I'm Leah. I've worked weekends at a garden centre for two years, mostly on the tills and helping customers find plants. I applied here because I love the idea of a busier shop where no two days are the same. Outside work, I run a small book club, so I'm used to getting a group talking."

One job, one reason, one personal detail that hints at a skill. About 60 words, under 30 seconds.

## Mistakes to avoid

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Talking over someone | The clearest negative signal in the room | Wait for a pause; [interrupt politely](/speaking-coach/small-talk/how-to-politely-interrupt/) only if needed |
| Staying silent until the end | Assessors have nothing to score | One short point in the first few minutes |
| Dismissing an idea | Shows how you'd treat colleagues | "Yes, and…" or a respectful concern |
| Repeating someone's idea as yours | Others notice; assessors do too | Credit them and add something new |
| Playing to the assessors | Looks performative | Talk to the group, not the observers |
| Long speeches | People stop listening | 15–30 seconds per point |

## If you're naturally quiet

Quiet people often do well in group interviews because they listen. The trick is to make that visible. Prepare two procedural lines in advance (timekeeping, summarising), use them early, and then contribute one well-chosen point per task. ## Questions people ask

### How do you stand out in a group interview?

Speak early, keep points short, build on others by name, and help the group finish the task. Assessors look for people who make the group better, not the loudest voice.

### What if another candidate dominates the conversation?

Don't compete with volume. Wait for a breath and redirect: "That's a good point. Can we hear from the people who haven't spoken yet?" It shows the skills they want.

### Should I try to be the leader in a group task?

Only if it comes naturally. Leadership can mean keeping time, summarising or bringing others in. A self-appointed leader who ignores people scores worse than a helpful team member.

### What do assessors look for in a group interview?

Usually communication, teamwork, listening, problem solving and how you treat others. The exact criteria depend on the role.

### What should I do if someone gives my answer before me?

Acknowledge it and add something: "I'd say the same as Tom, and I'd add one thing from my time in a café…" Never pretend you didn't hear it.
