How to prepare for a panel interview: tips for answering a group

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

In a panel interview, answer the person who asked the question, then let your eyes move to the others during the answer and come back to the asker to finish. Learn each panelist's name and role at the start, expect each to care about something different, and prepare at least one question for each person.

What changes when there are three people

A panel interview has two to five interviewers in the same room or call. Each usually has a set of questions, and they take turns. That changes three things:

  • Your attention has to move. Talking only to the most senior person or only to whoever smiles most leaves others out.
  • The pace changes with each handoff. One panelist asks short, factual questions; the next wants long stories.
  • Each person scores something different. One answer has to work for several listeners at once.

The upside: you meet more of the team in one hour, and a good answer is heard by everyone who decides.

Learn the names and roles first

Ask the recruiter beforehand who will be on the panel. Look each person up so you know their role. At the start of the interview, when they introduce themselves, write their names on your pad in the order they're sitting. On video, the tiles may move, so jot a word about each person's background next to their name.

Use names once or twice, not in every answer. "That's a good question, Priya" every time sounds rehearsed. Using a name when you link back to something they said earlier is natural: "Priya, you mentioned the team is moving to a new CRM…"

Where to look while you answer

A simple pattern that works for most answers:

  1. Start with the person who asked. Your first sentence goes to them.
  2. Widen as you go. During the middle of the answer, let your eyes rest on each other panelist for a sentence or so.
  3. Finish with the asker. Return to them for the last line, so they know you've answered their question.

On video, you can't look at individuals. Look at the camera for your key point and use names when you want to bring someone in.

Reading the different panel roles

Not every panel has all of these, but most have two or three.

PanelistWhat they usually care aboutHow to address it
Hiring managerCan you do the job and be managed easily?Concrete examples tied to the role's main problems
HR or recruiterValues, behaviour, fit with policies, practical detailsClear, honest answers on motivation, conflict and logistics
Future peerWould I want to work next to this person?Collaboration examples, how you share work and credit
Senior leaderJudgement and the bigger pictureShorter answers, reasons, trade-offs, business impact
Technical expertDepth in your specialismSpecific detail; admit gaps cleanly
Quiet note-takerScoring against a formAnswer the question fully; don't play to the room

A quiet panelist is not a hostile one. They may be scoring, or saving their questions. Include them with your eyes as much as anyone.

Handling handoffs

Handoffs between panelists are where people lose rhythm. A few habits help.

  • Reset for each new person. Look at them, pause, and answer at the length their questions suggest.
  • Connect, don't repeat. If a later question overlaps an earlier one, refer back: "I touched on this with Marcus, so let me add a different example."
  • If two panelists disagree in front of you, don't pick a side. Acknowledge both: "I can see how both could work. In my last job, we started with the faster option and reviewed after a month."
  • If you're interrupted by another panelist, stop, take their question, and offer to finish the first: "Happy to come back to the budget point after."

Example answer for a panel

Question from the HR partner: "Tell me about a time you disagreed with a colleague." Made-up details, written for a panel that also includes the hiring manager.

"Last year, a colleague in product wanted to launch a campaign two weeks before the feature was stable. I thought we'd get complaints. Instead of arguing in the meeting, I asked for half an hour with her, and we looked at the support tickets from the last launch together. We agreed to launch to 10% of customers first. It went fine, and we rolled it out a week later. [Looking toward the hiring manager] That's how I'd want to handle the launch calendar here, which I know is busy in Q1."

The answer serves HR (handled conflict well) and gives the hiring manager something about their own team.

Your questions at the end

When they ask "Do you have any questions for us?", aim one question at each of two or three panelists, based on their role:

  • To the hiring manager: "What would a great first six months look like?"
  • To the peer: "What's the part of the week you most look forward to?"
  • To HR: "What happens after today, and when should I expect to hear?"

Mistakes to avoid

MistakeWhy it hurtsInstead
Talking only to the most senior personOthers feel ignored and score you lowerStart with the asker, widen, finish with the asker
Forgetting namesYou can't link answers back to peopleWrite names in seating order at the start
Giving every panelist the same answer lengthSenior leaders want short; experts want detailMatch the length to the person's questions
Treating the silent panelist as unimportantThey may hold the scoring sheetInclude them in eye contact
One thank-you email to "the panel"Looks genericShort individual notes, each mentioning one thing they said

Questions people ask

How do I prepare for a panel interview differently?

Prepare the same answers as for a one-to-one, then add two things: research each panelist's role, and prepare a question for each. Practise moving your gaze between two or three points in the room.

How long is a panel interview?

Often 45 to 90 minutes, longer than a one-to-one because each panelist has their own questions. Ask the recruiter so you can pace yourself.

Should I shake hands with everyone?

If it's in person and the setting is formal, yes, with each person, starting with whoever greets you. If it's crowded or awkward, a smile and a nod to each is fine.

What if one panelist seems unfriendly?

Stay steady and answer them as fully as the others. Some interviewers are neutral on purpose so they don't influence the rest of the panel.

Do I send a thank-you note to every panelist?

If you have their email addresses, a short note to each is better than one group email. Mention one thing each person said.
