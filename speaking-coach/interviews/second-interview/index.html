What to expect in a second interview and how to prepare for it

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

A second interview usually goes deeper than the first: more detailed questions about your experience, meeting the people you'd work with, and sometimes a task or short presentation. Prepare by reviewing what you said in round one, fixing your weakest answer, bringing new examples rather than repeating old ones, and asking sharper questions about the team and the work.

How round two differs from round one

In the first round, the company checks that you could fit. In the second, they check that you will. Here's what usually changes.

First interviewSecond interview
Who you meetRecruiter or hiring managerHiring manager, team members, sometimes a senior leader
Question depth"Tell me about your experience with X""Walk me through exactly how you did X, step by step"
FormatOne conversationOften several back-to-back conversations, a panel, or a task
FocusSkills match, motivation, basicsHow you work, judgement, fit with this specific team
Your questionsGeneral ones about the roleSpecific ones about the team, the work and the first months
Practical talkSalary range, maybeSalary, start date and notice period come up more often

Not every company follows this pattern. Ask the recruiter: "Who will I be meeting, and is there anything I should prepare?" Most will tell you.

Review your round-one answers

Write down, from memory, every question you were asked in the first interview and what you said. Then mark each answer:

  • Strong: they reacted well or asked an interested follow-up.
  • Weak: you rambled, blanked, or gave a vague example.
  • Unfinished: they said "we'll come back to that" or seemed unconvinced.

The weak and unfinished ones are your best preparation. The same topic often returns, sometimes from the same person. Fixing it can be as simple as opening round two with it:

"Last time, Dana asked how I'd handle reporting for three regions at once, and I don't think I gave a clear answer. I've thought about it since. I'd start by…"

That sounds confident, not apologetic. It shows you listen and reflect, which is part of what they're testing.

Bring new examples

If you told the same story to different people in round one, fine. But don't give the same three stories to people who already heard them. Prepare two or three fresh examples, and expect each to be pushed further:

  • "What did you do when that didn't work?"
  • "Who disagreed with you, and how did you handle it?"
  • "What would you change if you did it again?"
  • "What exactly was your part, compared with the team's?"

If you can't answer three follow-ups on a story, choose a different one. A behavioral interview story bank makes this easy.

Meeting the team

Peers often join in round two. They're asking themselves a simple question: would I want this person next to me on a hard week? They tend to care less about polish and more about:

  • How you talk about past colleagues.
  • Whether you'd share work and credit.
  • Whether you'd ask for help or quietly struggle.
  • Whether you're curious about what they actually do.

Ask them real questions: "What does a normal Tuesday look like for you?" "What's the part of the job you'd change if you could?" Their answers tell you more about the role than anything else in the process.

If there's a task or presentation

Some second rounds include a short case, a work sample or a presentation. Read the brief twice, answer the exact question asked, and state your assumptions out loud. If it's a presentation, see how to give a presentation in a job interview for timing and Q&A.

Example: a stronger answer in round two

Question: "How would you approach your first few months here?" Made-up details for a data analyst role.

"From what Sam and Leila told me today, the biggest pain is that the sales and finance dashboards show different revenue numbers. In my first month I'd map where each one pulls its data from and sit with both teams to see how they use them. By month two I'd want one agreed definition of revenue written down. And I'd ship small fixes early, so people see changes while the bigger work is under way."

It uses what the team said that day. That is the difference between a round-one answer and a round-two one. For more, see what would you do in your first 90 days.

Prepare in the three days before

  1. Day 3: Write down every round-one question and mark weak answers. Ask the recruiter who you'll meet.
  2. Day 2: Rebuild your weak answers. Prepare two new stories. Look up each person you'll meet.
  3. Day 1: Say your answers out loud, including the fixed ones. Prepare a researched salary range in case it comes up. Write three specific questions per person.
  4. On the day: Reread the job post and your round-one notes on the way.

Speaking the reworked answers aloud matters more than rewriting them. Speaking Coach can play the interviewer and dig into your stories with follow-ups, then show you a line you said and a better way to say it.

Questions people ask

Is a second interview a good sign?

Yes, it means they think you could do the job. It doesn't mean you have it; several candidates are usually invited, so prepare as seriously as before.

Will they ask the same questions in the second interview?

Some may return, especially ones you answered weakly. Most second-round questions go deeper into your experience and how you'd work with this team.

How long is a second interview?

Often longer than the first, from one hour to half a day if it includes several meetings or a task. Ask the recruiter for the schedule.

Should I bring up salary in the second interview?

Let them raise it, but be ready. If they ask, give a researched range. If they don't, it's fine to ask the recruiter about the next steps and timing instead.

What should I wear to a second interview?

The same level as the first interview. If you met the team and saw how they dress, you can match that, slightly smarter.
