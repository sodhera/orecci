What is a stress interview? How to stay calm and answer

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

A stress interview is one where the interviewer deliberately applies pressure, through long silences, interruptions, blunt challenges or a cold manner, to see how you think and behave when uncomfortable. Stay composed by slowing down, pausing before answering, keeping your tone level, and using short steady lines that acknowledge the challenge and return to your evidence.

Why some employers do it

Most interviews try to put you at ease. A stress interview does the opposite, on purpose. Most employers don't interview this way, but you may meet it, or elements of it, in roles where pressure is part of the job: sales, trading, consulting, client-facing roles, some emergency and security services.

The reasoning is that a relaxed interview shows how you behave when things are easy, and some jobs are rarely easy. The interviewer wants to see whether you:

  • Keep thinking clearly when someone is hostile.
  • Stay polite when provoked.
  • Defend a position without becoming defensive.
  • Recover after a stumble.

Not every tough interview is a stress interview. Some interviewers are simply rushed, tired or bad at interviewing. The response is the same either way, and remember that you're also learning what working there might be like.

Common tactics and steady lines

TacticWhat it looks likeA steady line
Long silenceYou finish; they stare and say nothingWait a few seconds, then: "Would you like me to go deeper on any part of that?"
InterruptionThey cut you off mid-answerStop, let them finish, then: "Let me finish that point briefly, then I'll come to yours."
Direct challenge"That's not a very impressive result.""I understand it may look small. In context, it was a 20% improvement on a team that had been flat for two years."
Dismissal"I don't think you're right for this role.""I'd like to understand why. What concerns you most?"
Rapid-fire questionsQuestions faster than you can answer"Let me take those one at a time, starting with the budget question."
Repeating a question"But why did you leave?" asked three timesGive the same answer, calmly, a little shorter each time
Cold mannerNo smile, no nods, looking at the phoneKeep your own warmth; don't mirror the coldness
Odd or impossible questions"How many windows are in this city?"Think out loud: "I'll estimate. Let me start with the number of buildings…"

Each line does the same three things: it stays polite, it doesn't argue about the tactic itself, and it moves back to substance.

Composure, physically

Pressure shows up in the body first: a faster heartbeat, a tight voice, rushing. You can't stop the feeling, but you can stop it from running your speech.

  1. Breathe out slowly before you answer. A long out-breath steadies a shaky voice. The guide on stopping your voice shaking has more on this.
  2. Pause for one or two seconds before answering a hostile question. It looks considered, not rattled.
  3. Slow your pace slightly. Pressure speeds most people up.
  4. Lower your pitch a little at the end of sentences. Rising endings sound uncertain under challenge.
  5. Keep your hands still and visible, resting on the table.

If your mind goes blank, say so calmly and take a moment: "Let me take a second to think about that." See what to do when your mind goes blank.

Example exchange

A made-up example for a sales role.

Interviewer: You missed your target two quarters last year. Why should I believe you can hit ours?

Candidate: That's fair to ask. I did miss it twice. Both times it was a territory change mid-quarter, and I closed the gap the following quarter both times. Over the full year I finished at 104% of target. I'd expect your targets to be harder, and I'd want to see the pipeline in my first week to plan for that.

Interviewer: Everyone has an excuse.

Candidate: I understand why it might sound like one. The number I'd point to is the full year: 104%. I'm happy to share how I rebuilt the pipeline after the territory change, if useful.

The candidate admits the fact, gives context once, doesn't repeat the excuse when pushed, and offers more evidence.

What not to do

ReactionWhy it hurtsInstead
Getting defensive or sarcasticIt's exactly what's being testedAcknowledge the point calmly and give evidence
Arguing about the questionTurns it into a fightAnswer it, or ask what they mean
Over-apologisingLooks like you're foldingOne acknowledgement, then substance
Rushing to fill silenceOften leads to rambling or backtrackingWait, then offer to expand
Changing your answer under pressureLooks like you'll say anythingKeep your position if you believe it; say why
Mirroring the coldnessComes across as unfriendlyStay warm and professional

When it crosses a line

Pressure about your work is one thing. Personal insults, or questions about things like religion, family plans, health or ethnicity, are another. Rules on what employers may ask vary by country. You can decline politely and move on:

"I'd rather keep to my experience and how I'd do this role, if that's all right."

If an interview leaves you feeling it was abusive rather than challenging, that tells you something about the employer. You don't have to accept an offer from them.

How to practise

Practising against real pressure is the only way to get used to it. Ask a friend to play a hostile interviewer: interrupt, stay silent, challenge each answer. Do three rounds, and each time make your steady lines a little shorter.

Questions people ask

What is the purpose of a stress interview?

To see how a candidate thinks and behaves under pressure, usually for roles where pressure, conflict or difficult people are part of the job.

How do you know if it's a stress interview?

Signs include deliberate silences, frequent interruptions, blunt dismissals and a cold manner that doesn't change. It may also just be a poor interviewer; respond the same way.

What are examples of stress interview questions?

"Why should I believe you?", "That answer wasn't good enough, try again", "Why did you fail at that?", or impossible estimates like counting windows in a city.

Should I push back in a stress interview?

Yes, politely, with evidence. Holding your position calmly is often what's being tested. Arguing, sarcasm or giving in all score worse.

Are stress interviews legal?

Using pressure is generally allowed, but discriminatory questions may not be, depending on where you live. You can always decline a personal question politely.
