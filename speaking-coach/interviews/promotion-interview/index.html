Promotion interview: how to prepare and make your case to move up

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

In a promotion interview, the strongest case is evidence that you already do parts of the next-level job. Get the criteria for the higher role, gather three to five examples where you worked at that level, and answer with those, not with how long you've been there. Be ready to talk honestly about any gaps and, if you'd manage former peers, how you'd handle that.

What the panel is really deciding

A promotion interview isn't asking whether you're good at your current job. Presumably you are, or you wouldn't be there. It's asking: is there evidence you'll succeed at the next one? That changes how you prepare.

  • Tenure isn't evidence. "I've been here four years" tells them about loyalty, not readiness.
  • Hard work isn't evidence either. Many people work hard at their current level.
  • Next-level behaviour is. Examples where you've already taken on part of the bigger job.

Get the criteria first

Ask HR or your manager for the job description or level guide for the role you're interviewing for. Many companies have written expectations by level. Read them side by side with your current level and note the differences. Those differences are what the panel will probe.

Show the gap is already closed

For each difference, find an example. Here's what that might look like for a move from senior analyst to team lead (made-up examples):

Next-level expectationCurrent-level versionEvidence you're already there
Sets priorities for othersManages own priorities"I ran sprint planning for six weeks while our lead was on leave."
Develops peopleHelps colleagues when asked"I onboarded our two newest analysts and wrote the onboarding guide we now use."
Handles stakeholders independentlySupports manager in meetings"I've been the main contact for the finance team's monthly reporting since March."
Owns team-level resultsOwns individual outputs"I proposed and led the dashboard rebuild that cut report time from two days to four hours."
Makes trade-offsRaises issues"I decided to delay the regional report by a week so we could fix the data source properly, and explained why to the stakeholders."

Three to five strong examples like these make a better case than a long list of duties. Prepare each as a short story: the situation, what you did that went beyond your level, and the result. The STAR format from the behavioral interview guide works well.

Answering "Why should you get this promotion?"

Reframe from deserving to ready. Example answer with made-up details:

"Because I'm already doing a good part of the role, and the team has seen it work. While Maria was on leave, I ran planning for six weeks and we hit every deadline. I've onboarded our last two hires, and I've been finance's main contact since March. What I'd add as lead is making that consistent: a regular planning rhythm and clear ownership, so the team relies less on any one person, including me."

It points to evidence, names what the team gains, and avoids comparing yourself with colleagues who may also be applying.

If you'd manage former peers

This is one of the most common promotion questions, and one of the hardest. The panel wants to know you've thought about it honestly.

"It would change some relationships, and I wouldn't pretend otherwise. In the first week I'd meet each person one-to-one, acknowledge the change directly, and ask what they need from a lead. I'd be clear that I'll still be fair and approachable, but that I'll also give direct feedback when it's needed. With Sam, who also applied, I'd want to talk early about what he wants to grow into, and how I can help."

See what is your leadership style for more on describing how you'd lead.

Being honest about gaps

You won't meet every expectation yet. The panel knows that. Pretending you do is riskier than naming one gap with a plan:

"The part I've done least is formal performance reviews. I've sat in on two with Maria, and I'd want to shadow the next cycle with HR before running them myself."

Naming one real gap, briefly, with a plan, reads as self-aware. Naming five reads as unready.

Questions to expect

  • "Why do you want to move up now?"
  • "What would you do differently from the current lead?" (Be respectful; talk about additions, not corrections.)
  • "Tell me about a time you led without a title." See showing leadership.
  • "Tell me about a difficult conversation you've had with a colleague."
  • "What's your biggest achievement in your current role?"
  • "Where would you take the team in the next year?"

Rehearse your answers out loud. Speaking Coach can play the panel if you describe the promotion, and after each session shows how you sounded and a line you said with a better way to say it.

MistakeWhy it hurtsInstead
Pointing to tenureIt's not evidence of readinessExamples of next-level work

Criticising the current leadLooks disloyal and politicalTalk about what you'd add

If the answer is no

Ask: "What would I need to show for this to be a yes in six months?" Write down the answer, agree it with your manager, and check in on it. A clear, specific path is the most useful thing a rejected promotion can give you.

Questions people ask

How do I prepare for a promotion interview?

Get the criteria for the higher role, compare them with your current level, and prepare three to five examples where you already worked at the next level. Practise answering out loud.

How do I answer "Why do you deserve this promotion?"

Talk about readiness, not deserving: give two or three examples of next-level work you've already done, and say what the team would gain with you in the role.

Should I mention how long I've worked here?

Briefly, if it's relevant to your knowledge of the team. Don't make it your main argument; experience isn't the same as readiness.

What if a colleague is also interviewing for the same promotion?

Don't mention or compare yourself with them. Focus on your own evidence, and stay supportive whatever the outcome.

What should I do if I don't get promoted?

Ask for specific feedback and what would make you ready next time, agree a plan with your manager, and keep performing well. How you react is noticed.
