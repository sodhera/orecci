How to give a presentation in a job interview: tips and timing

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

For an interview presentation, answer the brief directly in the first minute, give two or three main points with evidence, and close with a clear recommendation or next step, finishing about a minute under the time limit. For 10 minutes, plan around 6–8 slides and about 1,200–1,350 spoken words; then expect 10–20 minutes of questions, which often count as much as the talk.

What the panel is judging

When an employer asks you to present, they usually want to see the work you'd actually do: explain a plan, make a recommendation, or present to stakeholders. They're judging:

  • Did you answer the brief? Many candidates present what they know rather than what was asked.
  • Is it structured? Can the panel follow your logic without effort?
  • Did you keep to time? In most jobs, overrunning a meeting slot is a real problem.
  • How do you handle questions? This is where they see how you think when the script runs out.

Content and slides matter less than people expect. A clear 8-slide talk delivered to time beats a 20-slide deck that runs over.

A structure for a 10 or 15-minute slot

Section10-minute slot15-minute slotWhat goes in it
Opening1 min1–1.5 minThe brief in one line, your answer in one line, the outline
Context1–1.5 min2 minWhat you understood about the situation, and assumptions
Point 12 min3 minYour first main idea, with evidence
Point 22 min3 minSecond idea, with evidence

Buffer1 min1.5 minUnused. It protects you from overrunning

The buffer matters. Nerves, a question mid-talk or a slow laptop will eat it.

How many slides and words

A common guide is one slide per one to two minutes, so 6–8 slides for 10 minutes and 9–11 for 15, plus a title slide. Fewer, clearer slides help: each one should make one point that you could state as a sentence in its title.

For words, at a typical presentation pace of about 130–150 words per minute:

20 minutes18 min2,340–2,700

You can check your own figures with the speech length calculator. Don't script every word, but knowing your rough word budget stops you writing a 2,500-word talk for a 10-minute slot.

Open with the answer

Don't spend the first two minutes on "about me". The panel has your CV. Example opening with made-up details, for a brief asking how you'd grow a regional sales team's revenue:

"You asked how I'd grow revenue in the northern region over the next year. My answer in one line: focus the team on the 40 existing accounts with the most room to grow before chasing new ones. I'll cover three things: what the numbers show, the plan for the first 90 days, and the risks. That should take about nine minutes, leaving time for your questions."

Answer, outline, timing. The panel now knows what to listen for.

Rehearse to time

  1. Rehearse out loud, standing, with the slides, not reading notes silently. Silent reading runs much faster than speech.
  2. Time each section against the table above, not just the total.
  3. Mark a checkpoint: know which slide you should be on at the halfway mark.
  4. Cut, don't speed up. If you run over, remove a point. Talking faster makes everything harder to follow.
  5. Do a final run with someone watching who then asks you three questions.

Handling the Q&A after

The questions are where many panels decide. Expect:

  • Challenges: "Why not focus on new customers instead?"
  • Detail: "How did you get that figure?"
  • Extension: "What would you do if the budget were cut by half?"
  • Fit: "How would you get the existing team on board?"

For each question:

  1. Pause for a second. It shows you're thinking, not reacting.
  2. Answer in one sentence first, then give a reason or example.
  3. Stop. Let them follow up rather than covering everything.
  4. If you don't know, say so and explain how you'd find out: "I don't have the regional churn figure. I'd want it before committing, and I'd get it from the CRM in the first week."
  5. If you disagree with a premise, say so politely: "I'd see it slightly differently. Here's why."

Practical details

  • Send your slides in advance if asked, and bring them as a PDF on a USB stick and in your email.
  • Print a copy for each panelist; they may want to make notes.
  • Ask beforehand whether you'll present on your own laptop, a shared screen or in person.
  • For an online presentation, test screen sharing in the same app the day before.

Questions people ask

How many slides for a 10-minute interview presentation?

About 6 to 8 content slides, plus a title slide. One clear point per slide, and leave a minute spare.

Should I memorise my interview presentation?

Memorise your opening and closing lines, and know the order of your points. Speak the middle from keywords so it sounds natural and survives interruptions.

Can I use notes during an interview presentation?

Yes, short notes or cards are fine. Avoid reading full sentences; glance at a keyword, then look at the panel.

What if I run out of time during the presentation?

Skip to your conclusion: "I'm conscious of time, so let me go straight to my recommendation." It's better than rushing through every remaining slide.

How long is the Q&A after an interview presentation?

Often 10 to 20 minutes, sometimes as long as the presentation itself. Prepare for questions as carefully as for the talk.
