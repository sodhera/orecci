One-way video interview tips: how to do a recorded interview

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

In a one-way video interview, a question appears on screen, you get a short time to think, and then you record your answer against a timer, with no live interviewer. Read the instructions on think time, answer length and retakes first, plan each answer to finish before the clock, put the follow-up into the answer since nobody will ask it, and talk to the lens as if a person were behind it.

How a one-way interview works

A one-way interview (also called asynchronous or recorded) runs on a hiring platform in your browser or an app. There's no one on the other side. The usual flow:

  1. You get a link and a deadline, often a few days.
  2. You check your camera and mic, and many platforms give you a practice question.
  3. A question appears as text or a short pre-recorded video.
  4. A think-time countdown starts.
  5. Recording begins automatically and stops when time runs out.
  6. The next question appears.

The rules differ by employer and platform: think time, answer length, and whether retakes are allowed. Read the instructions screen carefully before starting the first real question. If retakes aren't mentioned, assume you get one go.

Plan for the timer

The timer is the hardest part. In a live interview, you can see when someone has heard enough. Here, the recording just cuts off.

Convert the time limit into a rough word budget. At a typical presentation pace of about 130–150 words per minute:

Answer limitRough word budgetWhat fits
30 seconds65–75 wordsOne clear point and one supporting detail
60 seconds130–150 wordsA short CAR answer: context, action, result
90 seconds195–225 wordsA full STAR story, or a point plus two reasons
2 minutes260–300 wordsA STAR story with a lesson learned
3 minutes390–450 wordsTwo short examples, or one with more detail

You can check other lengths with the speech length calculator. Aim to finish 10–15 seconds before the limit. Being cut off mid-sentence leaves a bad last impression; finishing a little early doesn't.

Glance at the countdown once in the middle, not constantly. If you're past halfway and still on the background, skip to what you did.

Use the think time

Think time is often short: sometimes 30 seconds, sometimes a couple of minutes. Use it for structure, not for writing sentences.

  • Write three words on a pad: the example, the action, the result.
  • Decide your first sentence. Starting well is half of it.
  • Don't try to memorise a paragraph in 30 seconds. It comes out flat.

Answer the follow-up before it's asked

A live interviewer would say "And what happened then?" or "What would you do differently?" Nobody will here. So each answer has to be complete:

  • Give the result. Never end on what you did; end on what changed.
  • Name your part. Say "I" clearly; there's no one to ask "But what did you do?"
  • Add one line of reflection if time allows: what you learned or would repeat.
  • Answer the actual question. Reread it in the think time. A recorded answer that's off-topic has no second chance.

Example: a 60-second answer

Question: "Tell us about a time you worked to a tight deadline." Made-up details for a graduate.

"In my final year, our group's market research report was due on a Friday, and on Tuesday one of our four members dropped out of the course. I took over his section on competitor pricing. I split it into three evenings, asked our lecturer for a one-page brief on what mattered most, and checked in with the group each night so we weren't duplicating work. We submitted on time and got the second-highest mark in the class. What I took from it: under pressure, ask early what matters most, and drop the rest."

That's about 115 words, so roughly 50 seconds, which leaves a safe margin on a 60-second limit.

Retakes: when to use them

If retakes are allowed, decide your rule before you start:

  • Retake if you lost your thread, read the question wrong, or were cut off before the result.
  • Don't retake for a single "um", a small stumble, or because you think you could be slightly better. Later takes often sound more rehearsed and less natural.
  • One retake per question, at most, is a sensible limit. It also keeps your energy for later questions.

If retakes aren't allowed and you stumble, keep going. "Let me put that more clearly" sounds composed on a recording.

Talking to a camera, not a person

This is what makes one-way interviews feel strange. Without a face nodding back, people speed up, flatten their voice, and look at their own image.

  • Look at the lens. Put a small sticky note with an arrow just next to it.
  • Cover or minimise your own video if the platform lets you.
  • Picture one friendly person behind the lens and talk to them.
  • Lift your energy slightly. On a recording, normal energy reads as low.
  • Smile at the start and end of each answer; the first and last seconds stick.

Set up as you would for a live video interview: light in front of you, camera at eye level, quiet room, headphones with a mic if your laptop mic picks up echo.

How to practise

  1. Record yourself on your phone answering five common questions, each to a 60- or 90-second timer.
  2. Watch them back once, following the steps in how to record yourself speaking. Note where you went over time and where you looked away.
  3. Re-record the worst one. Compare.
  4. Do the platform's practice question, if it has one, before the real questions.

Questions people ask

Can I redo answers in a one-way video interview?

It depends on the employer's settings. Some allow one or more retakes, others none. The instructions screen or practice question usually tells you.

Can I use notes in a recorded interview?

You can keep a few keywords near the camera. Reading full sentences is easy to see on video because your eyes move and your voice flattens.

What if my internet drops during a one-way interview?

Most platforms let you resume or contact support. Take a screenshot of any error and email the recruiter promptly to explain what happened.

Who watches one-way interview recordings?

Usually a recruiter or the hiring team reviews them, often in batches. You usually won't know exactly who, so speak as if to the hiring manager.

How long should a one-way interview answer be?

Use most of the time but finish 10–15 seconds early. For a 2-minute limit, about 90 to 105 seconds of speaking is a good target.
