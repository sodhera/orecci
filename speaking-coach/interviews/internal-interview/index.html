How to prepare for an internal interview at your company

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Prepare for an internal interview as seriously as an external one: the panel may know your name but not your work, so bring full examples with results. Use what you know from inside, such as the team's projects, tools and problems, to show you understand the new role, tell your current manager before they hear it elsewhere, and answer 'why move?' with what draws you to the new team, not what pushes you from the old one.

The two traps of an internal interview

Internal candidates have real advantages: you know the company, the systems, the culture and maybe the people. But two traps catch many of them.

Trap 1: assuming they know your work. The hiring manager may have seen you in meetings, but they probably don't know what you did on the project that saved your team a month. Interview panels often score answers against a form. If you don't say it, it doesn't get scored.

Trap 2: treating it as a formality. Relaxed, casual answers ("You know me, I'm good with customers") come across as unprepared next to an external candidate who has rehearsed every answer.

Before the interview

  1. Tell your current manager. Check your company's internal move policy first. Most managers prefer to hear it from you than from HR or a colleague. Keep it simple: "I've applied for the analyst role in the product team. It's about where I want to grow, not about leaving this team."
  2. Talk to someone on the new team. Ask what a typical week looks like and what the hardest part of the job is. It's the research external candidates can't do.
  3. Read the job description as if you were external. Underline the requirements and map an example to each.
  4. Prepare full answers. Including tell me about yourself, even if it feels odd. Focus it on what's relevant to the new role.
  5. Collect evidence. Results, feedback from customers or colleagues, performance review comments you're happy to share.

Answering "Why do you want to move?"

This is the question every internal candidate gets, and the one most get slightly wrong. The panel listens for two things: that you're moving towards something, and that you're not escaping a problem they'll inherit.

Example answer with made-up details, for someone moving from customer support to product:

"I've spent three years in support, and the part I enjoy most is the pattern-spotting: noticing that 30 tickets a week are really one design problem. Last year I wrote up the checkout issues for the product team, and seeing that turn into a fix that cut those tickets in half is what made me want to be on the other side. I'd bring the customer's voice into product decisions, because I've heard it every day."

Notice what it doesn't say: nothing negative about the support team, the manager or the workload. More on this in why do you want this job.

Using inside knowledge without overstepping

Inside knowledge is persuasive when it shows understanding and dangerous when it sounds like criticism or gossip.

Instead ofTry
"Everyone knows the product team is behind on the roadmap.""I know the team has a lot on with the new release. I'd want to help where support insight is useful."
"Your last analyst didn't really get on with sales.""From the support side, I've worked closely with sales, so I can help with that relationship."
"The current process is a mess.""I've seen how the handoff from support to product works. I'd have ideas, after I'd learned how it looks from your side."

Avoid repeating anything you know from private conversations, and don't refer to the person who previously held the role unless asked.

Questions you might get

  • "Why now?" (timing, and whether you've given your current role time)
  • "What will your current team lose when you leave?" (shows maturity; be generous)
  • "How will you handle the transition?" (offer a handover plan)
  • "What do you know about this team's priorities?" (your inside-knowledge chance)
  • "What would you do in your first months?" (see what would you do in your first 90 days)

Rehearse these out loud, not just in your head. Speaking Coach can play the interviewer if you describe the move, and follows up on what you actually said.

If you don't get it

Internal rejections are harder because you'll keep seeing the people involved. Handle it well and it can set up the next move.

  1. Thank the hiring manager, in person or by message, within a day.
  2. Ask for specific feedback: "What would have made me a stronger candidate?"
  3. Agree one or two things to work on, and mention them to your manager.
  4. Keep doing your current job well. People notice how internal candidates react to a no.

Questions people ask

Should I tell my manager I'm applying for an internal job?

Usually yes, before your application becomes known. Check your company's policy, and frame it as growth, not leaving.

How do I answer "Tell me about yourself" in an internal interview?

Briefly: your current role, one or two achievements relevant to the new job, and why this move. Don't assume they know your history.

Is an internal interview easier than an external one?

Not necessarily. You know more, but you're often compared with external candidates who prepared hard, and expectations of insiders can be higher.

What if I know the interviewer well?

Stay friendly but answer as formally as with a stranger. They may need to justify their decision to others, so give them clear evidence to point to.

What should I do if I don't get the internal job?

Thank the hiring manager, ask for specific feedback, and keep performing well in your current role. How you handle a no often shapes the next opportunity.
