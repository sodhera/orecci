# What to Talk About When Meeting Your Partner's Parents

When you meet your partner's parents, ask your partner beforehand what to call them, what they're into and what to steer clear of. On the day, greet them warmly, then ask about them: their home, their work, their town, and stories about your partner growing up. Answer their questions about you simply and honestly, and avoid politics, money and your relationship's private details.

## Ask your partner five things first

Your partner is your best source. Ten minutes of questions the week before saves you from guessing on the day.

1. **What should I call them?** First names, Mr and Mrs, or something else. Don't guess.
2. **What are they into?** Gardening, football, a TV show, a church choir, their dog.
3. **What should I avoid?** A family conflict, a sibling who's not talking to them, a recent loss, a political topic that gets heated.
4. **What do they already know about me?** Your job, where you're from, how you met.
5. **Anything about the family's customs?** Shoes off, saying grace, who serves, whether to bring a gift and what kind.

Also agree on **how you met**. Parents almost always ask. If it was on an app or somewhere you'd rather keep vague, decide together what you'll say.

## The first minute

The first minute is mostly about warmth. Have your greeting ready so you're not searching for words.

> "Hi, I'm Jordan. It's really nice to finally meet you. Thanks for having me."

Follow your partner's lead on hugs or handshakes. If you brought something (flowers, a dessert, a bottle of something they like), hand it over with one plain sentence: "Alex said you love lemon cake, so I brought one from the bakery near us."

Then a simple comment about where you are: "Your garden is lovely. Did you plant all of that?" That gives them something easy to talk about.

## Questions to ask them

Parents are often nervous too. Asking about them is the easiest way to warm things up. These are examples; pick what fits.

**About their life**
- "How long have you lived here? What's the area like?"
- "Alex said you used to be a teacher. What did you teach?"
- "What are you up to these days, now you're retired?"

**About their interests** (from what your partner told you)
- "Alex mentioned you're into birdwatching. What's the best thing you've seen this year?"
- "I heard you make your own bread. How long did it take to get good at it?"

**About your partner growing up** (most parents love this one)
- "What was Alex like as a kid?"
- "Was Alex always this organised, or is that new?"

**About the family**
- "How did you two meet?" Many couples enjoy telling this story, but check with your partner first in case it's complicated.

Follow up on what they say rather than moving down a list. "Teacher" leads naturally to "What age?" and "Do you miss it?"

## Answering their questions about you

Expect questions about your work, your family, where you're from, how you met and sometimes your plans. Answer each in two or three sentences, then ask something back.

| They ask | A good kind of answer |
| --- | --- |
| "What do you do?" | "I'm a physiotherapist at a sports clinic, mostly knee and shoulder injuries. I really like it. Alex said you used to play rugby?" |
| "Where's your family from?" | "My parents are in Leeds. I've got one brother who's still at university. Do you have family nearby?" |
| "So what are your plans?" | "Work's going well and I'd like to run my own clinic one day. For now we're just enjoying living in the city." |
| "How did you two meet?" | The version you agreed on with your partner, told in under 30 seconds. |

If they ask something you'd rather not answer, such as salary or marriage timelines, a friendly deflection works: "We're taking it as it comes. We're really happy." Then ask them a question.

## Topics to avoid

| Topic | Why | If it comes up |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Politics and religion | Too easy to clash, and you're a guest | "I don't know enough about that to have a strong view. How was the trip you mentioned?" |
| Money and salaries | Can sound like boasting or complaining | Talk about work, not pay |
| Your partner's exes | Uncomfortable for everyone | Let it pass |
| Private relationship details | Not for the dinner table | Keep it to the light stories |
| Complaints about your partner | Even as a joke, it lands badly | Say something you love about them instead |
| Family conflicts your partner told you about | Not yours to raise | Act as if you don't know |

## Small things that help

- **Offer to help** set the table or clear up. Even if they say no, offering matters.
- **Put your phone away** for the whole visit.
- **Include everyone,** including the quiet sibling or the grandparent at the end of the table.
- **Compliment the food** specifically: "This sauce is amazing. Is it a family recipe?"
- **If there's a language barrier,** a few words in their language, even simple ones, usually goes a long way. Speak a little slower and let your partner help translate.

## If it goes quiet

Silences at a first meeting are normal. Everyone is a bit careful. Have two or three backup topics: a photo on the wall, the dog, a holiday they mentioned, a dish on the table. "Is that photo from a trip?" restarts most conversations. More in [what to say when a conversation goes quiet](/speaking-coach/small-talk/awkward-silence/).

## Saying goodbye

End warmly and specifically:

> "Thank you so much for having me. The lamb was incredible, and I loved hearing about the sailing trip. I hope we see you again soon."

A short thank-you message through your partner the next day is a nice touch.

## Practice it out loud first

If you're nervous, rehearse the greeting, two questions and your answer to "What do you do?" out loud. It makes the first five minutes much easier. You can describe the situation to Speaking Coach, and an AI partner will play the parent and ask follow-up questions based on what you say.

## Questions people ask

### What should I not say when meeting my partner's parents?

Avoid politics, religion, money, your partner's exes, and complaints about your partner, even as jokes. Keep private relationship details private.

### What questions should I ask my boyfriend's or girlfriend's parents?

Ask about their home, work, interests, and what your partner was like as a kid. Use what your partner told you to make the questions specific.

### Should I bring a gift when meeting the parents?

Ask your partner. In many families a small gift like flowers, a dessert or something they enjoy is welcome. Some cultures have specific customs, so check.

### What if they ask about marriage or kids?

Keep it light and positive: "We're taking it as it comes and really enjoying it." Then ask them something. It's fine not to discuss your plans.

### How long should the first visit be?

Follow your partner's lead. A meal and an hour or so of chatting is common for a first meeting. Leaving while it's still going well is better than staying too long.
