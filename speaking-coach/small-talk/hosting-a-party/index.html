How to Be a Good Host: Introducing Guests and Starting Chats

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

As a host, your main job is introductions. Introduce people with their names plus a hook they share, such as "Leah, this is Tom. Tom just got back from Japan, and you're going in March", then step away once they're talking. Greet every guest in the first minute, look out for anyone standing alone, and keep moving between groups rather than settling in one.

The host's real job

Food and drinks matter, but a party is mostly remembered for how it felt to be there. As the host, you're the only person who knows everyone, so you're the bridge. Your job in conversation terms:

  1. Greet every guest so they feel expected.
  2. Introduce people who don't know each other, with something to talk about.
  3. Rescue anyone stuck alone or stuck with someone they'd rather leave.
  4. Circulate, so no group feels forgotten.

You'll talk to everyone a little and no one for long. That's normal for a host.

The greeting: the first 60 seconds

The first minute sets the tone for a guest's whole evening, especially one who knows few people.

"You made it! Let me take your coat. What can I get you to drink?"

Then, within a few minutes, take them to someone. Don't leave a guest who knows no one standing in the kitchen doorway. Even a brief "Come and meet my sister" is better than "Help yourself, make yourself at home".

Introductions with a hook

A bare introduction ("Leah, Tom. Tom, Leah.") leaves two strangers searching for something to say. A hook gives them their first topic.

The formula: name, name, plus one thing they share or one interesting fact about each.

Instead ofTry
"This is Sam.""Sam, this is Priya. Priya's just moved here from Manchester, and Sam's lived in this street for twenty years. Sam knows every good pub."
"Do you two know each other?""Have you two met? You're both teachers. Primary and secondary, so you can argue about which is harder."
"Everyone, this is Jo.""Everyone, this is Jo. We met at climbing. Jo's the one who talked me into the bouldering competition."
"This is my friend from work.""This is Marcus. He's the reason our team still has a working coffee machine. Marcus, Ana's a fellow dog person."

Good hooks:

  • Shared interests: both run, both cook, both into the same band.
  • Shared situations: both new to the city, both have toddlers, both just changed jobs.
  • A story you know they'll enjoy telling: "Ask Raj about the time his flight got diverted to Iceland."
  • How you know them, which gives each person a reference point.

Then step away. Stay for one or two exchanges, until they're talking to each other rather than to you, and then excuse yourself: "I'll let you two compare notes. I need to check on the oven."

Rescuing stuck guests

Keep an eye out for these situations as you move around:

You noticeWhat to do
Someone standing alone with a drink"Come with me, there's someone you'll like." Walk them to a group.
A guest trapped by a talkerWalk up: "Sorry to steal her, I need her opinion on something in the kitchen."
A group that's gone quietJoin with a question for the group: "Did any of you catch the match?"
A shy guest in a loud groupTurn a question to them: "Ella, you've been to Lisbon, haven't you?"
Two guests who clearly don't get onIntroduce one of them to someone new

Giving a quiet guest a small job, like handing out snacks or choosing music, is another kind rescue. It gives them a reason to talk to people.

Joining and leaving groups as host

You'll move in and out of conversations all night. A few lines help:

  • Joining: "What are we talking about? It looks like the fun corner." Or join with a tray: "Has anyone tried these yet?"
  • Leaving: "I'm going to do a round with drinks, but keep going, I want to hear how that ends."
  • Connecting groups: "You lot need to meet the people on the balcony. They're having the same argument about pineapple on pizza."

As host you have the best exit excuses in the room: the oven, the door, the drinks, the music. Use them freely. Nobody minds a host moving on.

If you give a short toast

If it's a birthday, housewarming or celebration, a short toast can bring everyone together. Keep it under a minute: thank people for coming, say one warm thing about the reason for the party, raise a glass. The how to give a toast guide has a simple four-part shape.

Practise your introductions

Introductions with a hook take a bit of thought on the spot. Before the party, run through your guest list and think of one hook for each pairing you plan. Saying a couple out loud helps them come out naturally. Questions people ask

How do you introduce people at a party?

Say both names and add one thing they share or one fact about each person, so they have something to talk about. Then step away once they're chatting.

What do you do when a guest knows no one?

Greet them warmly, get them a drink, and introduce them to someone within a few minutes, ideally someone with a shared interest.

How do I make sure everyone has a good time?

Keep moving, introduce people, watch for anyone alone, and connect quiet guests with friendly groups.

How long should a host talk to each guest?

A few minutes at a time is normal. You'll check in with people several times rather than having one long chat.

How do I get a party started when everyone is quiet?

Make introductions with hooks, start a group topic, put music on, and bring food around. Movement and small tasks get people talking.
