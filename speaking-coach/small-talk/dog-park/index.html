How to Make Small Talk at the Dog Park (and Meet Neighbors)

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

At the dog park, start with the dog: "What's her name?", "How old is he?" or "What breed is she?" Owners love talking about their dogs, so it's the easiest opener there is. Then move to the person with one question, such as "Do you live nearby?", swap your own names (not just the dogs'), and say hi next time. Regular walk times turn strangers into neighbors.

Why the dog park is the easiest small talk there is

Most small talk needs an excuse. At the dog park, you have one running around at your feet. Dogs give you an instant shared topic, a reason to stand together, and a natural ending (one of you leaves). Owners generally enjoy being asked about their dog, so the pressure is low.

The challenge isn't starting. It's moving from "dog people who nod at each other" to people who actually know each other.

Dog-first openers

SituationOpener
Any dog"What's his name?"
A puppy"How old is she? She's got so much energy."
An unusual breed or mix"What breed is he? I've never seen one like him."
Dogs playing together"They've really hit it off. Mine doesn't usually play like that."
A well-trained dog"Her recall is amazing. How did you train that?"
A dog with a quirky habit"Does he always carry two balls at once?"
Your dog misbehaves"Sorry! He thinks everyone's his best friend."
A rescue look or story"Is she a rescue? How long have you had her?"

"How long have you had her?" is a quietly great question. It invites a story, like a rescue, a breeder, a sudden decision, and stories are where conversation starts.

Moving from the dog to the person

After two or three dog exchanges, the chat either stays at dog level or moves to the people. To move it along, ask one question about them, connected to the dog:

  • "Do you come here every morning?"
  • "Do you live nearby?"
  • "Have you always had dogs?"
  • "Does she come to work with you, or is she home alone?"
  • "Where do you take him on weekends? I need new walks."

Then share something about yourself: "We just moved to Oak Street, so I'm still learning the routes."

Swap your own names

Dog parks are famous for people knowing each other's dogs' names and not each other's. Fix that early:

"I'm Jamie, by the way. I always know the dog's name and never the owner's."

That one line is self-aware, friendly, and gets you their name. If you've been nodding for months and never asked, it's still fine: "This is embarrassing, I know Biscuit's name but not yours." See forgot someone's name for more.

On walks, not just in the park

On the pavement or a trail, the window is shorter: a few seconds as you pass. Keep it to a dog compliment or a question and a smile.

SituationWhat to say
Passing on a path"She's gorgeous." Smile, keep walking.
Dogs want to say hello"Is he friendly? Mine is." Let them sniff, chat for a minute.
Your dog is reactive or nervous"Sorry, she's nervous with other dogs, we'll give you some space."
Their dog is on a lead and pulling awayGive space. A nod is enough.
Same person, same time, third time"We keep meeting! I'm Sam, this is Pepper."

Always ask before letting your dog greet theirs. Some dogs are anxious, ill or in training, and owners appreciate being asked.

Turning regulars into friends

The dog park has the most valuable thing for making friends as an adult: repetition. You see the same people at the same time every day. Use it:

  1. Go at the same time. You'll start seeing the same faces.
  2. Remember one detail each time, human and dog. "How did Luna's vet visit go?"
  3. Walk together. "Are you heading round the lake? Mind if we walk with you?"
  4. Suggest something small. "A few of us get coffee after the Saturday walk. Want to come?"
  5. Offer help. "If you ever need someone to let him out while you're away, I'm just round the corner."

Offering to help with dog care, or asking for it, builds trust fast. That's often the step that turns a park acquaintance into a real friend. The making friends as an adult guide covers the invite in more detail. Many dog park friends are also neighbors, so the neighbor small talk guide is useful too.

Etiquette that helps the chat

Small things make other owners want to talk to you:

  • Watch your dog while you chat. Owners who look away and miss a scuffle or a mess quickly lose friends.
  • Pick up after your dog, visibly.
  • Don't give unasked training advice. "Have you tried…" usually lands badly.
  • Ask before giving treats to someone else's dog.
  • Leave if your dog is getting overexcited. "We'll call it a day, she's had enough."

Questions people ask

What do you say to other dog owners?

Ask about their dog: name, age, breed, or how long they've had it. Then ask one question about them and share your own name.

How do you make friends at the dog park?

Go at the same time each day, learn names, remember details, and suggest walking together or a coffee after.

Is it rude to not want to chat at the dog park?

No. A smile and a nod is fine. Some people want a quiet walk, and other regulars will understand.

Should I ask before my dog greets another dog?

Yes. Always ask, "Is she friendly?" Some dogs are nervous, ill or in training.

What if I forget the owner's name but remember the dog's?

Very common. Say it lightly: "I know Biscuit's name but I've forgotten yours. Sorry!"
