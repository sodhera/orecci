How to Make Conversation at a Dinner Party (Guest Tips)

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

At a dinner party, talk to the people on both sides of you, not just the one you know. Start with how they know the host, then follow up. When the table talks together, offer a light question everyone can answer, bring quieter guests in by name, and steer away from politics with a neutral line and a new question.

The table is different from a party

At a standing party you can move around. At a dinner table you're seated for two or three hours with the same few people. That changes the job: you don't need to meet everyone, but you do need to help the people near you have a good evening.

Three things matter most: talk to both neighbours, contribute to the table conversation now and then, and keep the evening pleasant if a topic gets heated.

Talk to both sides

A common mistake is to talk only to the person you know, or the one who's easiest. The person on your other side then sits in silence.

An old dinner-party habit helps: talk mostly to one neighbour during one course, then turn to the other for the next. You don't have to follow it strictly, but do make sure each neighbour gets real time with you.

Opening lines for the person next to you:

"I don't think we've met. I'm Hannah. How do you know Tom and Lucy?" "Is this your first time at one of their dinners? I've heard the dessert is legendary." "Did you come far tonight?"

Then follow up on whatever they say, and share a little about yourself. For the full method, see how to keep a conversation going.

Questions for the whole table

At some point the table will talk as a group, often between courses. That's a good moment to offer a question anyone can answer. These are examples; pick ones that fit the crowd.

  • "What's the best thing you've eaten this year?"
  • "Has anyone been anywhere good recently? I need holiday ideas."
  • "What's everyone watching at the moment?"
  • "What's a skill you'd learn if you had a free month?"
  • "What was your first job?"

Good table questions are light, have no wrong answer, and give everyone a turn. Avoid questions that leave some people out, like "Where did everyone go to university?" or "Where are you all going skiing this year?"

Bring the quiet guest in

Every table has someone who isn't talking much. They may be shy, new to the group, or less fluent in the language. Including them is one of the kindest things a guest can do.

  • Use their name and an easy question: "Ravi, you said you'd just been to Morocco. How was it?"
  • Pick up something they mentioned earlier: "Didn't you say you play in a band?"
  • Ask for their view in an area they know: "You're a nurse, Ana. Is that true about night shifts?"

Then let them answer without others jumping in. If someone talks over them, a gentle "Sorry, Ravi, you were saying?" brings it back.

Compliment the host and the food

Specific beats general. "This is great" is fine; "This sauce is amazing, what's in it?" starts a conversation. Hosts usually enjoy talking about the food, where the recipe came from, or a disaster they averted in the kitchen.

Avoiding political fights

Politics, religion and money are where dinner parties go wrong. You can't control what others bring up, but you can avoid being the one who escalates.

What happensWhat to doExample line
Someone makes a political commentDon't take the bait; acknowledge and move"Everyone has strong views on that one. Did you get to the coast this summer?"
Two guests start arguingOffer a way out with a new topic"Okay, I need the table's ruling: is a hot dog a sandwich?"
Someone asks your opinion directlyShort, calm, then change course"I'm still making my mind up, honestly. What did everyone think of the film?"
A guest keeps pushingKind but firm"I'd rather not get into it tonight. I'm enjoying the food too much."
You said something that landed badlyShort apology, move on"Sorry, that came out wrong. Anyway, how's the new house?"

If you're with friends who enjoy debating, a good discussion can be a highlight. The test is whether everyone at the table is enjoying it. If one person has gone quiet or tense, it's time to change the subject. More in how to change the subject smoothly.

Telling stories at the table

Dinner is where stories get told. Keep yours short: under a minute, with a clear point or punchline. Watch the table; if eyes drift, wrap it up. And when someone else tells a story, react and ask a question rather than topping it with your own.

Ending the evening well

Thank the hosts specifically on the way out: "Thank you, that was such a fun night. The lamb was incredible." Saying goodbye to the people you sat beside is a nice touch, especially if you'd like to see them again.

Practice before the dinner

If dinner parties make you nervous, rehearse your opener for your neighbours and two table questions out loud. The conversation drill gives you prompts to answer. You can also describe the dinner to Speaking Coach and practise with an AI partner who plays the guest next to you.

Questions people ask

What do you talk about at a dinner party?

How everyone knows the host, the food, travel, films and shows, and light questions anyone can answer. Talk to both neighbours, not just one.

How do I avoid politics at a dinner party?

Don't take the bait. Acknowledge the comment briefly and ask a new, light question. If someone keeps pushing, say kindly that you'd rather not get into it tonight.

How do I include a quiet person at the table?

Use their name and ask about something they mentioned or know about. Give them time to answer and steer back to them if they get talked over.

What if I don't know anyone at the dinner?

Ask your neighbours how they know the host. It's an easy start, and everyone at the table has an answer.

How long should I talk to each person next to me?

Roughly a course each, so both neighbours get real time with you, then join the group conversation when it happens.
