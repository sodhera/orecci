How to Stop Being Awkward in Conversations: Practical Fixes

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Feeling awkward usually comes from attention turned inward: worrying how you come across instead of noticing the other person. Fix it with small, specific habits: answer with one detail and a question back, follow up on what they said, let short pauses happen, and use a clear exit line. Practice these out loud in low-stakes moments until they're automatic.

Why conversations feel awkward

Awkwardness is mostly a feeling of not knowing what comes next, mixed with watching yourself. While you're thinking "Am I saying this right? Do they like me? What do I say now?", you stop hearing the other person, and the conversation loses its thread.

The fix is not to become a different person. It's to learn a few small moves that keep the conversation moving, so your attention can go back outward. Most "awkward" people aren't awkward at all once they have those moves.

Eight awkward habits and what to do instead

Awkward habitWhy it feels awkwardWhat to do instead
One-word answers ("Good." "Yeah.")The other person has nothing to work withOne answer, one detail, one question back
Asking question after questionFeels like an interviewShare something of your own between questions
Planning your next line while they talkYou miss what they said and change the subjectListen for one word to follow up on
Rushing to fill every pauseYou blurt and rambleLet a 2–3 second pause happen; then follow up
Over-explaining or apologisingDraws attention to the awkwardnessSay it once, simply, and move on
Avoiding eye contact completelyReads as uninterested or anxiousLook at them while they talk; glance away when you think
Talking much louder or faster than usualComes across as nervousSlow down your first sentence on purpose
Not knowing how to leaveConversations drag on past their endUse an exit line: "Great chatting, see you later"

You don't need to fix all eight. Pick the one or two that sound most like you.

The one-detail rule

The most useful single fix: whenever someone asks you a question, answer it, add one detail, and hand the conversation back.

Awkward: "How was your weekend?" "Fine."

Better: "Pretty good, thanks. I finally tried the new climbing gym. Have you been?"

The detail gives them something to ask about. The question back gives them a turn. That's a conversation.

Follow-up instead of new topics

Awkward conversations often jump from topic to topic because each one dies. Instead of searching for a new topic, follow up on the last thing they said:

  • "How did that start?" for anything they do.
  • "What was that like?" for anything that happened to them.
  • "What's next for that?" for anything ongoing.

Following up is easier than inventing, and it makes people feel heard. See how to keep a conversation going for more.

Let pauses be normal

Short silences feel much longer to you than to the other person. A pause of two or three seconds in a relaxed conversation is normal. If you need to restart, go back to something they mentioned earlier: "You said you've just moved. How's the new place?" More in what to say when a conversation goes quiet.

When you've already said something awkward

Everyone does. The fastest recovery is a light, short acknowledgement:

"That came out wrong. What I meant was…"

"Ha, I don't know where I was going with that."

Then continue. Laughing at yourself once is charming. Apologising three times is what makes it awkward.

A one-week practice plan

Awkwardness fades with repetition. Use small, low-stakes conversations to practice one habit at a time.

  1. Day 1–2: Use the one-detail rule with everyone who asks you a question: cashier, colleague, neighbour.
  2. Day 3–4: In each conversation, ask one follow-up question about something they said.
  3. Day 5: Let one pause happen without filling it. Notice that nothing bad happens.
  4. Day 6: End two conversations with a clear exit line.
  5. Day 7: Record yourself answering "How was your week?" out loud and listen back. Is there one detail and one question?

Practicing out loud matters because awkwardness lives in timing, not words. The free conversation drill lets you answer prompts out loud and see your pace and filler words. When it's more than awkwardness

If conversations cause you intense fear, or you avoid people because of anxiety, that may be social anxiety, and it's worth talking to a doctor or a therapist. Practice tips help many people, but they aren't a replacement for proper support.

Questions people ask

Why am I so awkward in conversations?

Usually because your attention is on yourself (how you're coming across) instead of on the other person. A few habits, like follow-up questions and answering with one detail, move your attention back outward.

How do I stop being awkward around new people?

Prepare one opener and one follow-up question, answer questions with a small detail, and plan an exit line. Practice in short, low-stakes moments first.

Can you train yourself to be less awkward?

Yes. Conversation is a set of skills. Repeating a few specific moves, out loud, in real conversations, makes them automatic over a few weeks.

What do I do after saying something awkward?

Acknowledge it lightly once ("That came out wrong") and keep going. Most people forget it in seconds unless you keep apologising.

Is being awkward the same as social anxiety?

Not necessarily. Many people feel awkward without having social anxiety. If fear stops you from doing things you want to do, talk to a doctor or therapist.
