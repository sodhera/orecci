How to Change the Subject Smoothly in a Conversation

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

To change the subject smoothly, acknowledge what was just said in a few words, then bridge to a new topic, ideally one linked to something already mentioned: "That sounds stressful. Speaking of the move, did you find a new gym?" For sensitive topics, you can be direct and kind: "I'd rather not get into that today. How's your sister doing?"

Why some subject changes feel rude

An abrupt switch, from someone's story about their dog straight to "So, how about that football?", tells the other person you weren't listening, or that their topic bored you. That's why changing the subject can feel risky.

The fix is a bridge. A good subject change has two parts:

  1. Acknowledge what was just said. A few words is enough: "Wow, that sounds stressful."
  2. Connect to the new topic, using a link if you can find one.

The acknowledgement says "I heard you". The link makes the new topic feel like a natural next step rather than an escape.

Five ways to bridge

TechniqueHow it worksExample
The linked wordPick up one word or idea and take it somewhere else"Speaking of Spain, have you tried the new tapas place?"
The question to someone elseBring in another person with a new thread"Maya, didn't you just get back from a trip?"
The callbackReturn to something mentioned earlier"Going back to what you said about the new job, how's the team?"
The honest pivotSay you're changing topic"Totally random, but I've been meaning to ask you about your garden."
The environmentUse something around you"Is it just me, or is this cheese amazing?"

The linked word is the smoothest, because it feels like the conversation moved on its own. The honest pivot works better than you'd think. Saying "totally random" or "changing the subject" openly is friendly, not rude.

Changing the subject by situation

Boring topic, friendly person

You don't need to escape; you just want somewhere more interesting. Bridge from their topic to a related, more personal one:

They've talked about their commute for five minutes. "It sounds exhausting. What do you do to unwind when you finally get home?"

Uncomfortable personal question

Someone asks about your salary, weight, relationship status or a health issue. You can answer briefly and move on, or decline kindly:

"Ha, still single, still happy. How's your new place coming along?"

"I'd rather not get into that today. Tell me about your trip, though."

You owe no one an explanation. A light tone and a clear new topic usually do the job.

Heated topic: politics, religion, family conflict

When a conversation is getting tense, acknowledge the strength of feeling, then move on firmly:

"I can tell we see this differently, and that's okay. I don't want to argue over dinner. Did anyone watch the match last night?"

"Let's park that one. It's a holiday. Who wants more pie?"

If it's a group setting, a question to another person is especially useful, because it moves attention away from the two people disagreeing.

Gossip about someone not there

"I don't really know the full story there. Anyway, how's your project going?"

This avoids joining in without making a speech about gossip.

At work: a meeting drifting off track

"That's a good point, and worth its own conversation. Can we come back to the budget question so we finish on time?"

"Let's take that offline. Sam and I can pick it up after. Next item?"

In meetings, refer back to the purpose of the meeting. It gives your redirect authority without being personal. If you need to break in first, see how to politely interrupt.

If they bring it back

Sometimes the person returns to the topic. Escalate gently:

  1. Second time: "Ha, you really want to talk about this!" and redirect again, with a smile.
  2. Third time: be direct and calm: "I'd really rather not talk about my job search today. I'll let you know when there's news."
  3. Still going: step away. "I'm going to grab a drink." Use a polite exit. You've given them enough chances.

Having topics ready

Changing the subject is much easier when you have somewhere to go. Before a family gathering or work event, think of three or four safe topics that fit the people there: upcoming plans, a recent trip, a shared show, someone's new pet, the food. Questions people ask

How do you change the subject without being rude?

Acknowledge what they said in a few words, then bridge to a new topic, ideally one linked to something already mentioned.

What do you say to change the subject?

Phrases like "Speaking of…", "That reminds me…", "Going back to what you said earlier…" or the honest "Totally random, but…" all work.

How do you avoid a question you don't want to answer?

Give a short, light answer and move on, or say "I'd rather not get into that today" and ask them something.

Is it rude to change the subject?

Not if you acknowledge what was said first. It's only rude when it's abrupt or you do it every time the other person speaks.

How do I stop someone talking about politics?

Acknowledge the different views, say you'd rather not debate it today, and ask a question about something else, ideally to another person in the group.
