# How to Answer 'What Do You Do?' Without Being Boring

Answer "What do you do?" in three short parts: what you do in plain words, one specific or surprising detail, and something that hands the conversation back, often a question. For example: "I'm a nurse on a children's ward. Last week a six-year-old taught me a card trick. What about you?" It works whether you have a job title or not.

## Why the usual answer goes nowhere

"I'm an accountant." "Oh, nice." Silence. The trouble isn't your job. It's that a bare title gives the other person nothing to grab. They have to invent a follow-up question, and most people can't think of one for a job they don't understand.

At a party, "What do you do?" is rarely about your career. It's a handshake: a way of saying "tell me something about you". Your answer is good if it gives them an easy next line.

## The three-part answer

1. **Plain words:** what you do, the way you'd say it to a 12-year-old. Skip internal jargon and acronyms.
2. **One hook:** a specific detail, a recent moment, or the part you like. This is what they'll ask about.
3. **Hand it back:** a question, or a pause that clearly invites one.

Aim for about 10 to 15 seconds, or two sentences. Long enough to give a hook, short enough that it stays a conversation, not a pitch. If you need the work version for a networking event, see the [elevator pitch guide](/speaking-coach/presentations/elevator-pitch/) instead; that's a different job.

## Example answers by situation

These are examples of the shape. Swap in your own details.

| Situation | Flat answer | Answer with a hook |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Office job | "I work in finance." | "I do the budgets for a group of hospitals, so I'm the person who says no to new chairs. What do you do?" |
| Technical job | "I'm a backend engineer." | "I build the bit of a banking app you never see, the part that makes sure your payment actually goes through. Mostly I fix things at odd hours." |
| Student | "I'm just a student." | "I'm in my second year of geography. Right now I'm mapping flood risk in my home town, which is more exciting than it sounds." |
| Stay-at-home parent | "I'm just at home with the kids." | "I'm at home with our two-year-old at the moment. My main job this week has been negotiating with someone who only eats yellow food." |
| Between jobs | "I'm unemployed." | "I'm between jobs. I left logistics in the spring and I'm looking at moving into project management. Any chance you know that world?" |
| Self-employed | "I'm a freelancer." | "I run a small bakery stall at weekend markets. Sourdough, mostly. I'm up at four on Saturdays." |
| Retired | "Oh, I'm retired." | "I retired from teaching last year. Now I volunteer at the library and I'm finally learning Spanish." |
| Job that's hard to explain | "It's complicated." | "Short version: I help companies figure out why customers cancel. Long version involves a lot of spreadsheets." |

Notice the word "just" disappears. "I'm just a student" or "just a mum" tells the other person there's nothing to ask about. Drop it.

## If you'd rather not talk about work

Maybe you're between jobs and tired of explaining, or your work is dull to you, or it's confidential. You can answer briefly and move to something you *do* want to talk about:

> "I work in insurance, which honestly is the least interesting thing about me. Right now I'm mostly obsessed with my allotment."

> "I'm between things at the moment. The bigger news is I just got back from two weeks walking in Portugal."

> "Office stuff, mostly. Outside work, I play in a five-a-side team that loses every week."

This isn't dodging. It's choosing the hook. People will follow you to whichever topic you sound most alive about.

## When you're unemployed or job hunting

This is the version that worries people most. A few things help:

- **Use a forward-looking phrase:** "between jobs", "looking for my next role in…", "taking a few months out after…". Then name the field.
- **Keep it to one sentence.** You don't owe a stranger the story of your redundancy.
- **Turn it into a light ask, if you like:** "Do you know anyone in events?" Most people enjoy helping, and you might get a lead.
- **Then talk about something else.** What you're doing with the time, a course, a project, a trip.

If it comes up in a job interview rather than at a party, it's a different question. See [why is there a gap in your resume](/speaking-coach/interview-questions/why-is-there-a-gap-in-your-resume/).

## Asking it back without being dull

Once you've answered, you'll usually ask the same thing back. To get a better answer than their job title, try a version with more room in it:

- "What keeps you busy these days?"
- "What's taking up most of your time at the moment?"
- "What's the best part of that?" (after they name the job)
- "How did you end up doing that?"

The last two are especially good follow-ups. Almost everyone has a story about how they got into their work, and most have a favourite part.

## Mistakes to avoid

| Mistake | Why it falls flat | Try instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Title only | No hook, conversation stalls | Add one specific detail |
| Jargon or acronyms | They can't follow, so they can't ask | Plain words, as for a child |
| A two-minute career summary | Feels like a pitch | Two sentences, then hand it back |
| Apologising ("it's boring, sorry") | Gives them nothing and lowers the mood | Pick the most interesting part, even if small |
| Never asking back | The chat becomes an interview | End with "What about you?" |

## Practise it before you need it

You'll be asked this hundreds of times, so it's worth getting one answer you like and saying it aloud until it's easy. Try it a few times in the [small talk drill](/speaking-coach/tools/small-talk-drill/), which shows your pace and filler words, and listen for whether the hook comes out clearly. In the Speaking Coach app, the "Meet someone new" scene has a partner who asks about you and follows up on what you say, which is a good test of whether your hook gives them something to ask about.

## Questions people ask

### How do you answer "what do you do" if you're unemployed?

Say it in one forward-looking sentence, such as "I'm between jobs and looking at roles in marketing", then move to something you're doing with your time. No need for the backstory.

### Why do people ask "what do you do"?

Mostly as a friendly opener. It's a quick way to learn something about you, not a judgement. A detail beyond the job title gives them more to work with.

### Is it rude to ask someone what they do?

Not usually, but some people find it tiring. "What keeps you busy these days?" asks the same thing with more room for non-work answers.

### How long should my answer be?

About two sentences, or 10 to 15 seconds. Then hand it back with a question or a pause.

### What if my job is hard to explain?

Describe the problem you solve or who you help, not the method. "I help shops work out which products to reorder" beats a technical description.
