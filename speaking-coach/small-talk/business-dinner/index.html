How to Make Conversation at a Business Dinner With Clients

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

At a business dinner, keep the first part of the evening personal and light (travel, the city, food, families if they bring them up), and save business for after the main course, or let the most senior client raise it. If you're hosting, your job is to make guests comfortable: introduce people, guide the ordering and bring quiet guests in. Aim to listen more than you talk.

What a business dinner is really for

Most business dinners are not for closing anything. They're for trust: the client wants to know what you're like as a person, and whether working with you will be easy. That means the conversation is the point, and the food is the excuse.

So the main question isn't "What do I say about the deal?" but "How do I make two hours pleasant for everyone at the table?"

The shape of the evening

StageRoughlyWhat to talk about
Arrival and drinks10–20 minTravel, the venue, the city, their week
Ordering5–10 minThe menu, food preferences, recommendations
Starters and main40–60 minPersonal-but-safe topics, shared interests
Dessert and coffee20–30 minBusiness, lightly, if it fits
Leaving5 minThanks, next steps, getting home

This is a common pattern, not a rule. Read the senior client. If they start on business with the drinks, follow them.

Safe topics that build rapport

  • Travel: "Did you fly in today? How was it?" or "Where's the best place you've been for work?"
  • The city: "Have you had a chance to see much of the city?" Offer a recommendation if they're visiting.
  • Food: "Do you have a favourite cuisine? I'd love a recommendation back home."
  • Their career path: "How did you end up in logistics? It's not a field people plan for." People enjoy telling this story.
  • Interests outside work: sport, books, running, a hobby they mention. Follow their lead.
  • Families, only if they bring them up: "How old are your kids?" is fine once they've mentioned them.

The best material is something they said: "You mentioned you've just come back from Lisbon. Was that for work?" That shows you were listening, which is the core of building rapport.

When and how to talk business

Wait for a natural moment, usually after the main course, and keep it light. A bridge line helps:

"While we're all here, I'd love to hear how the rollout is landing with your team."

"No need to get into detail tonight, but is there anything you'd like us to do differently next quarter?"

If the client clearly doesn't want to talk shop, let it go. "We can pick that up next week" is a perfectly good answer. Never bring out a laptop or papers at the table unless they ask.

If you're the host

The host has a job beyond conversation:

  1. Arrive early and greet each guest at the door or the table.
  2. Introduce people with a hook: "Anna, this is Raj. Raj runs the Singapore office and has just finished a marathon."
  3. Guide ordering: suggest a dish or two, and order at a similar level to your guests so nobody feels awkward.
  4. Bring in quiet guests with a specific question by name.
  5. Watch the time. Wrap up before people get tired: "I know some of you have an early start."
  6. Handle the bill quietly, ideally arranged with the restaurant ahead of time.

If you're a guest

Let the host lead the agenda. Follow their cues on drinks, courses and when to talk business. Thank them at the end and follow up with a short message the next day.

Getting comfortable before the dinner

If you're new to client dinners, prepare three things: two questions about the client's life (from what you already know), one short story about yourself that isn't about work, and one bridge line for business. Questions people ask

When should you bring up business at a business dinner?

Usually after the main course, or when the client raises it. Use a light bridge line and keep it short; the dinner is mostly about the relationship.

What should you not talk about at a business dinner?

Politics, religion, money, complaints about your company, gossip about other clients or competitors, and anything too personal.

How do I keep the conversation going with a quiet client?

Ask about something they mentioned earlier, their career path or their travel. Open questions like "How did you end up in this field?" usually get people talking.

How much should I drink at a business dinner?

Less than the guests, if you drink at all. It's fine to order soft drinks; you don't need to explain.

Who pays at a business dinner?

The host, which is usually the person who invited. Arrange it with the restaurant in advance so the bill never comes to the table.
