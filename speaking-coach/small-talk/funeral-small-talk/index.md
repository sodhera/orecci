# What to Say at a Funeral or Wake: Talking to Family and Guests

To the family, keep it short and sincere: "I'm so sorry. I'll really miss her", or a single specific memory, then let them lead. With other guests at a funeral or wake, ask how they knew the person who died and share a story of your own. You don't need perfect words; being there and saying something simple matters more than what you say.

## The short version

Most people worry about saying the wrong thing at a funeral. In practice, the most common mistake is saying nothing at all, or avoiding the family because you don't know what to say. A few simple, sincere words are enough. Grieving people rarely remember the exact words. They remember that you came and spoke to them.

Customs differ between cultures and religions, so follow the lead of the family and the setting. The suggestions below are general and adapt to most situations.

## What to say to the family

Keep it to two or three sentences. They'll be speaking to many people, often while exhausted.

1. **Acknowledge the loss:** "I'm so sorry about your dad."
2. **Say who you are, if they might not know:** "I'm Ana. I worked with him at the depot for eight years."
3. **Add one warm thing, if you have it:** a memory, a quality, something they did for you.
4. **Let them lead.** If they want to talk, listen. If they say "thank you" and look to the next person, that's your cue to step aside.

### Example condolence lines

These are examples to adapt. Use your own words where you can.

| Your relationship | What you might say |
| --- | --- |
| Close friend of the person | "I'm so sorry. I'm going to miss her so much. She was the first person I called with good news." |
| Coworker | "I'm so sorry for your loss. Your mum made our office a kinder place. Everyone's talking about her today." |
| Neighbor | "I'm sorry. He waved at us every single morning. The street feels different without him." |
| You knew the family, not the person | "I'm so sorry. I'm here for you. How are you holding up today?" |
| You didn't know them well at all | "I'm so sorry for your loss. I'm thinking of you all." |
| You don't know what to say | "I don't really have the words. I'm just so sorry." |

The last line is honest and completely fine. Admitting you have no words is often more comforting than reaching for the perfect phrase.

### A specific memory is the best gift

If you have one, share a short, specific memory. Families often hear "he was a great guy" all day. A detail they didn't know means a lot:

> "When I started at the firm, he stayed late every night for a week to teach me the system. He never mentioned it to anyone."

Keep it to one or two sentences unless they ask for more. Save long stories for the reception, if they want to hear them.

## What not to say

| Avoid | Why | Instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "I know exactly how you feel" | Every loss is different | "I can't imagine how hard this is." |
| "Everything happens for a reason" | Can feel dismissive | "I'm so sorry." |
| "At least they're not suffering" / "At least they had a long life" | "At least" minimises the loss | Talk about the person instead |
| Asking how they died | Intrusive; they may not want to relive it | Let them share if they choose |
| "Let me know if you need anything" (alone) | Puts the work on them | Offer something specific: "Can I drop off dinner on Thursday?" |
| Long stories about your own losses | Shifts the focus to you | One sentence of connection at most |

If you've said something clumsy, a simple "Sorry, that came out wrong. I'm just so sorry for your loss" is enough. Don't over-explain.

## Talking to other guests

At the wake, reception or gathering afterwards, conversation is often lighter than people expect. Sharing memories, and even laughing, is normal and often welcome. The easiest opener is the person you're all there for:

- "How did you know Margaret?"
- "Did you work with him too?"
- "I loved what her granddaughter said in the eulogy."
- "I didn't know he'd lived in Canada. Did you know him then?"

Then share your own connection and a memory. Stories about the person who died are the natural topic, and families often like overhearing them.

If you sit with people you don't know and the person who died hasn't come up, gentle neutral topics are fine: how far people travelled, the venue, family members in common, the service. Avoid work networking, politics, and anything about the estate or the will.

## When conversation stalls or turns hard

| Situation | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| Silence with another guest | It's okay. Quiet is normal here. Or: "It was a lovely service, wasn't it?" |
| Someone starts crying | Stay with them. "Take your time." A hand on the arm, if appropriate. You don't need to fix it. |
| Someone shares a family conflict | Listen, don't take sides. "That sounds really hard." |
| A guest gets loud or inappropriate | Step away politely; it's not your job to manage it |
| You need to leave | Find the family if you can: "Thank you for having me. I'm thinking of you." |

For more on quiet moments, see the [awkward silence guide](/speaking-coach/small-talk/awkward-silence/). At a funeral, though, a silence is rarely awkward. People understand.

## After the funeral

Grief goes on long after the service, when the cards stop arriving. A short message a few weeks later, ("Thinking of you today. I still smile when I remember the barbecue he hosted") can mean as much as anything said on the day. Specific offers of help, like a meal, a lift, or an hour of childcare, are often more useful than general ones.

If you've been asked to speak at the service itself, that's a different task. The [eulogy guide](/speaking-coach/speeches/eulogy/) walks through writing and delivering one.

## Questions people ask

### What do you say to the family at a funeral?

Something short and sincere: "I'm so sorry for your loss", plus who you are and one warm memory if you have one. Then let them lead.

### Is it okay to laugh at a wake?

Yes, usually. Sharing funny stories about the person is common and often comforting. Follow the family's lead on the mood.

### What should I avoid saying at a funeral?

Avoid "everything happens for a reason", "at least…", "I know how you feel", and asking how they died.

### What if I didn't know the person well?

Go anyway if you knew the family. "I'm so sorry. I'm here for you" is enough. Your presence is what counts.

### How long should I talk to the family?

Usually under a minute at the service, since many people are waiting. If they want to talk more, stay and listen.
