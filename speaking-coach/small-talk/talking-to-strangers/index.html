How to Talk to Strangers Without Feeling Weird or Awkward

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Start with a one-line comment about something you're both seeing, like the wait, the weather or a dog, and expect nothing more than a smile back. If they answer with more than a word or ask something back, they're open to chatting; if not, you've lost nothing. Doing this often is what makes it stop feeling weird.

Lower the stakes: aim for one line, not a conversation

Talking to strangers feels weird mostly because people treat it as a big ask. You're not trying to make a friend or get a phone number. You're making one friendly remark, the kind you'd make to a neighbour.

Think of it as a "one-and-done" comment:

  • At the bakery queue: "Everything here looks too good. I never know what to pick."
  • At the park, to a dog owner: "Your dog is having the best day."
  • On a delayed train: "Well, at least it's not raining on the platform."

If they smile and say "Ha, yeah," that's a complete, successful exchange. If they say more, you have a conversation. Either way, nothing was at risk.

Low-stakes places to practice

Some places make talking to strangers much easier than others, because there's a shared moment and a natural end.

PlaceWhy it worksExample line
A queueShared wait, built-in end when the line moves"Is this the line for both counters?"
A cafe counterShared choice, staff and customers expect chat"What's the one you always get here?"
A park or dog parkDogs, kids and weather give easy topics"How old is he? He's got so much energy."
A bookshop or marketPeople are browsing, not rushing"Have you read anything by her? I'm deciding between two."
A class or workshopSame reason to be there"Is this your first session too?"
Waiting for a delayed bus or trainShared mild annoyance"Any idea what's going on? The board just went blank."

Harder places: people rushing, people on headphones, people eating alone with a book, late at night on empty streets. Not off limits, but people there are more likely to want to be left alone.

How to tell if they want to chat

You can usually tell within the first reply. Look for signals, not words alone.

Open to chattingNot right now
Turns toward youKeeps facing away or stays angled toward the exit
Answers with a full sentenceGives one word and looks back at their phone
Asks you something backDoesn't ask anything back
Smiles and holds eye contactPolite smile, then looks away quickly
Takes out an earbudLeaves both earbuds in

One closed signal isn't a no; people are sometimes just caught off guard. Two or three together usually mean "not now." Respect it: "Have a good one," and turn back to what you were doing. Leaving quickly and kindly is part of being good at this.

Turning a comment into a short chat

If they respond warmly, add a second line that gives them more to work with. The easiest is a follow-up on whatever they said.

Example in a cafe:

You: "What's the one you always get here?" Them: "The cardamom bun. It's ridiculous." You: "Okay, sold. Are you a regular? I just moved nearby and I'm still finding my places." Them: "Pretty much every Saturday. Where did you move from?"

Now you're talking. You followed up, shared one small fact about yourself, and asked a question. That's enough for a two-minute chat. When the line moves or your coffee arrives, end it simply: "Thanks for the tip. Enjoy your bun."

A one-week plan to get comfortable

If talking to strangers makes you anxious, build up in small steps. Each day, do one thing slightly harder than the day before.

  1. Day 1: Make eye contact and nod or smile at three people.
  2. Day 2: Say thank you to someone serving you, and add one detail: "Thanks, the music in here is great."
  3. Day 3: Make one comment about a shared situation in a queue.
  4. Day 4: Ask one stranger for a small recommendation: a dish, a book, a route.
  5. Day 5: Make a comment and follow up on their reply once.
  6. Day 6: Have a chat of about two minutes with someone in a low-stakes place.
  7. Day 7: Notice what felt easiest and do more of that.

It's normal for some of these to go nowhere. The aim is repetition, not success every time. After a week, saying one line to a stranger usually feels much less strange.

Practice before you're in public

Saying lines out loud at home helps more than you'd expect. Your first "Is this the line for both counters?" will feel stiff. By the tenth, it's ordinary. Questions people ask

Is it rude to talk to strangers?

No, as long as you keep it short and respect signals that they'd rather not talk. A friendly comment in a queue or park is normal in most places. In some cultures and cities people chat less in public, so let them lead.

What do I say to a stranger first?

A short comment on something you both see: the wait, the weather, a dog, the menu. Follow it with an easy question only if they seem open.

How do I talk to strangers when I have social anxiety?

Start with the smallest step: eye contact and a smile, then a thank-you with one detail. Build up slowly. If anxiety stops you from doing daily things, a doctor or therapist can help more than any tips page.

How do I end a chat with a stranger?

Use the natural end: the line moving, your stop, your coffee arriving. "Nice chatting, enjoy your day" is enough.

Is it okay to talk to someone wearing headphones?

Usually leave them be. If you need to ask something practical, catch their eye and wait for them to take an earbud out.
