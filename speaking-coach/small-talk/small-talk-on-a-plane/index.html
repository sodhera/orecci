How to Talk to the Person Next to You on a Plane (or Not)

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Say a quick hello when you sit down and, if you want to chat, use a light opener about the trip ("Heading home or away?"). Their answer tells you whether to continue. On a long flight, chat for a few minutes, then close it kindly: "I'm going to try to sleep, but nice talking with you." Headphones, a book or closed eyes are a polite, clear signal.

The seatmate rule: easy in, easy out

Plane conversations are different from most small talk because you can't walk away. That's why many people avoid them, and why the most important skill isn't the opener. It's the exit. If you know you can end it politely, starting a chat feels much safer.

The polite minimum

Even if you don't want to talk, a little contact makes the flight easier for both of you:

  1. A hello as you sit down: "Hi, I'm in the window, sorry to squeeze past."
  2. Sorting the shared space: "Do you want the armrest? I'm happy either way."
  3. Small courtesies during the flight: "Do you need to get out? I'll stand up."
  4. A goodbye at landing: "Have a good trip."

That's all you owe anyone. Everything else is optional.

How to tell if they want to chat

SignalWhat it suggests
Headphones in or book open straight awayPrefers quiet
Eye mask, pillow, settling in to sleepDefinitely prefers quiet

Looking around, not busy with anythingPossibly open
Short answers with a polite smileFriendly but would rather not talk
Comments on the delay, the food or the viewInviting a chat

Try one light line. If you get a full answer and a question back, keep going. If you get one word, smile and let it go. No hard feelings.

Openers that work on a plane

Good plane openers are about the trip you're both on:

"Heading home, or away?"

"Is this your final stop, or are you connecting?"

"Have you been to Lisbon before? It's my first time."

"Well, that was a long boarding. Glad to finally sit down."

If they're travelling for something interesting (a wedding, a conference, a long trip), follow it up: "Whose wedding? Are you in it?" Travel stories are easy to share and easy to stop.

What to talk about once you're chatting

  • Where you're each going and why.
  • Recommendations for the destination: "If you only had one day there, what would you do?"
  • Travel stories: best trips, worst delays.
  • Light work talk, if it comes up naturally.
  • The flight itself: the film selection, the food, the turbulence (lightly, if they seem calm).

Avoid topics that could start an argument. You have hours left together in a small space.

How to end it politely, especially on a long flight

On a long flight, a few minutes of chat is plenty. The key is to close warmly and clearly, so they don't wonder whether to start again.

"It's been really nice chatting. I'm going to try to sleep for a bit."

"I've got some work I need to finish before we land, but enjoy the rest of the flight."

"I'm going to start a film. Let me know if you need to get out."

Then do what you said: put your headphones on, open your laptop or close your eyes. That's the clear signal.

If the other person keeps talking after your line, repeat it once more, kindly and a bit more directly: "Sorry, I really do need to get some rest. It was great to meet you." Then put your headphones in.

If you're the one who wants quiet from the start

You don't need to explain much. A friendly line at the start sets expectations:

"Hi! Fair warning, I'm going to be asleep about five minutes after takeoff."

Most people are glad to know. A smile at landing keeps it friendly.

Mistakes to avoid

MistakeWhy it's a problemInstead
Pushing on after short answersThey feel trapped for hoursOne try, then let it go
Talking across someone wearing headphonesThey have to take them off to be politeWait for a natural break, like the meal
Starting a deep or heavy topic earlyHard to exit when you're stuck togetherKeep it light
Ending abruptly with no explanationCan feel like a rejectionOne warm closing line
Oversharing personal detailsYou don't know who's listeningKeep it general

Practice the exit line

The exit is the part most people never practice. Say your closing line out loud a few times before your next flight so it comes out warm instead of abrupt. Questions people ask

Is it rude not to talk to the person next to you on a plane?

No. A hello, help with shared space and a goodbye are enough. Many travellers prefer a quiet flight.

How do you start a conversation on a plane?

Ask about their trip: "Heading home or away?" or "Is this your final stop?" Then follow their answer.

How do I stop someone from talking to me on a plane?

Use a warm closing line ("I'm going to try to sleep, nice chatting") and then put headphones on or close your eyes. Repeat it once, kindly, if needed.

Should you talk to someone on a long flight?

A few minutes of chat can make the flight nicer, but most people prefer to rest or watch something after that. Chat briefly, then close the conversation clearly.

What if my seatmate seems nervous about flying?

A calm, friendly chat can help. Keep it light, talk about their destination and avoid dwelling on the flight itself unless they want to.
