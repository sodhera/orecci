How to Make Small Talk at a Wedding Table Full of Strangers

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Open with "How do you know the couple?" and answer it yourself, then follow the thread their answer gives you: a shared school, a job, a city, a story about the couple. Use the day itself for easy topics (the ceremony, the venue, the food), and bring in anyone who has gone quiet. You only need to carry about two hours, with speeches and dancing doing the rest.

Why the wedding table is easier than it looks

A table of strangers sounds hard, but it comes with three gifts: everyone has a reason to be there, everyone just saw the same ceremony, and the evening has built-in breaks (courses, speeches, the first dance). You aren't making conversation from nothing for hours. You're filling short gaps between events.

Your first 60 seconds at the table

  1. Introduce yourself to both neighbours as you sit down, not ten minutes later: "Hi, I'm Priya. I don't think we've met."
  2. Ask the default question: "How do you know Tom and Leah?"
  3. Answer it yourself, with one detail: "I worked with Leah in Manchester for three years. She trained me, actually."
  4. Say hello across the table with a nod or a quick "Hi, I'm Priya" if it's a round table. You can talk properly later.

That detail in step 3 matters. "We worked together" ends the topic. "She trained me" gives the other person something to ask about.

Where to go after "How do you know the couple?"

Their answer almost always gives you a next question. Listen for it.

If they say…Follow up with…
"We were at university together""What was Tom like back then?" or "What did you study?"
"I'm Leah's cousin""Is the family all here? Who should I know about?"
"We work together""What do you both do?" then move away from work after a minute
"I'm the plus-one""How long have you two been together? Have you met many people here?"
"We met through climbing""How long have you been climbing? Where do you go?"

Stories about the couple are the best material at a wedding. "How did they meet? I've only heard Tom's version" usually gets the whole table talking.

Easy topics from the day itself

When a thread runs out, reach for what you all just shared:

  • The ceremony: "Did the vows get to you too? I didn't expect to cry."
  • The venue: "Have you been here before? It's beautiful."
  • Travel: "Did you come far today?" People who travelled always have something to say.
  • The food: "Did you go for the fish or the beef?"
  • What's next: "Are you a dancer, or are you staying safely at the table?"

Then go one step more personal once there's a little warmth: "Are you staying around here this weekend, or heading straight back?" leads to work, home and plans naturally.

Bringing the whole table in

At round tables, conversation tends to split into pairs. A good guest pulls it back together once or twice.

"Wait, Marco, you went to university with Tom as well, didn't you? You two must have overlapped."

"We were just guessing who'll be first on the dance floor. Any bets?"

If someone has been quiet, ask them something easy and specific by name: "Hannah, did you come from London today too?" Don't put them on the spot with "You're quiet!"

Handling the quieter stretches

Pauses at a wedding table are normal, especially while food arrives or before speeches. You don't need to fill every gap. When you want to restart, use what's in front of you: "That cake looks serious. Do we know who made it?" For more lines, see what to say when a conversation goes quiet.

If you're giving a toast later, the table chat is a good warm-up. Our guide to giving a wedding toast as a guest covers what to say when it's your turn.

Practice before the day

If a table of strangers makes you anxious, say your answer to "How do you know the couple?" out loud a few times before you go, with one detail and one question back. Questions people ask

What do you say to strangers at a wedding table?

Start with your name and "How do you know the couple?", answer it yourself with one detail, then follow up on their answer. The ceremony, the venue and travel are easy topics after that.

Is it okay to leave the table and talk to others?

Yes, between courses and once speeches are done. Say "I'm going to grab a drink, can I get you anything?" so it doesn't feel like you're escaping.

What if I'm seated next to someone I don't get along with?

Keep it polite and short, and talk across the table or to your other neighbour more. You only need to be pleasant for a few hours.

What if I only know the couple and nobody else?

That's the most common situation. Everyone else at a mixed table is in a similar position, and "How do you know them?" works for everyone.

Should I talk about the couple's speeches?

Yes, kindly. "That best man speech was lovely" or "I didn't know that story about the camping trip" is easy common ground.
