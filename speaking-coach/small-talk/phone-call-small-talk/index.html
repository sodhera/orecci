How to Make Small Talk on a Phone Call Without Video Cues

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

On a phone call, keep the opening small talk short, about 30 to 60 seconds for work calls, and make your voice do the work your face would: sound warm, say their name, and react out loud ("Oh, nice!") so they know you're listening. Pause a beat longer than you would in person before speaking to avoid talking over each other, then signal the move to business: "So, I wanted to talk about…"

What's different on the phone

In person or on video, you constantly read faces: a smile, a nod, a look that means "your turn". On the phone, all of that is gone. That causes three common problems:

  • Talking over each other, because you can't see that they're about to speak.
  • Silences that feel longer and stranger than they would in a room.
  • Sounding flat, because your smile and expressions don't come through on their own.

Phone small talk works when your voice and words carry what your face normally would.

Openers for different calls

Call typeOpener
Calling a colleague"Hi Sam, it's Priya. Have you got a minute? How's your week going?"
First call with a client"Hi, Ms Patel, it's Leo from Brightline. Thanks for making time. How's your Tuesday treating you?"
They called you"Hi, Chris! Good to hear from you. How are things?"
Calling family or a friend"Hey! Is now a good time? I've been meaning to call all week."
A scheduled call that starts slowly"Hi, can you hear me okay? Great. Where are you calling from today?"
A call with someone in another city"What's the weather like where you are? It's pouring here."

Asking "Is now a good time?" or "Have you got a minute?" matters more on the phone than in person. You can't see that they're in a car or cooking.

Making your voice do the work

  1. Smile when you say hello. It warms up the sound of your voice.
  2. Use their name early. It sounds warm and personal.
  3. React out loud. On video, a nod is enough. On the phone, you need small sounds and words: "Oh, nice," "Mm-hm," "Really?" Otherwise the other person wonders if you're still there.
  4. Vary your tone. A flat voice sounds bored on the phone even when you're not. The monotone guide has simple exercises.
  5. Slow down a little. Phone audio can be patchy. A calmer pace is easier to follow. You can check your words per minute with the pace checker.

How not to talk over each other

Phone calls often have a small delay, especially on mobile or internet calls. Two people start talking at once, both stop, both say "Sorry, go ahead".

  • Wait a beat after they stop before you start, a bit longer than in person.
  • Signal the end of your turn with a question. "How about you?" clearly hands it over.
  • If you clash, give way quickly and with warmth: "Sorry, you go." Then actually wait.
  • Don't fill tiny pauses. A one-second silence is normal on the phone.

Small talk topics that suit voice-only calls

Choose topics that don't need visuals and that the other person can answer in a sentence or two:

  • Their week or day: "Busy week?" followed by "What's keeping you busy?"
  • Weather and location: "Are you in the office today or at home?"
  • Upcoming plans: "Anything good planned for the weekend?"
  • Something you know about them: "How did the conference go?"
  • Shared context: "Did you get through all those emails after the holiday?"

Remembering a detail from your last call is especially strong on the phone. "Did your son's team win the final?" shows you listened when they couldn't see you nodding.

Moving from small talk to business

On a work call, small talk is the warm-up, not the meeting. Aim for about 30 to 60 seconds, unless the other person clearly wants to chat. Then signal the change so it doesn't feel abrupt:

"Great to hear. So, I wanted to talk about the delivery dates."

"Glad the trip went well. I know you're busy, so let me get to why I'm calling."

"I'll let you get back to your day soon. The main thing I need is…"

On sales calls, the right amount of small talk depends on the person; see sales call small talk. On a phone interview, keep it brief and follow the interviewer's lead; the phone interview guide covers that.

Handling silence

A silence on the phone feels longer than one in a room, because you can't see the other person thinking. Things that help:

SituationWhat to do
They're thinkingGive them a few seconds; don't jump in
You need a momentSay so: "Let me think about that for a second."
You're not sure the line is working"Sorry, did I lose you?"
The small talk has run outMove to business, or close the call
You're looking something up"Bear with me, I'm just pulling that up."

Narrating what you're doing ("I'm just checking my calendar") replaces the visual cue they'd normally see.

Ending the call warmly

Endings on the phone can drag, with lots of "Okay, bye, yes, bye". Close clearly:

"Thanks so much, Sam. I'll send that over today. Have a good afternoon."

Summarising the next step, then a warm goodbye, ends it cleanly.

Questions people ask

How do you make small talk on the phone?

Smile as you greet them, use their name, ask an easy open question about their day or week, and react out loud to what they say.

How long should small talk be on a work call?

Usually 30 to 60 seconds. Follow their lead: if they want to chat more, do; if they sound busy, get to the point.

How do I stop talking over people on the phone?

Wait a beat longer than you would in person, end your turns with a question, and give way quickly if you clash.

How do I sound friendly on the phone?

Smile while you talk, vary your tone, use their name, and add small reactions like "Oh, nice" so they know you're listening.

What do you say to move from small talk to business?

Something like "So, I wanted to talk about…" or "Let me get to why I'm calling." A clear signal keeps it from feeling abrupt.
