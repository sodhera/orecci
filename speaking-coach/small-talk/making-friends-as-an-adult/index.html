How to Make Friends as an Adult: Turning Chats Into Friendships

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

Adult friendships rarely start in one conversation. They grow from seeing the same person repeatedly, remembering what they told you, and then making one small, specific invite such as "I'm going to that market on Saturday, want to come?". The invite is the step most people skip, so make it, keep it low-stakes, and follow up once if they say yes but nothing happens.

Why it feels harder than it used to

At school and university, friendship came with a timetable. You saw the same people every day without planning it, and closeness built up on its own. As an adult, that built-in repetition mostly disappears. You meet plenty of nice people once, chat for ten minutes, and never see them again.

So the fix is not being more charming in the first chat. It is two things:

  • Repetition: put yourself where the same people turn up every week.
  • Escalation: at some point, one of you has to say "we should do something outside this". Usually nobody does. Be the one who does.

Step 1: Pick a place you'll go back to

One-off events (a big party, a conference) are fine for practice but poor for friendship. Look for something with the same faces each week:

Choose one and commit to at least six or eight sessions. Most people need several meetings before an invite feels natural.

Step 2: Make the second conversation better than the first

The first chat is small talk. The second one is where friendship starts, because it shows you were listening. Open with a callback to something they said last time:

"Hey, Priya. How did the flat viewing go?"

"Did you end up watching that series you mentioned?"

"Last week you said you were new to the area. Found a decent coffee place yet?"

Remembering one detail tells someone "you're not a stranger to me any more". If names and details slip, write a note in your phone straight after: "Priya, Thursday pottery, flat hunting, from Leeds."

Then share a bit more of yourself than you did the first time. Friendships deepen when both people reveal a little, in turns. If they mention their new job is stressful, you might say what made your own last move hard. Keep it light, but real.

Step 3: Make the invite

This is the step most adults skip, then wonder why nothing grows. A good first invite has three features:

  1. It's specific: a day, a place, an activity. "We should hang out sometime" almost never happens.
  2. It's low-stakes: short, public, easy to say no to. Coffee after class beats a whole Saturday.
  3. It links to what you already share: a reason, not a date.

Example invites, adapted to where you met:

"A few of us are getting a drink after the game. Want to come?"

"I'm going to that board-game café on Friday. You said you liked Catan. Want to join?"

"I'm trying the new ramen place near the station on Sunday. Fancy it?"

"Want to grab a coffee after class next week instead of rushing off?"

If you find a direct invite hard, start with a group one. "A few of us" feels less like a request for friendship and more like an open door.

A no is rarely about you. Adults are busy, tired and already have routines. If someone turns down two specific invites without suggesting another time, let them come to you.

Step 5: Follow up so it becomes a habit

One coffee is not a friendship yet. What turns it into one is the second and third meeting.

  • Send a short message after: "Really enjoyed that. Let me know how the interview goes."
  • Suggest a repeat: "Same time next month?" Recurring plans take the effort out of it.
  • Share something small in between: a photo of the café you talked about, a link to that band. Keep it light; no long messages.

You will usually be the one who reaches out more at first. That's normal, not needy. Most people are glad someone else did the organising.

Practising the awkward part

The hardest moment is usually the switch from chatting to asking, because it feels like a bigger risk than it is. Questions people ask

Why is it so hard to make friends as an adult?

Mostly because the daily repetition of school or university is gone, and adults are busy. You need to choose places where you see the same people each week, and then make the invite yourself.

How many times should I meet someone before inviting them out?

There is no fixed number, but after two or three friendly conversations where they've shared something personal, a small invite rarely feels too soon.

Is it weird to ask someone to be friends?

You don't need to say "be friends". Invite them to a specific, small thing. That's how most adult friendships actually start.

What if they say yes but never follow through?

Suggest one concrete time. If that falls through too and they don't offer another, keep things friendly but stop pushing. Put your energy into people who respond.

Where is the best place to make friends as an adult?

Anywhere you'll see the same people regularly and do something together: a class, a club, a team, volunteering. Shared activity gives you easy topics and repeated contact.
