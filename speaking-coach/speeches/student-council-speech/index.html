How to Write a Student Council Speech That Wins Votes

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

A winning student council speech is short, usually 1 to 2 minutes: say who you are in a way classmates will remember, make two or three concrete promises you can actually deliver, show you've listened to what students want, and finish with a memorable line that asks for their vote. Specific, believable promises beat big ones every time.

What voters are actually deciding

Most students don't vote on policy. They vote on two questions: Do I like this person? and Would they actually do something? Your speech has about 90 seconds to answer both. That's why the best speeches are short, a bit funny, and concrete.

The structure

PartWhat to sayTime
HookA line that makes people look up5–10 seconds
Who you areName, grade, role, and one human detail15–20 seconds
What you've heardThe problems students actually complain about10–15 seconds
Two or three promisesSpecific changes, and how you'll do them30–45 seconds
Why youOne example that shows you follow through10–15 seconds
CloseA memorable line and a clear ask for the vote10 seconds

That adds up to 80 to 115 seconds. Check your school's time limit; many keep speeches to one or two minutes.

Promises that win votes (and ones that lose them)

Students have heard "no homework" and "longer lunch" before, and they know it won't happen. Unrealistic promises make you sound like you're not taking them seriously. Pick changes the student council can actually influence.

Sounds big but isn't believableSpecific and doable
"No more homework!""I'll ask teachers to put all homework deadlines on one shared calendar so tests don't pile up in the same week."
"Better food in the cafeteria.""I'll run a lunch survey in the first month and take the top three requests to the catering manager."
"More fun events.""A movie night every term in the gym, with tickets that pay for the next one."
"I'll make your voice heard.""A suggestion box outside the office, and I'll read out every answer at the monthly assembly."
"I'll change everything.""I'll fix the two things you complain about most: the broken lockers and the queue at the water fountain."

Before your speech, ask ten classmates what bugs them most. Using their exact complaints shows you listened, and gives you lines that land: "A lot of you told me the second-floor lockers have been broken since September."

Proving you'll follow through

One short example of something you've already done makes your promises believable.

"Last year I organised the bake sale that paid for the new netball bibs. I like getting things done, not just talking about them."

If you haven't held a role before, use anything that shows reliability: a part-time job, looking after siblings, running a club, finishing a project.

Example speeches

These are made-up examples. Use the shape and your own details.

Middle school (about 60 seconds)

"Hi, I'm Maya, and I'm in seventh grade. You might know me as the girl who fell over in the cross-country race and still finished. I don't give up easily.

I asked a lot of you what you'd change about our school. Two things came up again and again: lunch lines that take half of lunch, and not enough clubs after school.

So here's what I'll do. First, I'll ask the office to open a second lunch line on pizza day. Second, I'll start a club request form. If ten people sign up for something, I'll find a teacher to run it.

I can't promise no homework. I can promise I'll show up and keep you updated. Vote Maya for class rep. I'll finish what I start, even if I fall over."

High school class president (about 90 seconds)

"Good morning. I'm Jordan Lee, and I'm running for junior class president.

Three weeks ago I put a sheet on the cafeteria wall asking, 'What would you fix?' Eighty-two of you wrote on it. The top answers were: the parking lot gets locked too early, prom tickets are too expensive, and nobody knows what the student council actually does.

So here are my three promises. One: I'll take the parking lot issue to the principal in my first month, with your signatures. Two: we'll run two fundraisers in the fall so prom tickets can cost less in the spring. Three: after every council meeting, I'll post a one-paragraph update in the class group chat, so you always know what we're doing.

Last year I ran the robotics club's fundraiser and we raised enough to go to regionals. I know how to turn a plan into something real.

If you want a president who listens and then actually reports back, vote Jordan Lee. Let's make junior year easier to love."

A close they'll remember

Your last line is the one people carry into the voting booth. Options:

  • A callback to your hook: "I'll finish what I start, even if I fall over."
  • A rhyme or slogan, if it's clever and short: "Don't be late, vote for Kate."
  • A plain promise: "I'll report back every month. Vote Jordan."

Avoid ending with "So, yeah, vote for me I guess." Say your name and the role clearly as the final words.

Delivering it in front of the school

  1. Walk up slowly and wait two seconds before you speak. It looks confident and gets attention.
  2. Know the first and last lines by heart. Use a small card for the middle.
  3. Look up for every promise. Read it, look up, say it.
  4. Slow down in the gym or hall. Echo makes fast speech hard to follow.
  5. Smile at the funny line, and wait if people laugh.

Questions people ask

How long should a student council speech be?

Usually 1 to 2 minutes. Check your school's rules; going over time can look like you didn't prepare.

Should my student council speech be funny?

A little humour helps people remember you. One light line in the hook or the close is enough; the promises should be serious.

What should I promise in a student council speech?

Two or three specific changes the council can actually influence, based on what students told you. Avoid promises only teachers or the principal could make.

How do I start a student council speech?

With a short, surprising line or a quick story about yourself, then your name. See how to start a speech for more openings.

What if I'm running against a popular student?

Be the candidate with the clearest plan. Specific promises and proof that you listened can beat popularity.
