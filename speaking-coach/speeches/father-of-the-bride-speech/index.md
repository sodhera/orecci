# How to Write a Father of the Bride Speech (With Examples)

A father of the bride speech usually has five parts: welcome everyone as host, thank the people who made the day happen, share one memory of your daughter growing up, welcome her new spouse and their family, and propose a toast to the couple. Aim for 4 to 6 minutes, about 520 to 780 words, and keep the childhood section to one or two stories rather than a full timeline.

## Your job in this speech

In many weddings the father of the bride speaks first, and he speaks as a host. That shapes everything: you open the speeches, you set the tone, and you are the person who says "thank you for coming" on the family's behalf. Even if you didn't pay for the day, the role is still to welcome.

Your speech does three jobs the others don't: it welcomes the room, it speaks for the bride's family, and it formally welcomes the new spouse.

## The structure

| Part | What to say | Time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Welcome | Greet guests, mention those who travelled | 30–45 seconds |
| Thanks | The partner's parents, anyone who helped, the people who made the day | 30–60 seconds |
| Your daughter | One or two stories from growing up, and who she is now | 90–120 seconds |
| The new spouse | What you've seen in them, a welcome to the family | 45–60 seconds |
| Advice (optional) | One short line, not a lecture | 15–30 seconds |
| Toast | To the couple | 15 seconds |

## Part by part, with example lines

These are made-up example lines to show each part. Use your own names and memories.

### The welcome

> "Good afternoon, everyone. I'm David, Emma's dad. On behalf of Susan and me, welcome. Some of you have come from Toronto, from Cork and from just down the road, and we're grateful to every one of you for being here."

### The thanks

Keep it short and specific. Name the partner's parents first, then group everyone else.

> "Thank you to Richard and Anne, who've been wonderful from the first dinner, and who have raised a son we are very fond of. And thank you to everyone who baked, drove, set up chairs or calmed nerves this morning. You know who you are."

### Your daughter

This is the heart of the speech. Pick one memory that shows a quality she still has, then bring it to the present. Avoid the full timeline from birth to graduation; guests will drift.

> "When Emma was six, she decided our cat needed a passport. She made one from cardboard, with a photo, and insisted we stamp it every time the cat came back from the garden. That was the first sign that Emma would do things properly or not at all. She's brought the same energy to her nursing career, to her friendships and, I'm told, to every decision about today's seating plan."

### The new spouse

Speak to them directly, by name, and say something specific you have seen.

> "Tom, the first time you came for dinner you offered to wash up, and then you actually did it. Susan noticed. I noticed. But mostly I noticed how Emma laughs when you're around. Welcome to our family. You're stuck with us now."

### The toast

> "Will you please stand and raise your glasses. To Emma and Tom."

## Words and ideas to avoid

| Avoid | Why | Instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "I'm not losing a daughter, I'm gaining a son" | Heard at nearly every wedding | Say one specific thing you value about the new spouse |
| Jokes about the cost of the wedding | Can embarrass the couple or the other family | Leave money out entirely |
| Every milestone from birth to now | The room can't hold that many scenes | One or two stories that show who she is |
| Exes, old boyfriends, past heartbreaks | Hurtful, even as a joke | Nothing about past relationships |
| Long marriage advice | It's their day, not a seminar | One line, or none |

## Variations

**If you're a stepfather or the father figure.** Say how you came into her life in one sentence ("I met Emma when she was nine and I was dating her mum; she interviewed me"). Then speak like any father would.

**If the bride's parents are divorced.** Thank both sides warmly and include her mother by name. Today is not the place for anything else.

**If the bride's mother is also speaking.** Agree who covers what: perhaps she takes the childhood memories and you take the welcome and the thanks. See our [mother of the bride speech guide](/speaking-coach/speeches/mother-of-the-bride-speech/).

**If someone is missing.** If the bride's mother or a grandparent has died, a brief, warm line before the toast is usually right: "Emma's grandmother would have loved today. She'd have had opinions about the flowers." Check with your daughter first.

## Delivering it

Most fathers of the bride read from cards or a printed page, and that's fine. What helps the room is how you read: slow down, look up at the end of each sentence, and look at your daughter when you speak about her. Our guide on whether to [memorise your speech or use notes](/speaking-coach/guides/memorize-speech-or-use-notes/) explains a middle path: memorise the opening and the toast, and read the rest.

The part most fathers struggle with is the line about their daughter as a child. Read that paragraph aloud until your voice stops catching on it. If no one in the house can listen without hearing the surprise, you can rehearse it with Speaking Coach in the app and check your pace and long pauses afterwards.

## Questions people ask

### How long should a father of the bride speech be?

Four to six minutes, around 520 to 780 words at a steady pace. If there are many speeches, aim for four.

### Who does the father of the bride thank?

The partner's parents, people who helped organise or pay for the day, guests who travelled, and sometimes the venue team. Group people where you can, so it doesn't become a list.

### Should I include advice for the couple?

Only if you have one genuine, short piece. A sentence about what has worked in your own marriage is enough.

### Does the father of the bride still give the first speech?

Often, but not always. Many couples now change the order or have a parent speak together. Ask the couple what they've planned.

### Is it okay to cry?

Yes. Guests expect it and it's part of the moment. Pause, breathe, and carry on from the next sentence.
