What to Say in Your Own Retirement Speech (With Examples)

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

In your own retirement speech, thank the people who made the work good, tell one story from your career that shows what it meant to you, say briefly what's next, and close with a toast or a final thank-you. Keep it to 3 to 5 minutes and resist the urge to walk through every role you've held: people remember one good story, not a résumé.

What the room wants from you

At a retirement party, people have already heard someone else praise you. When you stand up, they want to hear you: a little gratitude, one memory they can share, and a glimpse of who you'll be next. They don't need your full career history. Most of them lived part of it with you.

A simple structure

PartWhat to coverTime
OpeningThank whoever introduced you; a light line20–30 seconds
ThanksGroups first, then two or three names60–90 seconds
One career storyThe moment that sums up the work for you60–90 seconds
What you learnedOne sentence that came out of the story20–30 seconds
What's nextPlans, briefly20–30 seconds
CloseA wish for the people staying, a toast15–30 seconds

That's about 3 to 5 minutes. At a relaxed pace of around 130 words per minute, that's roughly 400 to 650 words. You can convert any draft with the speech length calculator.

Thanking people without reading a list

The thank-you section is where retirement speeches drag. Thirty names in a row lose the room, and someone always feels left out. Try this instead:

  1. Thank groups with a detail. "To the night shift, who taught me that the best decisions are made at 3 a.m. with cold coffee."
  2. Name two or three people, with a reason. "Carol, who hired me in 1998 when I had no idea what I was doing, and never told anyone."
  3. Thank your family last if they're in the room. They gave up evenings and weekends too.

Picking the one career story

The best story is rarely your biggest achievement. It's the moment that shows why the job mattered to you. Some prompts:

  • The day you realised you were good at this.
  • A mistake that taught you something everyone in the room would recognise.
  • A person you helped, or who helped you, without knowing it.
  • Something that has changed completely since your first day.

Example story (made up) for a long-serving teacher:

"In my second year, a boy called Danny told me he hated reading. Every lesson he sat at the back with his hood up. I tried everything, and nothing worked, until one day I found him reading a car manual under the desk. So I stopped fighting it. For a term, Danny read manuals, magazines, anything with an engine in it. Twenty years later he came to parents' evening as a dad, and told me he runs his own garage. He still doesn't read novels. But he reads."

What's next, in one or two lines

Keep it light and brief. People like to picture you happy.

"As for what's next: Jean has given me a list. It's longer than any staff memo I've ever written. Mostly it involves the garden, our grandchildren, and finally learning how the dishwasher works."

Example retirement speeches

These are made-up examples with different tones. Swap in your own details.

Office worker, warm and short (about 2 minutes)

"Thank you, Priya, for that introduction. Most of it was even true.

When I started here, we had one computer for the whole floor and a fax machine that only worked if you hit it. I'm leaving a little more advanced, and that's down to you. To the finance team: thank you for answering every 'quick question' that was never quick. Mike, you've been my work partner for fifteen years, and you never once let me sign something I hadn't read.

What I'll miss most isn't the work. It's Tuesday cake and the way this team closes ranks when someone is having a bad week.

I'm off to walk more, worry less, and visit my sister in Portugal for longer than a week. Please keep in touch. Please also keep feeding the office plant; it's older than some of you. To all of you, and thank you."

Nurse, more reflective (about 90 seconds)

"Thirty-four years ago I walked onto Ward 9 terrified. Today I'm walking off it slightly less terrified, and much prouder.

I've been asked what I'll remember. It isn't the busy nights, though there were plenty. It's a patient in my first year who couldn't sleep, and asked me to sit with her for ten minutes. I had twelve other things to do. I sat down anyway. She told me about her husband, and fell asleep halfway through. I learned that night that ten minutes can be the most important care you give all shift.

To the ward sisters who trained me, to the porters who always knew where everything was, and to Amira, who covered for me more times than either of us will admit: thank you.

To everyone staying: look after each other the way you looked after me."

Mistakes to avoid

MistakeWhy it hurtsInstead
Walking through every job and promotionIt's a résumé, not a speechOne story that shows the work
Long lists of namesPeople tune out; someone is missedGroups with details, two or three names
Airing old grievancesThe last word people hear is the bitter oneKeep it generous, even if it wasn't always
Inside jokes from 1997Newer staff are lostOne short line, explained
Going past five minutesThe party wants to get back to talkingCut the second story

Handling emotion

Retirement speeches catch people off guard. The thanks to a long-time colleague or to family is usually the line that does it. Read that paragraph out loud several times beforehand until it stops surprising you. If you get emotional on the day, stop, smile, and take a breath. Everyone in the room understands.

Rehearse it at least three times out loud. You can also practise with Speaking Coach in the app; afterwards it shows your pace, filler words and long pauses, so you can see whether you're rushing the parts that matter.

Questions people ask

How long should a retirement speech be?

Three to five minutes for your own speech. If several people have already spoken, two or three minutes is plenty.

Should I mention people who have left or passed away?

A brief, warm line is fine if they mattered to your career and the room knew them. Keep it to a sentence.

Is humour okay?

Yes. Gentle humour about yourself, the old systems or office habits works well. Avoid jokes at anyone else's expense.

Do I need to say what I'll do next?

It's nice to include, but keep it to one or two lines. People enjoy hearing your plans, not a detailed itinerary.

What if I'm being given a retirement toast by someone else?

Thank them first in your opening, and don't repeat the stories they told. If you're the one giving it for a colleague, see our retirement toast guide.
