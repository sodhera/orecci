What to Say in a Rehearsal Dinner Speech or Toast (Examples)

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

A rehearsal dinner speech is smaller and more personal than a reception speech: thank the people who travelled, tell one story the wider wedding crowd wouldn't hear, and end with a toast to the couple. Hosts speak first and welcome everyone; guests keep it to two or three minutes.

How the rehearsal dinner is different from the reception

The rehearsal dinner is usually the night before the wedding, with immediate family, the wedding party and people who travelled far. The room is smaller, everyone knows the couple well, and nobody is waiting for a first dance. That changes what you say:

Rehearsal dinnerReception
Audience15 to 60 people, mostly close family and friendsThe full guest list, including colleagues and plus-ones
ToneRelaxed, personal, room for inside jokes everyone getsPolished, inclusive, jokes that work for strangers
SpeakersHosts, then often an open floorUsually a fixed list: parents, best man, maid of honour
Length2 to 4 minutes each3 to 5 minutes each
Best materialChildhood stories, family history, "the night I knew"One clear story and a toast that suits everyone

The simplest rule: if a story only lands with people who were there, tell it tonight. Save the version for everyone for tomorrow.

Who speaks, and in what order

  1. The host or hosts. Whoever is paying for or organising the dinner speaks first. In many families this is the groom's parents, but it can be either set of parents, the couple themselves, or a relative. The host welcomes people and thanks those who travelled.
  2. The other parents, if they want to, often with a shorter toast.
  3. Wedding party members or close friends who are not speaking at the reception. This is their chance.
  4. The couple, who may close the evening with a short thank-you.

If there's an open floor, the host should say so clearly ("If anyone would like to say a few words, now's the time") and keep an eye on the clock. Six speakers at three minutes each is already twenty minutes.

A structure for the host

  1. Welcome (20 seconds). Who you are and why you're glad everyone's here. Name the people who travelled furthest.
  2. The couple (60 to 90 seconds). One story about your child, then one about how their partner fits into the family.
  3. The other family (20 seconds). A warm word for the new in-laws, by name.
  4. Logistics, if any (10 seconds). Tomorrow's start time is fine to mention. Keep it to one line.
  5. The toast (10 seconds). Raise your glass and name them.

That lands at roughly two and a half minutes, or about 300 to 400 words at a relaxed pace. Check your draft with the speech length calculator.

Example lines

These are made-up examples to show the shape. Use your own names, places and stories.

Example: host parent

"Good evening, everyone. I'm Mark, Daniel's dad, and Linda and I are so happy you're here. Some of you flew in from Dublin this morning, and the Okafors drove nine hours from Atlanta. Thank you.

When Daniel was eight, he spent a whole summer building a treehouse that never got a roof. He said the roof was 'phase two'. So when he told us he'd met someone who finishes things, we paid attention. Priya, you finish things. You also laugh at his phase twos, which is love.

To Priya's parents, Raj and Meena: we've known you for two years and it already feels like longer. Tomorrow we become one family officially. Tonight we're just glad to share a table.

Please raise a glass: to Daniel and Priya."

Example: close friend at an open floor

"I'm Sam, I lived with Priya for three years at university, and I'm not speaking tomorrow, so you get the unedited version. In second year Priya made a spreadsheet to decide whether to go on a second date with Daniel. He scored a 6. She went anyway. I'm told the spreadsheet has been updated. To the 10 out of 10."

Example: the couple, closing the night

"We just want to say thank you. Everyone at these tables is here because they shaped one of us. Tomorrow is going to go fast, so tonight, while it's quiet: we love you, and we're so glad you came."

What to save for tomorrow, and what to skip entirely

  • Save for the reception: the big, polished story; anything that needs setup for people who don't know the couple.
  • Fine tonight: family nicknames, a childhood story with a few relatives as the punchline, a gentle roast the couple has already laughed at.
  • Skip entirely: exes, anything about wedding planning stress or money, stories that embarrass the partner's family, and anything you'd hesitate to say in front of a grandparent.

Mistakes to avoid

MistakeWhy it hurtsInstead
Giving your reception speech earlyPeople hear it twice, or the reception version falls flatDifferent story tonight, or speak at only one event
A long list of thank-yousGuests tune out by the fourth nameThank groups ("everyone who travelled"), then one or two names
Inside jokes with no setupHalf the room feels left outOne line of context before the punchline
Drinking before you speakSmall room, everyone noticesSpeak early in the evening, then relax
No clear endingSpeakers trail off and sit down awkwardlyAlways finish with the toast line

How to prepare

  1. Write the story first, then wrap the welcome and toast around it.
  2. Say it out loud three times with a timer. Writing it isn't the same as saying it.
  3. Cut anything over three minutes, starting with background, not the punchline.
  4. Put four keywords on a card: welcome, story, in-laws, toast.

Questions people ask

Who gives a speech at the rehearsal dinner?

The host speaks first, often a set of parents. After that it's flexible: the other parents, wedding party members, close friends and the couple themselves. Many dinners have an open floor once the hosts have spoken.

How long should a rehearsal dinner toast be?

Two to three minutes for guests, up to four for the host. With several speakers, shorter is kinder to the room.

Can I give a speech at both the rehearsal dinner and the wedding?

Yes, but make them different. Tell the personal, family-only story at the rehearsal dinner and keep the reception speech for the wider crowd.

Is the rehearsal dinner speech more casual?

Usually. You can be more personal and use stories that need a little family context, but it's still a speech: plan a clear start, one story and a toast.

What if I'm not close to the couple?

Keep it to a short toast. Thank the hosts, say one specific thing you admire about the couple, and raise your glass. Thirty seconds is enough.
