How to Give a Welcome Speech for an Event (With Examples)

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

A welcome speech greets the audience, says why everyone is here, covers the logistics people need, and hands over to the first speaker or activity. For most events it should take two to four minutes. Open with a greeting and the event's purpose, not with thanks to a long list of names.

What a welcome speech has to do

The first minutes of an event set its energy. People are still finding seats, checking phones and wondering whether this will be worth their day. Your welcome has four jobs: make people feel they're in the right place, tell them why it matters, tell them what they need to know, and get out of the way.

The five-part structure

PartWhat to sayTime
1. GreetingWho you are, a warm hello to the room15 seconds
2. PurposeWhy this event exists and why today30 to 45 seconds
3. The audienceWho's here, a nod to people who travelled or are new20 seconds
4. LogisticsWi-Fi, breaks, exits, schedule changes, how to ask questions30 seconds
5. HandoverIntroduce the first speaker or activity15 to 30 seconds

Total: about two to three minutes, or 250 to 400 words. For a short meeting, compress parts 2 to 4 into one sentence each and you'll be done in under a minute.

Example welcome speeches

These are made-up examples. Replace the names, numbers and details with your own.

Example: conference opening (about 2.5 minutes)

"Good morning, and welcome to Northfield Design Week. I'm Leah Grant, and I chair the organising committee.

Three years ago, this started as twenty people in a library meeting room, arguing about fonts. This morning there are four hundred of you, from fourteen countries. We still argue about fonts. But the question we keep coming back to is bigger: how do small studios do work that matters when the budgets keep shrinking? That's what the next two days are about.

If this is your first time here, raise your hand. Look around: about a third of the room. Please, everyone else, say hello to these people at lunch.

A few practical things. The Wi-Fi name and password are on the back of your badge. Coffee is in the atrium at 10:30. Session C has moved to Room 4 because of the projector. And questions for any speaker go through the app, so nobody has to fight for a microphone.

Our first speaker has run an independent studio for fifteen years and turned down more clients than most of us will ever have. She's going to tell us why. Please welcome Marta Lindqvist."

Example: graduation or award ceremony (about 1.5 minutes)

"Good evening, families, friends, staff and, most of all, graduates. I'm David Chen, head of the adult learning centre, and welcome to the class of 2026 ceremony.

Every person crossing this stage tonight did it while working, raising kids or both. Some of you started this course three times before you finished it. Tonight is about the time you finished.

The ceremony will take about an hour. Please hold your applause until each group of names has been read, then cheer as loudly as you like. Photos are welcome from your seats, and there's a photographer at the side of the stage.

To begin, I'd like to invite our student speaker, Rosa Martín."

Example: team meeting or offsite (about 40 seconds)

"Morning, everyone, and thanks for being here in person. Some of you haven't been in the same room since the spring. Today has one goal: leave with three priorities for next quarter that everyone in this room agrees on. We'll break at 11 and at 3, lunch is upstairs, and we'll finish by 5, promise. First, Sam's going to show us where we landed this year."

Adapting it to your event

  • Conference or summit: the purpose matters most. Name the question the event is trying to answer.
  • Ceremony (graduation, awards, memorial): slower and more formal. Mention the people being honoured early, and explain the running order so families can relax.
  • Community or charity event: thank volunteers as a group and say what the money or time goes toward, in one concrete sentence.
  • Internal meeting: skip the formality. One sentence of purpose, the agenda, the handover.
  • Virtual event: add the logistics people need online: how to unmute, where the chat is, whether it's recorded.

Delivery tips for the first minutes

  1. Wait for quiet. Stand at the front, smile, and pause until the room settles. Starting over noise teaches people they can keep talking.
  2. Know your first two sentences by heart. Nerves peak in the opening seconds; see how to start a speech.
  3. Speak to the back row. Large rooms swallow voices. Projecting your voice matters more than a clever line.
  4. Read logistics, don't memorise them. It's fine to glance at a card for room numbers and times.
  5. Slow down for the handover name. It's the line people need to hear clearly.

Mistakes to avoid

MistakeWhy it hurtsInstead
Opening with "Can everyone hear me?"Weak first line, and the answer is usually yesTest the mic beforehand, then open with your greeting
Thanking twelve sponsors by nameThe room's energy drops before anything startsThank sponsors as a group, or move thanks to the close
Repeating the printed agenda in fullPeople can readMention only changes and the one thing not to miss
A long story about how the event came togetherIt's about the organisers, not the audienceOne sentence of history at most
Running overEvery minute you take comes from the first speakerRehearse with a timer and cut to fit

Questions people ask

How long should a welcome speech be?

Two to four minutes for most events, under a minute for a meeting. If you have a lot of logistics, put them on a slide or handout and mention only the essentials.

How do I start a welcome speech?

Greet the room and say who you are in one sentence, then go straight to why everyone is here. Save thanks for later in the speech or for the close.

Who should I thank in a welcome speech?

The audience, briefly, and any group without whom the event couldn't happen, such as volunteers or hosts. Detailed thank-yous to sponsors and organisers fit better in the closing remarks.

Should I tell a joke at the start?

Only if it's about the event or the room and you're comfortable with it. A warm, specific line works just as well and has no risk of falling flat.

Can I read my welcome speech?

You can read logistics, but try to deliver the greeting and purpose while looking at the audience. Practise it out loud a few times so you only need key words on a card.
