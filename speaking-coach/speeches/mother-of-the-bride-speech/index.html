What to Say in a Mother of the Bride Speech (With Examples)

By the Speaking Coach team at Sodhera Intelligence · Updated October 22, 2026

A mother of the bride speech is usually 2 to 4 minutes: thank everyone for coming, share one or two warm memories of your daughter, welcome her partner and their family by name, and finish with a short toast. If the father of the bride is also speaking, agree beforehand who covers the welcome and thanks, so you can spend your time on your daughter and the new family.

Where the mother of the bride fits

Traditions vary, and more mothers of the bride speak now than in the past. You might:

  • Speak on your own, often early in the speeches, as one of the hosts.
  • Speak instead of the father of the bride, if he's absent, has died, or would rather not.
  • Give a joint speech with the bride's father or your partner, taking turns.
  • Give a short toast at the rehearsal dinner or engagement party instead of at the reception.

Ask the couple which they'd like. If the father is also speaking, decide together who does the welcome and the thanks. Repeating them back to back slows the evening. See the father of the bride speech guide for what he usually covers.

The structure

PartWhat to sayTime
WelcomeA brief greeting, especially if you're speaking first15–30 seconds
A memoryOne or two moments that show who your daughter is60–90 seconds
Who she is nowBring the memory to the present20–30 seconds
Welcome the partnerWhat you've seen in them, said directly30–45 seconds
Welcome their familyBy name, with warmth15–20 seconds
ToastTo the couple10–15 seconds

That's about three minutes, or 350 to 400 words at a gentle pace.

Finding the memory

Mothers often have too many memories, not too few. Choose the one that:

  • Shows a quality she still has. Kindness, determination, mischief, a gift for organising everyone.
  • Has a small, clear picture in it. A place, an object, something she said.
  • Won't embarrass her. Funny is lovely; humiliating isn't. No bath-time stories.
  • The partner would recognise. If they'd say "that's so her", it's the right one.

Example memories (made up), shown as the line you'd say:

"When Olivia was eight, she organised a funeral for a goldfish. She wrote an order of service, assigned readings to her brothers, and made everyone wear black socks. She has been organising all of us ever since, including, I suspect, most of today."

"Zara was the child who brought home every lost animal in the neighbourhood: a hedgehog, two cats that weren't lost, and once, briefly, a goat. She hasn't changed. She still collects people who need looking after, and she's still the first to show up."

Welcoming the partner and their family

This is the part only you can give. Speak to the partner by name, and say one thing you have actually noticed.

"Sam, the first time Olivia brought you home, you asked me for the recipe for my lasagne. Nobody had asked in thirty years. I knew then you were paying attention. Thank you for loving our daughter the way you do: patiently, and with snacks. Welcome to our family."

Then the other family:

"To Mark and Linda, and to all of Sam's family: we're so glad to be sharing this day, and these two, with you. We've gained more than a son-in-law today."

If it's a same-sex wedding, the structure is identical. Use the words the couple uses for each other.

The toast

Keep it short and give people a clear cue to stand.

"Please raise your glasses to Olivia and Sam: to a long, happy and well-organised life together."

Full example (about 2 and a half minutes)

A made-up example to show the whole shape.

"Good evening, everyone. I'm Helen, Olivia's mum. Thank you all for being here, especially those who came from Edinburgh and survived the motorway.

When Olivia was eight, she organised a funeral for a goldfish. She wrote an order of service, assigned readings to her brothers and insisted we all wear black socks. She has been organising all of us ever since, and when she started planning today, none of us were surprised by the spreadsheet.

But what I love most about Olivia isn't the planning. It's that she always makes room for the person who's left out. At every party since she was small, she's been the one sitting with the quiet guest.

Sam, the first time you came for dinner, you asked me for my lasagne recipe. Nobody had asked in thirty years. Thank you for noticing the small things, and for loving our daughter so well. Welcome to the family.

Mark and Linda, we're delighted to share these two with you.

Please raise your glasses. To Olivia and Sam."

Staying composed

The line about your daughter as a child, or the welcome to her partner, is often the one that brings tears. Read it out loud ten times in the days before, until it stops catching you. Print the speech in large type, mark the places to breathe, and keep water nearby. Our guide on stopping your voice shaking has a breathing exercise you can do in your seat. Questions people ask

Does the mother of the bride give a speech?

She can, and many do. It depends on the couple and family tradition; ask them what they'd like.

How long should a mother of the bride speech be?

Two to four minutes. If the father is also speaking, keep yours to the shorter end.

What if the bride's father has passed away?

You may give the main parent speech. A short, warm line about him before the toast is often right; check with your daughter first.

Can I give a joint speech with the father of the bride?

Yes. Split by section: one of you welcomes and thanks, the other shares the memory, and you give the toast together.

Should I give advice to the couple?

One line is enough, and only if it's your own. Skip generic marriage advice.
